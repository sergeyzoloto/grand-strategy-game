Central power generation distributed over a grid to homes, businesses, and
industry — converting energy access from something each user had to
generate locally (a private steam engine, gas lighting) into a purchased
utility service delivered on demand.

## Timing

Commercial central power generation begins with Edison's Pearl Street
Station (1882); alternating-current transmission technology, letting power
travel efficiently over much longer distances than direct current allows,
wins out through the 1890s "War of the Currents," with grid electrification
spreading across urban and eventually rural areas of major economies
through the 1930s.

## Mechanism

Centralized generation with AC long-distance transmission lets a single
power plant serve a wide area far more efficiently than every building
generating its own power locally, converting electricity from a capital
investment each user had to make individually into a metered utility
service — but building out grid infrastructure to reach a given area
remains a substantial fixed capital investment, meaning access rolls out
wherever population density and expected revenue justify that investment
first, leaving lower-density areas electrified last regardless of overall
national wealth.

## Social consequence

Grid electricity access becomes a durable marker of economic
inclusion versus exclusion at the household level — electrified homes and
businesses gain access to an entire subsequent cascade of electricity-
dependent technology (lighting, refrigeration, electric machinery) that
non-electrified ones simply cannot use regardless of ability to pay for the
devices themselves, making the grid's physical reach as consequential as
any individual technology it later enables.

## Regional specifics

Japan's rapid electrification, following American and European demonstration
by only a few years, is consistent with the broader wholesale-technology-
adoption pattern already well documented for Meiji Japan elsewhere in this
record. India's decades-long urban-rural electrification gap under colonial
and early postcolonial administration directly determines which regions
could subsequently adopt any electricity-dependent technology at all,
compounding rather than merely reflecting existing urban-rural economic
disparities.

## Contested

Whether AC's victory over DC in the "War of the Currents" reflects pure
engineering merit (superior long-distance transmission efficiency) or was
also substantially shaped by patent strategy and public-perception
campaigns independent of technical merit is debated. Model AC's technical
transmission advantage as real and decisive for the long-distance grid
outcome specifically, while treating the particular pace and manner of
DC's displacement as also shaped by commercial and reputational factors
specific to that historical rivalry.

## Hook

Grid electrification's physical reach — gated on population density
justifying the fixed infrastructure investment — determines access to an
entire subsequent cascade of electricity-dependent technology regardless of
individual ability to pay, making the grid's rollout geography as
consequential a divide as any single downstream technology it later
enables.
