Converting pulped plant fiber, suspended in water and dried into thin
sheets, into a cheap, foldable, mass-producible writing surface —
removing the cost ceiling parchment (an entire prepared animal hide for
a few pages) and papyrus (geographically restricted to reed-growing
river valleys) both impose on written material at any real scale.

## Timing

Archaeological paper finds in China predate the traditionally-credited
2nd-century-CE Cai Lun refinement by a couple of centuries; the technique
transmits westward across the following centuries, reaching Baghdad's
own paper mills by 793 and, later, Christian Europe through Islamic
Spain and Sicily by around 1200.

## Mechanism

Pulping plant fiber (originally rags, bark, and hemp waste; later
increasingly cheap agricultural fiber) into a water suspension, then
draining and pressing thin, even sheets from it, produces a writing
surface at a fraction of parchment's cost and labor per unit area —
parchment production consumes an entire animal hide per small number of
pages, while a single pulping operation produces many sheets at once
from cheap or waste raw material, converting the cost structure of
written material from labor-and-livestock-intensive to
labor-and-fiber-intensive instead.

## Social consequence

Cheap paper makes sustained record-keeping, correspondence, and
eventually mass copying economically viable for institutions and
individuals who could never have afforded parchment at comparable
volume, directly setting up the economics `ENG-006`'s later printing
press depends on — a press with nothing affordable to print onto
doesn't scale into mass production regardless of how good the
mechanical printing technique itself is.

## Regional specifics

Baghdad's paper mills convert papermaking from an imported Chinese
technique into a full Islamic-world manufacturing industry, refining
pulp preparation along the way rather than simply relaying an unchanged
process. Mesoamerican amatl bark-paper reaches comparable practical
function — a foldable, prepared surface for codices and ritual use —
through an entirely independent raw material and process, with zero
Old World contact. Sub-Saharan Africa's reliance on parchment
(Ethiopia) and imported paper (Timbuktu, via `COM-028`'s trans-Saharan
caravan trade) rather than local production shows the absence tracks
manufacturing technique specifically, not the presence of a serious
written scholarly culture to use the material.

## Contested

The popular "Battle of Talas" transmission story — captured Chinese
papermakers personally teaching Abbasid captors in 751 CE — is a
widely-repeated simplification; historians increasingly read the actual
transmission as more gradual and multi-channel, likely already
underway via Silk Road contact independent of that one battle. Model
the westward spread as a diffusion process with a genuinely uncertain
specific mechanism, rather than crediting one dramatic transfer event.

## Hook

Cheap paper removes the cost ceiling both parchment and papyrus impose
on written material — a gate `ENG-006`'s printing press depends on to
scale into genuine mass production, since a press mechanism alone has
nothing affordable to print onto without it.
