This entry owns the Bessemer process as engineering technique and its
direct commercial consequence — mechanizing the labor-intensive
decarburization step that had kept steel a scarce, expensive specialty
good throughout COM-020's much longer craft-commodity history, and
converting it into cheap bulk infrastructure material within a single
generation.

## Timing

Henry Bessemer patents and demonstrates the process in 1856; industrial-
scale adoption across British and then American steelmaking proceeds
rapidly through the 1860s-70s, with bulk steel's price relative to
wrought iron collapsing by an order of magnitude within roughly two
decades of the process's introduction.

## Mechanism

Converting molten pig iron into steel requires removing excess carbon
(decarburization), a process earlier steelmaking methods performed
slowly and at small batch scale through skilled, labor-intensive
technique — precisely the craft-commodity model COM-020 describes.
Bessemer's innovation blows air directly through the molten iron,
letting oxidation remove the excess carbon in minutes rather than the
hours or days earlier methods required, at a batch scale an order of
magnitude larger — a single process change that removes both the skill
bottleneck and the batch-size ceiling simultaneously, which is why the
price collapse follows so much faster than ordinary incremental
process improvement would produce.

## Social consequence

Cheap bulk steel becomes available as a structural and infrastructure
material for the first time, directly enabling the rail-network buildout
(COM-200) and structural-engineering projects that follow — converting
what had been, for millennia under COM-020's craft-commodity model, an
expensive specialty good reserved for weapons and tools into an ordinary
bulk input any sufficiently capitalized project could now specify.

## Regional specifics

American adoption under figures like Carnegie outpaces British
exploitation of Britain's own invention within a generation, a case of
invention and dominant commercial scaling separating across two
different economies. South Asia's much older wootz steel tradition — a
real, sophisticated, exceptional-quality craft achievement documented in
COM-020 — provides no head start on Bessemer-scale bulk production, since
the two are different problems (small-batch excellence versus large-batch
speed and cost) requiring different technical solutions.

## Hook

A single process innovation can collapse a specific commodity's price by
an order of magnitude within a generation by removing both a skill
bottleneck and a batch-size ceiling at once — converting steel from
COM-020's ancient, expensive, craft-scale specialty good into cheap bulk
infrastructure material, and directly unlocking the rail-network buildout
(COM-200) that follows.
