Glass as a structural and architectural material, not merely a decorative
or vessel one — the material that eventually lets a building admit light
through a wall that still keeps weather out, at a scale early glassmaking
never approached.

## Timing

Glassmaking is attested from around -3500 in Mesopotamia and Egypt; Roman
glassblowing technique (from the 1st century) sharply lowers production
cost and enables early window glass; industrial plate-glass manufacturing
matures with structures like the Crystal Palace (1851), whose iron-and-
glass construction anticipates the curtain-wall skyscraper architecture of
the following century.

## Mechanism

Early cast and core-formed glass production is slow and produces small,
often irregular pieces suited to vessels and decorative inlay but not to
covering a large architectural opening; glassblowing collapses production
time and cost per unit dramatically, and later industrial rolling and
casting processes push flat glass production to large-panel, structural-
grade scale and consistency — each production-technique advance directly
expands the maximum practical size and application of glass as a building
material rather than merely a luxury one.

## Social consequence

Cheap, large-format glass changes the relationship between a building's
interior and exterior — natural light and visual connection to outside
become available at a scale previously requiring open, unglazed apertures
that sacrificed weather protection entirely, reshaping domestic and civic
architecture's relationship to daylight, privacy, and display.

## Regional specifics

Roman glassblowing's origin in Syrian technique and subsequent
Mediterranean-wide scaling makes the Middle East the technical origin point
for the innovation that eventually enables architectural glass at scale,
not merely Rome's own achievement. Chinese architectural tradition's
continued preference for wood-and-masonry construction with screens and
paper for light control, rather than structural glazing, is consistent
with the broader alternative structural philosophy already noted in
ENG-004 — glassmaking skill existed without architectural glass application
because the surrounding building tradition solved the light-and-privacy
problem differently.

## Hook

Each advance in glass production technique — blowing, then industrial
rolling and casting — directly expands the maximum practical size and
structural application of glass as a building material, converting it from
a luxury decorative good into a genuine architectural surface only once
production cost and panel size cross a threshold late enough that most
building traditions had already settled on other solutions to the same
light-and-weather problem.
