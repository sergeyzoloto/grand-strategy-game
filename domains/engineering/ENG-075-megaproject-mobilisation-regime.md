A megaproject needs more labour, food, material and coordination than any ordinary
administration handles. The state that attempts one must build an apparatus to
assemble, feed, rotate, record and direct tens of thousands of workers. The works are
one output; the apparatus is the other, and it remains after the works are done,
available for war, tax, famine relief or the next project.

## Timing

From the Giza pyramids around 2560 BCE, through the Qin walls and roads after 221 BCE,
the Roman aqueducts, the Sui Grand Canal of 605-610, the Inca road and terrace system
of the fifteenth century, and the Ottoman and Mughal endowed complexes of the sixteenth
and seventeenth centuries, to the Egyptian corvee canals of the nineteenth century, the
Soviet penal canals and the TVA of the 1930s, the Khmer Rouge works of 1975-79, the
Aswan and Bhakra dams, and the Three Gorges Dam completed in 2012.

## Mechanism

**The boundary with ENG-020.** ENG-020 owns hydraulic technique: how a dam holds water,
how a canal keeps its gradient, how a Nile basin differs from a monsoon tank. This
entry owns something that is not technique. It is the property of a regime that can
mobilise labour at scale, and that property appears in pyramids, walls, roads, tombs
and mosques as readily as in dams. A pyramid and a canal share almost no engineering,
and their builders shared almost everything administrative. The split lets the game
track two different things: whether a polity knows how to build a structure, and
whether it can make a population build it.

**The apparatus is the durable output.** To raise a pyramid or dig a canal a state
must know how many people it has and where they are (FIN-020), feed them away from
their fields, rotate them so agriculture continues, move materials over long distances,
and keep records of all of it. Merer's logbook at Wadi al-Jarf records the trips of a
single crew of forty; the Inca kept equivalent accounts on khipu. Each project enlarges
the register, trains supervisors and scribes, builds storehouses and supply lines, and
habituates the population to turning out. The result is `mobilisation capacity`: a
retained administrative ability to assemble and sustain large labour forces, which does
not disappear when the project ends. It is the same machinery a state uses to raise an
army, collect tax in kind or distribute grain in famine. The roads of the Qin and the Inca were
themselves such an apparatus, carrying the garrisons and couriers that held a
conquered territory together (DIP-050). That is why regimes that built
megaprojects often became better at everything else, and why a successor that inherits
the apparatus can use it without having paid to build it.

**The legitimacy return.** A megaproject is a visible statement that the regime can
command nature and people. That is worth most when legitimacy is least secure, so
megaprojects cluster at the starts of dynasties and after revolutions:

- **The Qin and the Sui** each unified China after centuries of division and each built
  on a scale no predecessor had.
- **Soviet canals** were built in the first Five-Year Plans.
- **The TVA** was the New Deal's answer to the Depression.
- **Aswan** was Nasser's after the 1952 revolution.
- **Bhakra Nangal** was built by Nehru's new republic.

ENG-065 explains why the incentive is to build rather than maintain: building carries
the ruler's name. A megaproject is that incentive at its largest.

**Labour regimes differ, and the difference decides the cost.** Megaprojects drew on
four sources of labour:

- **Paid and provisioned rotation.** Giza's crews, Roman contractors, the Taj Mahal's
  craftsmen, the TVA.
- **Corvee owed by communities.** Inca mit'a, Chinese labour levies, Egyptian corvee
  from the pharaohs to Muhammad Ali.
- **Enslaved labour.** WAR-035 carries the economics, including why supervision is cheap
  on a work site.
- **Penal labour.** The Gulag canals.

The source is set by the regime's resources and by whether its labour is free to leave.
Rotation and provisioning cost more but preserve the population and the agriculture it
works. Corvee without limits and penal labour cost less in money and far more in lives,
and they spend the `mobilisable manpower` the regime depends on.

**The failure mode: the project destroys the regime.** A megaproject taken beyond what
the population will bear consumes the legitimacy it was meant to create. Qin and Sui are
the paired cases. Each unified China, each built walls, roads and canals with conscript
labour on top of foreign war, and each fell within a generation to revolts begun by
conscripts. In each case the successor dynasty — Han, Tang — inherited the works and
the administrative apparatus and ruled for centuries. COM-070 carries the Grand Canal's
later life as a route that had to be maintained by whoever held it. The Great Leap's
water campaign and the Khmer Rouge irrigation works show the modern form: mobilisation
without technical competence, producing works that did not function and deaths on the
scale of famine.

**Megaprojects create maintenance debt.** A canal or aqueduct that took a generation to
build takes permanent upkeep to keep, and the regime that builds it rarely provides for
that. The Ottoman waqf and the Mughal endowment of the Taj Mahal are the exceptions:
the building and a revenue stream to maintain it were founded together. Rome's
aqueduct gangs are another. Without such provision, every megaproject adds to the
`maintenance debt` its successors inherit (ENG-065).

**Captured apparatus.** A mobilisation regime can be taken over. Toledo's viceroyalty
redirected the Inca mit'a to the silver mines of Potosi, keeping the rotation, the
community quotas and the records and discarding the reciprocity that had made it
bearable. The capability outlived its builders and served a regime they would not have
recognised.

## Social consequence

At human scale the megaproject is a season's absence from home:

- **An Egyptian farmer.** They serve a rotation at Giza during the flood, fed on bread,
  beer and beef, in a named gang.
- **A Sui conscript.** They are marched to dig a canal hundreds of kilometres from home,
  with women from the village following when the men are gone.
- **An Andean household.** It sends members for a mit'a turn and receives maize beer and
  food in return, until the Spanish turn the same obligation into a year in the mines.
- **A Nubian family in 1964.** It is moved out of a valley that the Aswan reservoir will
  cover.
- **A Gulag prisoner in 1932.** They dig the White Sea canal with a wheelbarrow in the
  Karelian winter.

The regime's return — the apparatus, the legitimacy — accrues to the state. The cost
falls on the bodies of the people it moves. Displacement is the modern megaproject's
largest human cost, and MIG-250 carries state-driven population movement in general;
the Three Gorges resettled more than a million people.

## Regional specifics

The strongest cases are outside Europe, and the entry leads with them:

- **Egypt.** It supplies the earliest regime of paid, provisioned rotation, and in the
  nineteenth and twentieth centuries the corvee canals, Suez (MAR-285) and Aswan.
- **China.** It supplies the paired failures of Qin and Sui, the Great Leap's mass
  water campaign, and the Three Gorges.
- **The Andes.** It supplies mit'a as reciprocity-framed rotation and its capture by
  Spain.
- **The Ottoman and Mughal empires.** They supply the endowment that built maintenance
  into the foundation.
- **South Asia after independence.** Bhakra Nangal was the republic's temple.
- **Rome.** It supplies emperor-named aqueducts with a standing maintenance workforce.
- **The Soviet Union.** It supplies penal-labour megaprojects that were built fast,
  killed thousands and saw little use.
- **The United States.** The TVA is the democratic paid-labour case exported as a
  development model.
- **Cambodia.** It supplies the limiting case of mobilisation without competence.

The absences are monumental building without a mobilisation regime. Gobekli Tepe and
the moai of Rapa Nui were built by feasting work parties and competing lineages, and
left monuments without leaving an administration behind.

## Contested

Two debates bear on this entry:

- **Capacity first or project first.** Wittfogel's hydraulic thesis (carried in
  AGR-100) held that large works produced centralised states; archaeology found the
  states often came first. This entry reads the relationship as reciprocal.
- **Whether megaprojects repay their cost.** Flyvbjerg's studies found most run over
  budget and deliver less than forecast. Critics of large dams found displacement and
  ecological costs understated.

Model a megaproject's return as three outputs — the works, the apparatus, and
legitimacy — that can succeed or fail independently.

## Hook

**Three outputs.** A megaproject produces the works themselves, `mobilisation
capacity`, and legitimacy.

- **Mobilisation capacity.** The retained apparatus for registering, feeding, rotating
  and directing labour persists after completion and converts to war, tax and famine
  relief. A successor that inherits it gains it free.
- **Legitimacy.** The return is highest when legitimacy is weakest, so new dynasties
  and post-revolutionary regimes should be drawn to megaprojects.

**Labour source.** Paid rotation preserves `mobilisable manpower`; unlimited corvee and
penal labour spend it. Enslaved labour draws on the `coerced labour stock`.

**Failure.** Demand beyond what the population bears converts the legitimacy return
into revolt, and the regime can fall while its successor keeps the works and the
apparatus.

**Maintenance.** Every megaproject adds `maintenance debt` unless its founders endowed
its upkeep.

**Capture.** A conqueror can take over a mobilisation apparatus and redirect it.
