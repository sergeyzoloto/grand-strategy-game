The Haber-Bosch process synthesizes ammonia directly from atmospheric
nitrogen and hydrogen, breaking agriculture's ancient dependence on
naturally-occurring nitrogen sources (manure, crop rotation, guano deposits)
for the first time in history.

## Timing

Fritz Haber demonstrates the ammonia-synthesis reaction in 1909; Carl
Bosch's industrial-scale engineering (the process's namesake pairing) makes
mass production viable by the early 1910s, with synthetic fertilizer
becoming a standard agricultural input across major grain-producing regions
by mid-century and a central Green Revolution component from the 1960s.

## Mechanism

Nitrogen is the single most yield-limiting nutrient for most staple crops,
and before synthetic fixation, the available supply was capped by whatever
manure, crop rotation with nitrogen-fixing legumes, or imported guano and
nitrate deposits a given region could access — the Haber-Bosch process
removes that cap entirely by manufacturing usable nitrogen compounds
directly from air, converting a naturally-bounded input into an
industrially-manufacturable one limited only by energy and production
capacity rather than by biological or geological nitrogen availability.

## Social consequence

Removing the natural nitrogen ceiling lets a given area of land sustain
far higher crop yields and, by extension, far higher population density
than premodern agriculture ever could — a capacity increase widely credited
with helping avert famines that earlier population growth trajectories
would otherwise have made far more severe, while also making farmers newly
dependent on purchased industrial inputs rather than nutrient cycles they
could manage entirely with land and labor they already controlled.

## Regional specifics

East and South Asian Green Revolution adoption from the 1960s, combining
synthetic fertilizer with high-yield seed varieties, converts several
previously import-dependent countries into food self-sufficiency within a
couple of decades — one of the most consequential agricultural
transformations in this record's timeframe. Sub-Saharan Africa's markedly
slower and more uneven adoption, driven by distribution infrastructure
gaps, cost relative to farmer income, and input packages not well matched
to local soil and climate conditions, shows technological availability
alone doesn't guarantee comparable uptake or benefit across regions.

## Contested

Whether the Green Revolution package's yield gains net out positive against
its costs (farmer dependency on purchased inputs, reduced crop diversity,
water and soil nutrient pollution) is disputed, with the yield and famine-
prevention benefits and the dependency and environmental costs both well
documented but weighted very differently across different assessments.
Model the yield effect as large and real while tracking input-dependency
and environmental costs as separate downstream effects, rather than
collapsing everything into one net verdict.

## Hook

Synthetic nitrogen fixation removes agriculture's ancient natural-nitrogen
ceiling entirely, converting land's carrying capacity from a biologically-
bounded limit into an industrially-manufacturable one — a massive yield
unlock that simultaneously creates new farmer input-dependency and a
measurable environmental `toxic burden`, with adoption itself gated on
distribution infrastructure and input-package fit, not just availability.
