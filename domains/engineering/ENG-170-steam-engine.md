Converting heat into continuous, controllable mechanical motion for the
first time — the specific technology that lets a factory (COM-170) locate
anywhere rather than only beside a fast-flowing river, and eventually
powers the railway (COM-200) and steamship (MAR-260) as well.

## Timing

Newcomen's atmospheric engine (1712), initially used for pumping water out
of mines, is the first practical steam engine; James Watt's substantially
more fuel-efficient separate-condenser design (from the 1770s) makes steam
power economically viable for general industrial use, with widespread
factory and eventually transport application through the 1820s-1830s.

## Mechanism

A steam engine converts the chemical energy in fuel — coal, and the
relationship is not incidental but the reason the machine exists
(ENG-165) — into
mechanical motion via heat and pressure, providing continuous rotational or
reciprocating power that doesn't depend on a specific geographic feature
(a river's flow) the way a water wheel does — this location-independence is
what lets industrial production concentrate wherever labor, raw material,
and market access are best, rather than being tied to wherever a suitable
watercourse happens to be.

## Social consequence

Freeing factories from riverside locations accelerates urban industrial
concentration, since factories can now cluster near labor supply and each
other rather than being scattered along whatever waterways provide
sufficient flow — directly enabling the industrial internal migration
pattern and urban labor concentration already covered in this record's
migration and health-adjacent entries.

## Regional specifics

This is a genuinely Europe-originated technology in this window, with no
independently-developed counterpart elsewhere to compare against — East
Asian mechanical and metallurgical sophistication in other domains doesn't
extend to an independent steam-power tradition, and the technology arrives
in East Asia only via direct transfer during later industrial modernization
efforts, consistent with the broader technology-adoption pattern already
documented for other Western industrial technologies in this record.

## Hook

The steam engine converts location-dependent water-wheel power into
location-independent mechanical power, directly enabling factories
(COM-170) to concentrate near labor and markets rather than rivers — the
same location-independence later extends to rail (COM-200) and steamship
(MAR-260) transport once the technology matures further.
