The infrastructure states built to beat the transport bound before
electricity existed — and the entry that defines `information speed` as a
purchasable quantity rather than a fixed property of geography. The road
itself is ENG-090's; what this entry owns is the chain of staffed stations
laid along it and the standing expenditure that keeps the chain alive.

## Timing

From the Achaemenid angarium (roughly -550) through the Roman cursus
publicus, the Chinese post-station system, the Mongol yam, the Mughal dak
and the Inca chasqui, to Chappe's optical telegraph of 1794 — the last and
fastest pre-electric form, superseded within two generations by ENG-230.

## Mechanism

A courier's speed is bounded by how far one animal or one runner can go
before exhaustion. A relay defeats that bound by never letting the carrier
be the constraint — the message changes mounts and changes riders at fixed
intervals, so the system's speed is set by station spacing and by how
reliably each station is stocked, not by any individual's endurance. The
consequence is that **information speed stops being a property of
geography and becomes a line item**. A state can buy a faster empire.

What it buys is unusual in this record because it is bought entirely in
standing cost and holds no residual value. Stations must be sited a
day's stage apart, staffed continuously, and supplied with remounts and
fodder whether or not any message is passing — the capability is the
readiness, and an unmanned station is not a slow station but no station.
This makes relay messaging a clean instance of design principle 4 with the
depletion made unusually fast. A road neglected for thirty years is still a
somewhat usable road; a relay network neglected for one season is gone, and
the whole chain fails at its weakest post rather than degrading in
proportion to the neglect.

Two consequences follow that are worth modelling separately. First, courier
speed becomes a direct and observable index of state capacity — it is one
of the few administrative variables an outside observer can measure without
access to the archives, which is why travellers' reports of transit times
are such a reliable proxy for how well a given polity was actually running.
Second, and for the same reason, the relay network is a standing casualty of
fiscal crisis. It is cheap to cut and the saving is immediate, which makes
it an early target; and the cut compounds, because a state that has slowed
its own information learns about the next crisis later than it would have.
A polity can enter a downward spiral in which fiscal stress degrades the
speed at which fiscal stress is detected.

## Social consequence

The cost falls on the people living along the route, and it falls as an
obligation rather than a price. The Roman system is financed by requisition
from the communities it passes through; the Persian term for the
requisition right passes into Greek as *angareia* and from there into
common usage meaning forced labour of the most arbitrary kind — a word for
being compelled to carry someone else's burden a stated distance. That
semantic drift is the social consequence in miniature. To the centre the
network is an administrative marvel that costs almost nothing; to a village
at a station it is an open-ended liability to feed, house, and horse
whoever arrives holding the right token, at whatever hour, with no
recourse.

Authorisation is therefore the real scarce good, and it is rationed
politically. The Mongol paiza, the Roman *diploma*, and their equivalents
elsewhere decide who may move at the state's speed, which makes access to
the network a form of rank — and makes forged or over-issued authorisations
a recurring administrative crisis in their own right, since every extra
token draws real fodder from real villages. The network also runs
asymmetrically. It is built to carry the centre's orders outward and the
periphery's reports inward along the same spine, but only the centre
controls who may use it, so the practical effect is that a capital hears
what it has arranged to hear, quickly, and hears everything else at walking
pace.

## Regional specifics

The six focus regions run the same mechanism on different substrates, and
the Andean case is the one that disciplines the model. The Inca chasqui
achieved rates comparable to the best mounted networks using paired runners
on foot, without horses, wheels, or draft animals of any kind — so the
mechanism cannot be gated on animal traction. What it is gated on is the
ability to keep people permanently posted at short intervals along a
maintained route, which the Inca state could do and which is exactly what
the African case above lacked. The Mongol yam makes the complementary
point from the other direction, planting a network across terrain with no
prior roads at all and demonstrating that the station chain, not the
surface under it, is the load-bearing investment.

The European branch splits in a way the others do not, and the split
matters for what follows. The commercial newsletter networks make speed
purchasable by someone other than a ruler for the first time, creating the
merchant information premium that ENG-230 will later destroy; Chappe's
optical telegraph, by contrast, is a pure state instrument, faster than any
horse relay and hostage to fog.

## Hook

`information speed` is defined here as a purchasable state capability rather
than a constant of geography — bought at a fixed standing cost in stations,
staff, and remounts spaced by one stage's travel, yielding nothing when idle
and collapsing within a season if the spend stops, since the chain fails at
its weakest post rather than degrading smoothly. It should be cheap to cut
and expensive to rebuild, it should read out as a visible index of state
capacity that neighbours and travellers can estimate without archives, and
cutting it should carry a compounding penalty — a state that has slowed its
own couriers detects its next crisis later. The requisition burden falls on
the settlements along the route rather than on the treasury, so the centre
sees a nearly free network and the periphery pays for it, and authorisation
to use it should be a rationed political good in its own right.
