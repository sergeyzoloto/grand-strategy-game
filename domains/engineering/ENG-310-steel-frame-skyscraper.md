An internal steel skeleton carries a building's entire structural load,
letting exterior walls become thin, non-load-bearing "curtain walls"
regardless of height — replacing a masonry problem (wall thickness must
scale with height, imposing a hard practical ceiling) with a steel-
fabrication and fireproofing one instead, the load-bearing precondition
`ENG-250`'s safety elevator alone cannot supply.

## Timing

Chicago's Home Insurance Building (1885) is the paradigm early case;
steel-frame technique matures and scales rapidly across Chicago and New
York commercial construction through the 1890s, converging with cheap
Bessemer steel's availability (`ENG-260`) and safety elevators
(`ENG-250`) to produce the fully-realized skyscraper by 1900.

## Mechanism

A load-bearing masonry wall must grow thicker at its base as a building
grows taller, since the wall itself must support the accumulating
weight of every floor above it — Chicago's Monadnock Building (1891),
among the tallest load-bearing masonry structures ever built, needed
walls several feet thick at ground level and sits close to the
practical ceiling that approach can reach at all. A steel skeleton
instead carries the entire structural load through a frame of columns
and beams, letting exterior walls shrink to whatever thin, light
material (brick veneer, and later `ENG-050`'s glass) suffices for
weather and appearance alone — converting maximum building height from
a wall-thickness problem into a steel-fabrication, wind-bracing, and
fire-protection problem instead.

## Social consequence

Removing masonry's height ceiling converts a city's usable floor area
on a given footprint of expensive urban land from a roughly fixed
quantity into one bounded mainly by construction cost and, jointly,
`ENG-250`'s safe vertical transport — reshaping which cities could
support the densest, most valuable commercial districts, and directly
rewarding urban land markets (Chicago's rebuilding boom after its 1871
fire prominent among them) already under pressure to extract more value
per unit of scarce central land.

## Regional specifics

East Asian building traditions continue wood, masonry, and mid-rise
construction through this era without independently adopting steel-
frame technique, arriving only through later direct technology
transfer, consistent with the broader Western-origin pattern already
documented for steam power and machine tools specifically.

## Contested

Whether the Home Insurance Building genuinely holds priority as the
first fully load-bearing steel-frame building, versus earlier partial
iron-frame precedents in Britain where iron columns and beams
reinforced construction internally while exterior walls still carried
real structural load, is a real, still-debated attribution question
turning partly on how strictly "fully load-bearing frame" gets defined.
Model the transition as a spectrum crossed gradually by several
buildings across the 1880s-90s, rather than crediting one building as a
clean, uncontested first.

## Hook

Steel-frame construction is the load-bearing precondition the
skyscraper actually needs alongside `ENG-250`'s safety elevator and
`ENG-260`'s cheap steel — a joint-necessity gate mirroring `ENG-290`'s
established "neither alone sufficient" pattern, since a load-bearing
masonry building physically cannot cross this domain's practical height
ceiling regardless of how good its elevators are.
