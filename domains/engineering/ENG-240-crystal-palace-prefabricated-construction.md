Standardized cast-iron and glass components, manufactured off-site to
uniform dimensions and assembled on location, let Joseph Paxton's
Crystal Palace go from design to completed structure in under six
months for the 1851 Great Exhibition — a construction-speed mechanic
distinct from `ENG-050`'s glass-panel-scale framing, though built
directly on top of it.

## Timing

Designed and built for London's 1851 Great Exhibition, the Crystal
Palace is conceived, fabricated, and erected in under six months, later
relocated and expanded before its eventual 1936 destruction by fire.

## Mechanism

Manufacturing standardized structural and glazing components off-site
in advance, rather than fabricating custom pieces on the construction
site as needed, converts building assembly from a sequential, weather-
dependent craft process into something closer to large-scale
component-kit assembly — trading design flexibility for dramatically
compressed construction time, since every component's dimensions are
fixed and mass-produced well before ground is broken.

## Social consequence

The building's speed and scale become a public demonstration of
industrial manufacturing capability in their own right, independent of
its function as an exhibition venue — a structure whose construction
method is as much the point being made to visitors as anything actually
displayed inside it.

## Regional specifics

The 1853 New York Crystal Palace, a direct and smaller-scale imitation
of the London original, shows the innovation diffusing outward from one
origin point rather than developing independently elsewhere.

## Hook

Off-site standardized component manufacturing trades design flexibility
for dramatically compressed on-site construction time — a narrow,
single-building-scale demonstration of prefabrication's potential
rather than a mechanism this domain's other entries build further on.
