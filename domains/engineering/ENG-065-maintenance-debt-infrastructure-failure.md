Infrastructure is built once and must be maintained continuously, and the two have
opposite political economies. Building is visible, attributable and creates
legitimacy. Maintenance is invisible, produces nothing new, and its success looks like
nothing happening. So maintenance is systematically underfunded, deferred debt
accumulates in the physical stock, and failure arrives suddenly after a long period in
which nothing appeared wrong.

## Timing

From the silting canals of Mesopotamia in the third millennium BCE and the Nile works
of pharaonic and Roman Egypt, through the Yellow River's dike system and its breaches
of 1128 and 1855, the Sri Lankan tank cascades and Angkor's hydraulic network, the
Roman aqueducts and the Dutch water boards from 1255, to the cascading grid failures
of 1965, 2003 and 2012. The mechanism runs through the present; the date end marks the
last case carried.

## Mechanism

**The boundary with COM-070.** COM-070 carries a trade route's carrying capacity as a
depleting pool: silt accumulates and roads weather whether or not anyone uses them.
This entry carries the reason that pool is so reliably allowed to deplete — the
political economy that makes maintenance lose to construction — and generalises it
from trade routes to every engineered system a society depends on. It also covers
what happens when maintenance fails in systems that are coupled together.

**The fiscal asymmetry.** A ruler gains from building. A new canal, dam, road or
palace is visible, carries the ruler's name, and demonstrates capacity. A ruler loses
nothing visible from deferring repair, because a dike that is not repaired this year
still holds this year. The cost of neglect accumulates as `maintenance debt`: the gap
between the upkeep a system needs and the upkeep it receives, stored silently in
thinning embankments, silting channels and fatigued structures. The system's nominal
capacity looks unchanged while the debt grows. Failure then arrives suddenly and
typically under a successor, who pays for choices made before their time. This is a
time-horizon mechanism with the same shape as WAR-020's stationary bandit. A ruler
who expects to hold power for a long time has a reason to maintain what will produce
revenue later, and a ruler with a short horizon, or a regime in fiscal crisis, has
every reason to defer. The Qing let the Yellow River dikes go while fighting the
Taipings, and the river moved.

**Hydrological systems are the premodern case, and they are not natural disasters.**
The dams, dikes and canals of ENG-020 are the oldest engineered systems whose
upkeep never ends. A river whose bed rises above the plain because of silt it deposits behind dikes will
eventually break out unless the dikes are raised and repaired indefinitely. A canal
network dredged every season silts shut in a few years without the dredging. A tank
cascade whose upper tanks breach sends water and silt down onto the tanks below. When
these fail, the flood or the famine looks like nature, but the failure is in an
engineered system whose upkeep lapsed:

- **The Yellow River.** It left its bed when the dynasty that held it could not pay.
- **The Nahrawan canals.** They stopped watering the Diyala plains when the fiscal
  order behind their maintenance broke down.
- **Egypt.** Augustus cleared Nile canals the Ptolemies had let silt.
- **Mesopotamia.** Salinisation (AGR-150) followed wherever drainage lapsed.

The distinction matters because it moves responsibility from the weather to the
institution. The Harappan decline is the limit case. The shift of the Ghaggar-Hakra
rivers is now widely read as a weakening of monsoon-fed flow beyond any maintenance a
society could have performed, which marks where the mechanism stops.

**Capability loss is a second route to failure.** Maintenance needs knowledge, and
knowledge that is not used decays. A system can fail because nobody paid, or because
nobody remembers how to repair it. ENG-130 carries Roman concrete, whose recipe and
supply chain were lost with the institutions that sustained them. The same happens to
hydraulic systems: the masters who knew how to clear a qanat's gradient, balance a tank
cascade or regulate a canal's flow were a trained workforce whose skills lived in
practice. When the work stopped for a generation, the `engineering knowledge pool`
emptied, and restoring the system required rediscovering how it had worked. Paying for
repair is not enough if no one remains who can do it.

**Cascade: coupling transmits failure.** Tightly coupled networks propagate failure.
One node goes down, its load shifts to neighbours, the neighbours fail in turn, and a
local fault becomes a system-wide collapse in minutes. Perrow (Normal Accidents, 1984)
names the combination of tight coupling and interactive complexity as the condition. The
coupling that makes a network efficient is the same coupling that transmits its
collapse. The recurrence of this across four thousand years is the entry's strongest
claim:

- **The Late Bronze Age trade network** (COM-040) broke apart through a few failed nodes
  it had optimised around.
- **Angkor's hydraulic network** transmitted local damage through its interdependent
  channels.
- **Sri Lanka's tank cascades** passed breaches downstream.
- **Electrical grids** (ENG-300) failed across whole regions in 1965 and 2003 from a
  misset relay and an untrimmed tree. In September 2003 a tree flashover on a Swiss
  import line blacked out almost all of Italy, and India's northern grid failed for six
  hundred million people in 2012.
- **Just-in-time container logistics** (MAR-370) removes the buffer stocks that once
  absorbed a disruption.

In each case `network fragility` is the price of integration, and neglected maintenance
of a single component is what triggers it.

**Institutions can solve it, and the choice matters.** The problem is structural: the
people who benefit from maintenance are diffuse and future, and the people who decide
the budget are concentrated and present. Durable solutions place maintenance outside
the ordinary competition for the sovereign's budget:

- **Dutch water boards.** They taxed the landowners they protected and governed
  themselves, so dike repair did not wait on a count or a king.
- **Qanat and aflaj systems.** In Iran and Oman shares in the water carried shares in
  the cleaning obligation, so every user maintained the channel they drew from.
- **Chinese river conservancy.** Ming and Qing river directorates were permanent state
  offices with dedicated budgets, effective while the dynasty's finances held and
  vulnerable when they did not.

AGR-100 offers state-hydraulic control and local commons management as alternative
institutional forms for irrigation. This entry supplies the reason the choice matters.
A commons that binds each user to maintain survives a weak sovereign. A state system
maintained from the centre fails when the centre's finances fail.

## Social consequence

At human scale maintenance is invisible labour until its absence arrives as disaster:

- **Corvée crews** dredge Mesopotamian canals every season.
- **Village households** turn out to repair a Sri Lankan tank bund.
- **Dutch landowners** pay their water-board levy.
- **Linemen** trim trees along transmission corridors.

None of them produces anything new, and their success is nothing happening. The
disaster, when it comes, falls on people who had no say in the deferral. Farmers
drowned when the Yellow River broke out in 1855. The Diyala villages were abandoned
when the canals silted. The population of Rome shrank to what the river could supply.

The political verdict attaches to whoever is in power when the debt comes due, which is
rarely whoever accumulated it. In China a catastrophic flood was read as a sign that the
dynasty had lost the mandate of heaven, so the consequence of a predecessor's neglect
landed on the reigning emperor's legitimacy. The same asymmetry that makes rulers defer
maintenance makes their successors pay for it.

Maintenance debt is one route to failure; the absence of any standard is another.
ENG-166 carries buildings that fail because no code was set, and the codes that
disasters produced.

## Regional specifics

The premodern hydraulic cases lead, and most are outside Europe:

- **China.** The Yellow River shows maintenance tied to dynastic legitimacy, with a
  breach in 1855 that a war-drained treasury could not close.
- **Mesopotamia and Egypt.** They show canal systems that required perpetual dredging
  and failed when fiscal order broke down.
- **Sri Lanka.** Its tank cascades show communal maintenance and downstream cascade.
- **Angkor.** It shows an integrated hydraulic network transmitting climatic damage
  through its own connectivity.
- **Rome.** Its aqueducts show neglect recorded by the official responsible, then
  abandonment after war.
- **The Netherlands.** It supplies the most durable institutional solution.
- **The modern grid.** The northeastern American and Indian blackouts show the cascade
  mechanism running at continental scale.

The absences are the inert structures that need no upkeep. Great Zimbabwe's mortarless
walls and the earthworks of Cahokia and the Hopewell survive the societies that built
them, because nothing in them requires continuous repair. A technology with a lower
maintenance burden can outlast a more capable one that depended on care.

## Contested

Whether infrastructure collapse is driven by environmental shock or by failure to
maintain is disputed:

- **Environmental accounts** stress drought, flood and river shifts.
- **Tainter** stresses the rising cost of maintaining complexity.
- **McAnany and Yoffee** argue many collapses were reorganisations.

The Harappan case is increasingly read as climatic rather than a neglected system.

Model failure as conditional on both a shock and the `maintenance debt` accumulated when
it arrives. A maintained system absorbs a shock that destroys a neglected one, and some
shocks exceed any maintenance a society could have done.

## Hook

**Maintenance debt.** Every engineered system accumulates `maintenance debt`, the gap
between required and performed upkeep, while its nominal capacity appears unchanged.

- **The fiscal asymmetry.** Rulers gain from building and lose nothing visible from
  deferring repair, so debt grows fastest under short time horizons and fiscal crisis.
- **Failure.** Failure is sudden and non-linear once debt passes a threshold or a shock
  arrives, and its cost falls on a successor.

**Capability loss.** Deferred maintenance also drains the `engineering knowledge pool`,
so a system neglected long enough cannot be restored merely by paying.

**Cascade.** In tightly coupled networks `network fragility` turns a single neglected
component into system-wide collapse, and the coupling that makes the network efficient
is what transmits the failure.

**Institutional solutions.** Maintenance survives weak sovereigns only where it is
institutionally insulated. Examples are water boards with their own taxation and commons
that bind each user to clean their share.

**Inert structures.** Dry stone and earthworks carry no maintenance debt and outlast the
states that built them.
