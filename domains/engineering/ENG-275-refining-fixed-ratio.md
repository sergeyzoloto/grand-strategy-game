Crude oil is not a fuel. It is a mixture, and what a refinery does is
separate it into fractions — which sounds like a processing detail and is
in fact the constraint that determines what an oil economy can make, what
it wastes, and whether its air force can fly.

## Timing

Commercial fractional distillation from the early 1860s, thermal cracking
patented in 1913, catalytic cracking from 1937 — with the crucial
inversion of demand, from kerosene to gasoline, falling between the first
two.

## Mechanism

**A barrel yields what it yields.** Distillation separates crude by
boiling point into light gases, gasoline, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil and
residue, and the proportions are set by the chemistry of the particular
crude, not by what the refiner wants. This is a joint-production problem
of an unusually rigid kind: a refiner optimising for one fraction is
stuck with all the others in fixed proportion, and cannot make more of
the wanted product without making more of everything else.

The consequence is that **a mismatch between the yield ratio and the
demand ratio appears as glut and shortage simultaneously, in different
fractions of the same barrel.** Early refiners wanted kerosene for lamps;
gasoline was a light, volatile, dangerous by-product with almost no
market, and it was dumped in rivers, burned off, or run into pits as
waste. Within forty years the automobile inverted the demand completely,
and the waste became the principal product while kerosene became the
lesser one. **The refinery did not change. The demand did**, and the
fixed ratio turned a disposal problem into a supply constraint without
anything happening inside the plant at all.

**Cracking is what makes the ratio adjustable.** Thermal cracking
(Burton, 1913, with Shukhov's earlier Baku work as a real priority claim)
breaks heavy molecules into lighter ones under heat and pressure, raising
gasoline yield from a given barrel. Catalytic cracking (Houdry, 1937)
does it better, at lower temperature, with far more control over what
comes out. That converts a fixed joint-product problem into a
controllable one and is the single most consequential thing in this
entry.

**Catalytic cracking is a war dependency, not a chemistry footnote.**
What it enables is high-octane aviation gasoline in quantity. Octane
rating governs how much a piston engine can be compressed or
supercharged before the fuel detonates, so it converts directly into
manifold pressure and therefore into power, climb rate, and altitude from
the *same* engine. The difference between fuel grades is a difference in
aircraft performance available to a state without changing its airframes
or its engines — an upgrade delivered through the refinery rather than
the factory, and one that a state without catalytic capacity simply
cannot buy. It belongs in the corpus for that reason and connects
directly to WAR-270.

## Social consequence

Refining concentrates an industry that extraction disperses. Wells are
where the geology is; refineries are where the capital and the market
decide, which means the processing centre and the producing region are
routinely different places and different polities. That separation is
where most of the value and nearly all of the technical capability
accumulates, and it is the structural reason a territory can supply crude
for fifty years without acquiring an industry.

A refinery is also built for a particular crude. Feedstocks differ in
sulphur, density, and fraction profile, so a plant configured for one
source cannot simply switch to another — which makes a supply
relationship far stickier than a commodity purchase and turns the choice
of supplier into a long-term capital commitment. Buyers and sellers are
locked together by metal, not by contract.

The waste is worth carrying too. For the first decades the unwanted
fractions went into rivers and pits as a matter of routine, in volumes
that would later be treated as catastrophic, and they were unwanted
purely because no market yet existed for them. The same substance was
poison and then product, with no change in the substance.

## Regional specifics

Germany is the entry's most instructive case, because it shows the
constraint being defeated by engineering at a cost no market would ever
bear. Coal-rich and oil-poor, it built Bergius hydrogenation and
Fischer-Tropsch synthesis to make liquid fuel from coal — technically
successful, strategically rational, and never adequate in volume. This is
the alternative to the dependency WAR-270's aircraft and the navies
elsewhere in the corpus all rest on, and it is the clearest instance
available of a state paying an enormous efficiency penalty to convert an
import into a domestic product.

Japan's case isolates the distinction between crude and fuel, since its
binding shortage was refining capability and high-octane capacity rather
than access to crude alone — a state can hold oil and still lack fuel.
Baku's priority in continuous distillation and early thermal cracking is
well documented and routinely omitted from accounts written from the
American side. And the Sumatran case establishes early that crudes are
not interchangeable, which is the fact behind the feedstock lock-in
above.

## Hook

`refining flexibility` is the entry's variable and it starts near zero. A
barrel yields fractions in fixed proportion set by the crude, not by
demand, so a mismatch shows up as glut and shortage at the same moment in
different products — and a demand shift alone can turn a disposal cost
into a supply constraint with no change inside the plant, which is
precisely what the automobile did to gasoline. Cracking raises the
variable and makes the ratio steerable, and catalytic cracking
specifically should gate high-octane aviation fuel, delivering an
aircraft performance increase through the refinery rather than the
airframe — available to states with the capacity and unbuyable by those
without. Refineries are built for a specific crude, so switching supplier
is a capital decision rather than a purchasing one and locks buyer and
seller together. And a region that exports crude unrefined should
accumulate no capability from doing so, however long it supplies the
industry.
