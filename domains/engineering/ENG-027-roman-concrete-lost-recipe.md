`ENG-004` covers Roman concrete's achievement — the Pantheon's dome,
unmatched in unreinforced concrete span for over 1,700 years — as a
capability gain; this entry owns what happened next, which every other
entry in this domain's default growth framing has no equivalent for:
the specific technique was not merely superseded, it was genuinely
lost.

## Timing

Following the Western Roman Empire's 476 collapse, the specific
pozzolanic volcanic-ash concrete formulation behind `ENG-004`'s
monolithic-pour achievements falls out of use across the former Western
provinces; post-Roman European construction reverts to weaker lime-
mortar masonry for over a millennium, with comparable durability only
reverse-engineered through a different chemical pathway — Smeaton's
hydraulic-lime experiments (1750s) and, decisively, Portland cement's
1824 patent — rather than by recovering the original Roman method
itself.

## Mechanism

Roman concrete's specific durability and structural capability depended
on a particular volcanic-ash (pozzolana) additive, sourced from specific
deposits and distributed across the empire by a shipping and
administrative network that itself required sustained state capacity to
operate — once that network and the institutional knowledge transmission
sustaining specialist builders collapsed alongside the Western Empire,
both the specific material supply chain and the accumulated craft
knowledge of how to use it correctly stopped being reproduced, a
genuine `engineering knowledge pool` depletion (design principle 4)
rather than a simple technology being voluntarily abandoned for
something better.

## Social consequence

For over a millennium, no European builder could reproduce a dome or
vaulted span approaching the Pantheon's — not because the ambition
disappeared, but because the specific combination of raw material
access and transmitted technical knowledge that had made it possible no
longer existed anywhere to draw on, a stark case where a civilization's
collapse can erase a capability level that its far-later successors
have to painstakingly rebuild from a different starting point rather
than simply re-adopt.

## Regional specifics

The Byzantine East retains meaningfully more of late-Roman construction
sophistication than the Latin West — Hagia Sophia's 537 brick-and-
mortar engineering is genuinely advanced — without ever reproducing the
specific pozzolanic monolithic-pour technique itself, showing the loss
was partial and uneven rather than a single clean break. Islamic
architecture's entirely separate masonry-vaulting solution to the same
large-span problem (`ENG-004`'s squinch and muqarnas) shows a
civilization can solve the underlying architectural need without ever
needing to recover the specific lost Roman method at all.

## Contested

Recent materials-science research into Roman concrete's self-healing
lime-clast chemistry is still actively evolving and only partly
settled, complicating exactly why the material was so durable beyond
its pozzolanic composition; separately, how much of its disappearance
reflects forgotten chemistry versus economically-abandoned volcanic-ash
logistics is disputed. Model both the precise durability mechanism and
the completeness of the knowledge loss as open questions rather than
settled fact.

## Hook

`Engineering knowledge pool` doesn't merely stop growing when the
institution sustaining it collapses — it can regress below a level not
recovered for centuries, and recovery, when it comes, often arrives
through a different technical path (Portland cement) rather than by
rediscovering the original recipe — the domain's one clear case of
capability moving backward rather than forward.
