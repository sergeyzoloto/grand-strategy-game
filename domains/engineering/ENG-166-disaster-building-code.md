Building codes are written after disasters. A city burns, a city falls down, a boiler
explodes or a factory fire kills the workers locked inside it, and the rules that would
have prevented it are enacted in the years that follow. Each disaster raises the
minimum standard, and the standard rarely falls back.

## Timing

From the Great Fire of London in 1666 and its Rebuilding Act of 1667 — with Edo's
different response to the Meireki fire of 1657 as the contemporary alternative —
through Lisbon's rebuilding after 1755, the fire codes of Istanbul, Chicago and Meiji
Tokyo, the boiler and mine inspectorates of the nineteenth century, the Triangle
Shirtwaist fire of 1911, and the seismic codes of Japan after 1923, Quetta after 1935
and California after 1933. The date end marks the point at which national codes were
general in industrial states. Bhopal in 1984 is carried as a later case of the uneven
ratchet.

## Mechanism

**The same ratchet as MAR-337.** MAR-337 carries the maritime regulatory ratchet in
full: ordinary disagreement over who pays for safety blocks new rules, a visible
disaster breaks the deadlock, a standard is enacted, and it does not reverse. Visibility
to the publics who make the rules, not the number of deaths, decides whether it turns.
Building and industrial codes follow the same pattern and are not re-derived here. This
entry carries what is specific to buildings: why the collective action problem is
sharper on land, the three hazards that drive the codes, and the countervailing force
that turns a safety standard into a cause of informal construction. It raises a
`building safety floor`, the land counterpart of MAR-337's `maritime safety floor`.
LAW-075 carries the general case of catastrophe opening institutions to change; this
is the narrow technical instance, where the institution changed is the standard to
which things are built. ENG-065 carries infrastructure that fails because upkeep
lapsed; this entry carries buildings that fail because no standard was set, and the
two meet when a disaster falls on a neglected system built to no code.

**Why the market does not do this alone.** A house built of brick in a timber street
protects its neighbours as much as its owner, because urban fire spreads from building
to building. The owner pays the full cost and receives only part of the benefit, so
each owner underinvests and the whole street burns. Earthquake risk is less of an
externality — a building mostly falls on its own occupants — but the buyer or tenant
cannot see the steel in a wall or the bracing in a frame, so a builder saves money by
leaving it out. In industrial accidents the risk falls on workers who do not control
the equipment. In all three cases the person deciding how to build is not the person
who bears the risk, which is why the correction arrives as law rather than contract,
and why it waits for a disaster to overcome the builders' opposition.

**Fire: the code as a materials mandate.** The first codes are rules about what
buildings may be made of. London's Rebuilding Act required brick or stone. Chicago's
fire limits banned wood. Istanbul required masonry, and Meiji Tokyo mandated
fireproof construction along its main streets. Codes pushed cities from timber to
fired brick (ENG-040) and later to steel and concrete, and in doing so turned a local
fuel and building material into a prohibited one in city centres (ENG-055 carries
timber as fuel and as a claim on land; its role as a structural material is recorded
as a gap). Edo shows the alternative. It accepted that wooden houses would burn and
engineered the city around it — firebreaks, watchtowers, district brigades that pulled
down buildings downwind — because rebuilding in wood was cheap and brick was not. The
choice between a materials mandate and firebreak planning is a choice about who pays:
the builder of every house, or the city.

**Insurance became an enforcer.** Barbon's Fire Office followed the London fire and
FIN-090 carries the insurance that scaled from it. In Chicago in 1874 the fire
underwriters threatened to withdraw cover from the city unless it enforced its code,
and the city complied. Insurers price the neighbours' risk that owners ignore, so where
cover is needed to borrow or to trade, the underwriter enforces the code more reliably
than the building inspector — the same role classification societies play at sea.

**Earthquake: the code as a structural rule.** Seismic codes are rules about how a
building is held together, not what it is made of:

- **Lisbon, 1755.** Pombal's timber cage braced masonry buildings against shaking, and
  the state imposed it on a whole district.
- **Tokyo, 1923.** The Kanto earthquake produced the first national seismic design
  requirement in 1924, while its fires, which killed most of the victims, reinforced the
  fire code.
- **Quetta, 1935.** Railway engineers wrote South Asia's first seismic regulations.
- **California, 1933.** The Long Beach earthquake collapsed schools, and the Field Act
  set seismic standards for them. The ratchet turned on the buildings whose collapse
  was most visible, not on housing generally.

Steel frames (ENG-310) made tall buildings possible and brought fireproofing and
structural rules with them.

**Industrial accidents: the code as inspection.** Boilers, mines and factories
produced a third line of codes, enforced by inspectors rather than by rules on
materials:

- **Boilers.** Steam boilers (ENG-170) exploded often and lethally in the early
  nineteenth century. The United States Steamboat Act of 1852 created one of the first
  federal inspection services. In Britain the Manchester Steam Users' Association of
  1854 inspected privately, and in the United States the Hartford Steam Boiler
  Inspection and Insurance Company from 1866 joined inspection to insurance.
- **Mines.** Britain's coal mines inspectorate dates from 1850. After the Hartley
  Colliery disaster of 1862 trapped 204 men behind a broken engine beam in a single
  shaft, a second exit was required by law. The Courrieres explosion of 1906 in France
  killed more than a thousand miners.
- **Factories.** The Triangle Shirtwaist fire of 1911 produced New York's
  factory-safety laws within a few years.

Workers' own organisation (LAW-200) was a large part of what turned the ratchet in
industry. A disaster gave unions and reformers a case they had already been making.

**The ratchet turns unevenly.** Where the victims are poor, foreign, or distant from
the people who make the rules, the ratchet turns less or somewhere else. The Benxihu
colliery explosion of 1942 in occupied Manchuria killed about 1,500 Chinese miners, the
worst mine disaster ever recorded, and changed nothing under the occupying power. After
Bhopal in 1984 the law moved further in the United States, where the company was
based, than in India, where the dead were.

**The countervailing force: codes push the poor outside the code.** A code raises the
cost of legal construction. Where most people can pay, the city becomes safer. Where
they cannot, they build without permission, outside the fire limits, on land where no
inspector goes, and the safety standard creates the informal city it cannot reach:

- **Chicago, 1872.** Immigrant homeowners fought the extension of the fire limits,
  because brick was beyond them and wood was not.
- **Hong Kong, 1953.** The squatter settlement at Shek Kip Mei burned on Christmas Day
  and left more than fifty thousand homeless, which pushed the colonial government into
  building resettlement housing. The response to fire in informal housing was to
  replace it rather than to regulate it.
- **Guatemala, 1976.** The earthquake killed about 23,000 people, overwhelmingly in the
  adobe houses of the poor, and was called a "class-quake" at the time.

Disaster mortality concentrates in the housing that codes do not reach, and as cities
grow faster than their `urban absorption capacity` (MIG-170), that housing is where
most new residents live.

## Social consequence

At human scale the code arrives as a rule on what a family may build:

- **A London householder in 1667.** They must rebuild in brick to a height and wall
  thickness fixed by the width of the street they face.
- **An Edo townsman.** They rebuild in wood within weeks, and the brigade of their
  district will pull their house down to stop the next fire.
- **An immigrant homeowner in Chicago in 1872.** They cannot afford brick, and they
  march against the ordinance.
- **A mine worker after 1862.** They have a second shaft to escape by because 204 men
  did not.
- **A garment worker in New York after 1911.** The door of their workshop can no
  longer be locked.

The safety each code creates was paid for by the people who died before it and is
enjoyed by those who can afford to comply. The political fight is rarely over whether
buildings should be safe. It is over who pays for the safety, and whether the people
who cannot pay are allowed to build at all.

## Regional specifics

The regions are handled as deliberately different routes rather than as copies of
London:

- **Europe.** London and Lisbon supply the first fire and seismic codes imposed on a
  whole city.
- **East Asia.** Edo supplies the most developed alternative to a materials mandate, and
  Japan after 1923 the first national seismic code.
- **The Middle East.** Istanbul's Tanzimat regulations rebuilt the city fire by fire.
- **South Asia.** Quetta produced the region's first seismic regulations, while Kashmir
  held seismic resistance in craft rather than law.
- **The Americas.** Chicago shows insurers as enforcers and fire limits drawn around
  poorer districts, New York the factory code, and California seismic rules aimed at
  schools. Guatemala shows mortality concentrating outside the code.

The absences are places where the externality the code corrects does not arise. Kashmir
had builders who already built to a learned seismic standard, and the steppe had
housing that could not carry a conflagration.

## Contested

Whether codes save lives or relocate risk is disputed:

- **Engineering evidence** shows enforced fire and seismic codes reduce deaths in
  buildings built to them, and Chile, which raised its code after earthquakes in 1928
  and 1939, dies less in comparable shaking.
- **Critics of code-led housing policy** (Turner, Housing by People, 1976) argue codes
  price the poor out of legal building and concentrate mortality in informal settlement.

Model a code's effect as conditional on enforcement capacity and on the cost gap between
legal and informal construction.

## Hook

**The ratchet.** A disaster raises the `building safety floor` and the floor does not
fall back, following MAR-337's ratchet: deadlock over who pays, broken by a disaster
visible to the people who make the rules.

- **Fire.** Fire codes are materials mandates that move cities from timber to brick,
  steel and concrete. Firebreak planning, as in Edo, is the cheaper alternative that
  accepts burning houses and limits spread.
- **Earthquake.** Seismic codes are structural rules and turn first on the buildings
  whose collapse is most visible.
- **Industry.** Industrial codes run through inspection and insurance, and turn faster
  where workers are organised.

**Enforcement.** Insurers enforce codes where cover is needed to borrow or trade, often
better than inspectors.

**Uneven turning.** The ratchet turns less where victims are poor, colonised or distant
from the rule-makers.

**The countervailing force.** Every rise in the floor raises the cost of legal
construction. Where that cost exceeds what residents can pay, construction moves
outside the code, and disaster mortality concentrates in informal housing, most sharply
when urban growth outruns `urban absorption capacity`.
