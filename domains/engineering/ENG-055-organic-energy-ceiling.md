Every pre-fossil economy runs on sunlight captured this year. That single
fact sets a ceiling on what such an economy can do, and the ceiling is
made of land — which is why energy, food, and timber are not three
separate problems but one allocation.

## Timing

From roughly -3300, when urban bronze-age metallurgy first creates a
non-food fuel demand large enough to force a land-allocation decision,
through the iron age's much larger draw, to the fossil escape from
around 1800. The constraint is continuous throughout; what varies is how
hard it binds and what each region does about it.

## Mechanism

An organic economy's total energy supply is bounded by **the annual
photosynthetic yield of the land it controls**. Firewood, charcoal,
fodder for draft animals, and food for human muscle are all current
solar income, captured on a surface, and the surface is finite. Wind and
water add a modest increment at fixed sites (ENG-120) but do not change
the arithmetic, because they cannot be concentrated or stored and they
do not scale with demand.

The consequence is that **energy competes directly with food and timber
for the same acreage, and the competition is zero-sum within a fixed
territory**. Charcoal for a furnace is woodland not cleared for grain.
Fodder for draft animals is arable not growing human calories — and a
working horse eats the produce of several acres that would otherwise
feed people, which is why animal traction is a genuine energy investment
rather than a free gain. Growth in any one use has to come out of
another. This is why organic economies hit walls rather than curves: a
region can intensify agriculture, expand metallurgy, or build a fleet,
but not all three, and past a point not any of them, because the land is
already fully allocated.

**Why the ceiling starts binding when it does.** Domestic cooking and
heating is a real draw but a small one against an unenclosed landscape,
and it constrains where a settlement sits rather than what an economy
can do. What forces allocation is smelting. Charcoal iron consumes wood
at tonnes per tonne of metal, and a single sustained furnace draws the
annual increment of a large area continuously — the first demand
anywhere big enough to make a society choose between fuel and food on
the same ground. That is why this entry starts at the bronze-age urban
horizon and not at the origin of agriculture: the constraint is
arithmetically present much earlier and binds on nothing until there is
an industry to bind on.

**Fossil fuel removes the competition rather than easing it.** Coal and
oil are stored solar income, accumulated over geological time, drawn
down at whatever rate extraction allows and costing no acreage at all.
The shift is not an increase in supply within the same system; it is an
exit from the system, which is Wrigley's organic-to-mineral distinction
and the deepest available explanation for why industrialisation happened
where and when it did. Everything downstream in this domain — ENG-170,
ENG-300, and the whole industrial chain under them — sits on the far
side of that exit and should be gated on it.

## Social consequence

Fuel is a household's largest physical input after food, and gathering
it is continuous, heavy, and overwhelmingly women's and children's work
in most of the societies this entry covers. As accessible wood recedes,
the labour cost of fuel rises before its price does, and the rise is
paid in time by people whose time is not priced — which is why fuel
scarcity is visible in household labour patterns long before it appears
in any market record.

The competition also runs directly through the household's other
decisions. Burning dung heats the meal and costs the field its
fertiliser (AGR-110); keeping a draft animal buys traction and spends
the acreage that would have fed a person. These are not metaphors for
the allocation problem, they are the allocation problem, made at
household scale in the same terms a polity faces at territorial scale.

Where the ceiling binds hard, access to fuel becomes a governed right
rather than a common one. Cutting rights, forest law, and the
criminalisation of ordinary gathering follow wherever woodland becomes
scarce enough to be worth controlling — which turns a subsistence
activity into a punishable one and makes the ceiling a matter of law and
policing rather than only of physics.

## Regional specifics

Tokugawa Japan is the case that matters most and it should lead, because
it is the only major polity to meet the ceiling squarely and solve it
without fossil fuel. Having stripped its accessible forest through the
seventeenth century, it responded with allocation of cutting rights,
regulation, and systematic replanting, and arrived at a sustainable
high-yield silviculture rather than at collapse or at coal. Modelling
that as a failure to industrialise gets it exactly backwards under
design principle 5 — it is a different solution to the same constraint,
and a durable one.

Song China is the complementary case, hitting the ceiling in its iron
industry around Kaifeng and escaping it by substituting coal roughly
seven centuries before England. Together the two East Asian cases
establish that neither the constraint nor the escape route was peculiar
to Europe, which is the finding that makes the whole entry worth having.
Mesopotamia supplies the inverse — an economy that never had timber,
imported cedar at long range, and substituted mudbrick and reed from the
beginning. Sub-Saharan African smelting shows the ceiling as a
constraint on where industry can sit, with sites relocating as wood is
worked out. South Asia supplies the household-scale version in the
dung-versus-fertiliser tension. England is one instance among these, and
its distinction lies in where its coal happened to be rather than in how
hard its ceiling bound.

## Contested

How hard the ceiling actually bound in any specific case is disputed.
Classical Mediterranean deforestation was asserted for a long time on
literary evidence and has been substantially revised by palynology, and
the English timber crisis has been read both as a genuine physical
shortage and as a price signal reflecting the cost of moving wood rather
than an absolute scarcity of it. The right model treats the ceiling as
binding **conditionally on transport cost between fuel source and
consumption point** rather than as an absolute physical limit, because a
forest that cannot be moved cheaply is functionally absent whether or
not it stands.

Separately, Wrigley's framing is contested by institutionalist accounts
that treat energy as permissive rather than causal. Model the ceiling's
relief as a necessary condition that enables rather than compels, so
that a polity with coal and no institutions does not industrialise, and
neither does one with institutions and no energy. Both failures should
be available and they should look different.

## Hook

A hard cap on total usable energy, set by the annual photosynthetic yield
of controlled land, which makes fuel, food, fodder, and timber four
claims on one finite surface rather than four separate supplies. `energy
budget` cannot be raised by investment within the organic regime, only
reallocated — so expanding metallurgy costs arable, and animal traction
is bought with the acreage that would have fed people, which should read
as a real trade against `yield ceiling` rather than a free gain. The
constraint should bind conditionally on the transport cost between fuel
and furnace, not as an absolute stock, since unreachable woodland is
functionally absent. Three exits exist and they should be genuinely
distinct: substitute to a stored stock and leave the regime entirely
(ENG-165), regulate and replant to a sustainable equilibrium inside it as
Tokugawa Japan did, or relocate industry as local fuel is exhausted, as
African smelting did. Relief is necessary but not sufficient — energy
without institutions industrialises no better than institutions without
energy.
