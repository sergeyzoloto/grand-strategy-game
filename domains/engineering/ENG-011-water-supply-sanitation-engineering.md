The disease-dynamics consequence of water and waste infrastructure is
HLT-006's territory; this entry owns the actual construction technique —
gradient calculation, channel and pipe materials, tunneling, and the
structural engineering that gets water in and waste out reliably.

## Timing

Indus Valley covered drains and household water connections date to
roughly -2500; qanat technology develops in Persia from around -800; Roman
aqueduct engineering scales from roughly -300 through the empire's height;
comparable capability isn't rebuilt in post-Roman Europe until the
19th-century sanitary engineering movement.

## Mechanism

Every water-supply or waste-removal system solves the same underlying
problem — moving water against or with gravity reliably over distance — but
the specific engineering challenge varies sharply by terrain and climate:
a qanat's gently-sloped underground tunnel minimizes evaporation loss
across arid terrain by keeping water shaded and enclosed the entire route,
while a Roman aqueduct's above-ground arched channels prioritize precise
long-distance gradient control across varied terrain where evaporation loss
matters less than maintaining exact fall over dozens of kilometers.

## Social consequence

Building and maintaining large-scale hydraulic infrastructure requires
sustained surveying precision (gradient errors compound catastrophically
over long distances) and ongoing maintenance labor, concentrating the
technical expertise required in a specialized engineering class whose
knowledge, once lost (as in post-Roman Europe), takes generations to
reconstruct rather than simply being re-learned quickly from first
principles.

## Regional specifics

The qanat's gravity-fed underground design, requiring zero pumping energy,
solves specifically for arid-climate evaporation loss and low energy
availability, and transmits successfully across an enormous geographic
range from Persia through Central Asia to Islamic Spain, showing the
technique's design logic generalizes well across similar climate
conditions regardless of political boundaries. Roman aqueduct engineering
solves a related but distinct problem — precise gradient control over long
above-ground distances — reflecting the Mediterranean's more reliable
surface water availability rather than the arid-groundwater-access problem
qanats address.

## Hook

Water-supply engineering technique is shaped entirely by local terrain and
climate — arid-region gravity-fed tunnels versus above-ground gradient-
controlled channels are different solutions to different underlying
problems, not a hierarchy of technique — and the surveying and maintenance
expertise required is exactly what HLT-006 shows can be lost wholesale
and take generations to rebuild once the sustaining institution collapses.
