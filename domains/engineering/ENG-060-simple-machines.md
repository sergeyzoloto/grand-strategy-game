The foundational mechanical-advantage toolkit — lever, pulley, screw,
wedge, and inclined plane — that converts a task requiring more force
than is locally available into one requiring less force applied over a
greater distance or longer time, the precondition this domain's
monumental construction entries have been quietly assuming since its
earliest cases.

## Timing

Practical use of levers, ramps, and rope-and-pulley systems for
monumental construction is attested from at least -3000 across multiple
independent traditions; Archimedes' formal mathematical treatment of
the lever principle, in the 3rd century BCE, converts long-standing
practical technique into calculable engineering theory.

## Mechanism

Each simple machine trades force for distance or time in a fixed,
calculable ratio: a lever lets a smaller force applied over a longer
arm move a larger load a shorter distance; a pulley system (particularly
a compound block-and-tackle arrangement) divides a load's effective
weight among multiple rope segments at the cost of pulling proportionally
more rope; a screw converts rotational force applied over many turns
into substantial linear force or lift. None of these devices creates
force from nothing — they convert an available force too small for a
task, applied directly, into an effective force large enough, applied
indirectly — which is what makes raising a multi-ton stone block or
tensioning a bridge cable achievable with human or animal muscle power
alone.

## Social consequence

Because mechanical-advantage devices convert available labor into
effective lifting and moving capacity at a calculable ratio, they
directly determine how large a stone block, how heavy a load, or how
wide a span a given labor force can actually manage — a society's
practical construction ceiling is set jointly by its available labor
and by how well it has developed this toolkit, not by labor availability
alone.

## Regional specifics

Archimedes' rigorous lever-principle mathematics converts already
widespread practical technique into calculable theory, letting later
engineering (including this domain's later crane and hoist
applications) be planned rather than only refined through trial.
Chinese well-sweep and windlass devices reach comparable practical
sophistication through entirely independent invention. Andean and
Mesoamerican monumental construction's reliance on organized mass labor
rather than mechanical-advantage devices shows the underlying
force-versus-distance problem this entry's toolkit solves has at least
one other viable solution — enough coordinated hands moving a load
directly, without ever needing to develop lever or pulley systems at
comparable sophistication.

## Hook

Mechanical advantage converts available force into effective force at a
fixed, calculable ratio — a gate this domain's monumental construction
entries (`ENG-020`'s dam earthworks, `ENG-090`'s bridge spans, `ENG-110`'s
dome centering) have been assuming exists without this record
previously establishing it, with the specific ratio achievable in a
given era and region setting a real, trackable ceiling on what scale of
load that era's construction can actually manage.
