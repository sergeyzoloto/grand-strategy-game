A compact, self-contained engine burning fuel directly inside its own
cylinders rather than using external combustion to heat a separate working
fluid (as a steam engine does) — lighter and more power-dense per unit
weight, which is what makes practical automobiles, and later aircraft
(WAR-270), possible.

## Timing

Nikolaus Otto's four-stroke engine (1876) establishes the basic practical
design; Rudolf Diesel's compression-ignition variant (from the 1890s) adds
a more fuel-efficient alternative; mass-market automotive application
matures with Ford's Model T and moving assembly line (COM-170) by 1913.

## Mechanism

What the engine burns is not incidental to it. An internal combustion
engine requires a refined light fraction delivered to the point of use at
consistent quality, which is a far stricter requirement than a steam
engine's — a boiler will burn coal, wood, dung, or oil with only a
performance penalty, while this engine runs on gasoline or it does not
run. The dependency is therefore on ENG-275's refining rather than on
crude, and the distinction is mechanically real.

The relationship runs both ways, and this is the more interesting half.
Gasoline was a waste fraction with no market, dumped or burned off,
until this engine created demand for it — so the automobile did not
merely consume a product, it **inverted the refinery's ratio problem**,
turning the fraction refiners had been paying to dispose of into the one
they could not make enough of. An entire industry's economics reversed
because of what this machine happened to require.

A steam engine's power-to-weight ratio is fundamentally limited by needing
a separate boiler and external firebox to generate the working steam; an
internal combustion engine burns fuel directly inside compact cylinders,
achieving far higher power output relative to the engine's own weight and
size — this power-density advantage is specifically what makes personal
road vehicles and, later, powered flight practical in a way a steam-powered
equivalent never achieved at comparable scale.

## Social consequence

Mass-market automobiles built on this engine and Ford's moving-assembly-
line factory discipline (COM-170) restructure personal mobility and urban
geography around private vehicle ownership over the following decades, a
transformation of daily life and city design as significant in its own
domain as railways were for long-distance transport a century earlier.

## Regional specifics

Ford's Model T converts the automobile from an expensive novelty into a
genuine mass-market good within a few years specifically by combining the
internal combustion engine with assembly-line production (COM-170),
neither innovation alone being sufficient for that outcome. Japanese
automotive capability developing only from the 1920s-1930s, later than
several other industrial technologies this record documents Japan adopting
comparatively quickly, shows technology-adoption speed isn't uniform even
within one country's broader modernization program — some capabilities
simply take longer to build domestically regardless of institutional
intent to adopt them — and in this case the lag sits against a domestic
petroleum absence (ENG-265) that made mass motorisation a fuel-supply
problem as much as a manufacturing one.

The absences make the sharper point. An engine is useless without a fuel
network behind it, and across colonial Africa and the steppe interior the
machine was available long before the distribution was. In a region with
no refuelling points an engine extends range only to the next place fuel
can be bought, which can be less far than an animal that refuels from the
ground it crosses — so the technology is not merely slow to arrive but
genuinely worse than what it replaces until the network precedes it.

## Hook

Internal combustion's power-to-weight advantage over steam is what makes
personal road vehicles and eventually powered flight (WAR-270) practical —
but the mass-market automobile specifically required pairing this engine
with assembly-line production (COM-170) simultaneously, neither the engine
nor the production method alone being sufficient for the transformation
each is usually credited with individually.
