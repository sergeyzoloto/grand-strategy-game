The exit from ENG-055's ceiling. Coal is stored solar income rather than
current flow, which changes not how much energy is available but what
kind of quantity energy is — and almost everything else follows from
that one change.

## Timing

From the mid-sixteenth-century English shift, with Song-dynasty
substitution five centuries earlier as the precedent that did not
generalise, through the deep-pit era and the drainage problem that
produced the steam engine, to the fully-formed coal economies of the
nineteenth century.

## Mechanism

**Coal converts energy from a flow into a stock.** A woodland yields a
sustainable annual increment indefinitely and cannot be made to yield
more by wanting more; a seam yields whatever can be lifted out of it and
is then gone. Three consequences follow, and they are the entry.

First, **output becomes bounded by engineering rather than by biology**.
Raising organic energy supply means waiting for trees, and no amount of
capital shortens it. Raising coal output means better pumps, better
haulage, and deeper shafts — all of which respond to investment
immediately. Energy supply becomes something a society can decide to
have more of, which is not true of anything in ENG-055.

Second, **the resource is geographically fixed and cannot be moved
cheaply relative to its value**. Coal is bulky and low-value per tonne,
so the delivered price rises steeply with distance from the pithead. The
consequence is that **industry relocates to the coal rather than the
coal to the industry**, which reorganises settlement, drags population
into places chosen by geology, and makes the map of industrialisation a
map of coalfields with transport access. This is also why coal endowment
alone predicts very little and delivered price at the point of use
predicts a great deal.

Third, **depth imposes an escalating cost with a specific binding
constraint — water**. Every pit floods, the flooding worsens with depth,
and drainage is the limit on how deep a field can be worked. This is the
loop that matters most for the corpus: the steam engine was invented to
pump water out of coal mines, and it then burned coal to do it, so the
fuel paid for the machine that made more of the fuel accessible. **The
causal arrow runs in both directions and the corpus should carry it in
both**, which is why ENG-170 takes this entry as a prereq rather than
merely referencing it.

The escape from the organic ceiling is not a larger energy budget within
the same system. It is an exit: coal costs no acreage, so it does not
compete with food, fodder, or timber at all, and the zero-sum allocation
that governs every pre-fossil economy simply stops applying.

## Social consequence

Mining creates an occupational population unlike any other in this
record — concentrated in single-industry settlements sited by geology
rather than by agriculture or trade, doing work that is dangerous in a
routine rather than exceptional way, and with no alternative employer
within reach. That combination produces unusually strong collective
organisation and unusually bitter disputes, because the workforce cannot
disperse and the employer cannot relocate. The pit village is a distinct
social form and it appears wherever deep mining does, largely
independent of national context.

The health cost is large, continuous, and falls in two places rather than
one. Underground it is injury and dust disease; above ground it is the
smoke, which turns coal-burning cities into a permanent respiratory
burden affecting people with no connection to the industry at all. Coal
is the first energy source in this record whose `toxic burden` is
substantially paid by non-users, and the delay between the exposure and
the illness is long enough that the cost is systematically
under-attributed while it is being incurred.

## Regional specifics

Song China is the control case and the reason the entry is contested
rather than triumphal. Coal substitution, at scale, with sophisticated
metallurgy and no industrial revolution — which means the fuel cannot be
the sufficient condition, and any model that treats coal as the trigger
is falsified by the best-documented early case. Pomeranz's answer is
delivered price: Chinese coal sat in the northwest and the demand sat in
the Yangtze delta, while English coal sat beside both its market and its
water transport. That is a concrete, measurable variable and it is the
one the model should use.

The American case runs the opposite causal path — abundant timber, cheap
water power, no binding ceiling, and coal adopted late for its
metallurgical qualities rather than out of scarcity. Same fuel, same
century, different cause, and it should not be modelled as the same
event. India's Raniganj and South Africa's Witwatersrand show coal
developed to serve something else, transport in one case and gold
extraction in the other, producing coal economies that never became
manufacturing economies.

## Contested

The Great Divergence question sits on this entry and should be modelled
rather than answered. Pomeranz's geographic explanation, the
demand-structure and high-wage explanations, the institutional
explanations, and the objection that the comparison unit should be
Jiangnan against England rather than China against Europe are all live.

The conditional to build: outcome depends on **delivered coal price at
the point of consumption** — pithead output, haulage distance, and
water-transport access together — rather than on coal endowment, **and**
requires an independent demand and institutional condition alongside it.
Cheap coal alone should not trigger industrialisation, and demand without
cheap coal should not either. The Song case is what makes the second
clause mandatory, and a model that industrialises Song China is wrong on
the evidence.

## Hook

The exit from `energy budget`'s organic cap. Coal costs no acreage, so it
does not compete with food, fodder, or timber, and the zero-sum land
allocation of ENG-055 stops applying — but it is a stock, so it depletes
rather than renews, and `extraction depth cost` should rise continuously
as a field is worked, with water ingress as the binding limit rather than
the seam running out. Because it is bulky and low-value per tonne, the
delivered price rises steeply with haulage distance, which means industry
must move to the coal and the map of industrialisation becomes a map of
coalfields with water access. Endowment should predict almost nothing and
delivered price at the point of use almost everything. The drainage
problem should generate ENG-170 as a requirement rather than a
convenience, closing a loop in which the fuel pays for the machine that
reaches more of the fuel. And `toxic burden` accrues to a population that
includes people who never touch the industry, on a delay long enough that
it is under-attributed while it is being paid.
