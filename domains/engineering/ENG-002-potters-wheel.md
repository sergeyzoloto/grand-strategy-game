A rotating platform that lets a potter shape symmetrical vessels far faster
and more consistently than hand-building allows, converting pottery from a
slow, individually-variable craft into an early standardized, semi-mass-
producible good.

## Timing

The slow wheel is attested from roughly -4500 in the Middle East, with the
faster kick-wheel or momentum-wheel technique developing by around -3500 and
spreading or independently arising across multiple regions through -1500.

## Mechanism

A spinning wheel lets centrifugal force and the potter's steady hand
control do the shaping work a hand-building technique would otherwise
require slow, deliberate coil-by-coil construction to achieve, cutting
production time per vessel dramatically while also producing far more
consistent wall thickness and symmetry — the specific consistency gain is
what makes wheel-thrown pottery practical to produce at the volume and
standardization the containerized cooking and fermentation practices in
HLT-004 depend on.

## Social consequence

Wheel-thrown pottery's speed advantage shifts production from a general
household skill toward a specialized, faster, higher-volume craft
practiced by dedicated potters supplying a wider market, an early instance
of the same craft-specialization-for-volume logic later manufactories
(COM-020) apply more systematically to other goods.

## Regional specifics

Chinese ceramic technique develops on an independent trajectory reaching
extremely high technical sophistication (eventually high-fired stoneware
and porcelain), feeding directly into the manufactory-scale production
already covered at Jingdezhen. The wheel's total absence from the
pre-contact Americas despite genuinely sophisticated hand-built ceramic
traditions tracks the same wheeled-technology and draft-animal absence
already noted for wagons and general transport in the migration domain —
ceramic ambition and skill were clearly present without the wheel, which
depended on unrelated technological preconditions.

## Hook

The potter's wheel converts vessel-making from a slow individually-variable
craft into a faster, standardized one — the same specialization-for-
consistency logic later manufactories apply more broadly, and its complete
absence in a region with otherwise sophisticated ceramics traces to
unrelated technology gaps (wheeled transport, draft animals) rather than
any deficit in ceramic skill itself.
