Kiln-firing converts raw clay into a durable, weather-resistant,
dimensionally-standardized modular building unit — a structural and
durability step change from sun-dried mudbrick, which crumbles under
sustained moisture and can't bear comparable load, and the single most
widely-used premodern building material this record had, until now, no
entry for.

## Timing

Fired brick production is attested from around -3500 in the Middle
East; Indus Valley civilization brick manufacturing reaches its
characteristic standardized form by around -2600; brick construction at
monumental scale, including fired-brick facing over mudbrick cores,
matures across multiple independent traditions by around -1000.

## Mechanism

Sun-dried mudbrick is cheap and fast to produce but remains vulnerable
to erosion and structural softening under sustained moisture exposure;
firing clay in a kiln at sufficient temperature vitrifies it into a
hard, water-resistant ceramic material that holds its shape and load-
bearing capacity indefinitely under weather exposure that would
gradually dissolve its unfired equivalent — at the direct cost of the
fuel and kiln-construction investment firing requires, which is why
many traditions use fired brick selectively (as a durable facing or for
specific high-exposure elements) rather than throughout an entire
structure.

## Social consequence

Producing fired brick at consistent, standardized dimensions requires
coordinated mold-making and kiln-firing practice across what could
otherwise be many independent, inconsistent local producers — the same
cross-site consistency this record already credits to Indus Valley
market-weight standardization shows up identically in that
civilization's brick dimensions, suggesting a shared underlying
administrative or craft-guild discipline rather than two unrelated
coincidences.

## Regional specifics

Indus Valley brick's remarkably consistent standard ratio across
widely-separated sites parallels and reinforces `COM-012`'s own
weights-and-measures case for the same civilization. Mesopotamian
ziggurats' two-material strategy — cheap mudbrick core, weatherproof
fired-brick shell — shows fired brick's higher cost was deliberately
targeted at exactly the structural role that needed it, not applied
uniformly by default. The Americas' reliance on stone and adobe instead
of fired brick shows the underlying durability problem fired brick
solves has more than one viable material solution, with dressed stone
substituting directly where it's the locally-abundant option.

## Hook

Firing converts cheap, moisture-vulnerable mudbrick into a durable,
weatherproof, standardized modular unit at a real fuel-and-kiln cost —
a modifier on construction durability and lifespan that traditions
apply selectively (facing, high-exposure elements) rather than
uniformly, with the specific fired-versus-unfired-versus-stone choice
in any given region set by local material cost and availability, not by
one material's universal superiority.
