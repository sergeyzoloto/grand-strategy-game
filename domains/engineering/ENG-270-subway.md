Moving rail transport underground to add transport capacity without
consuming scarce and expensive surface land — a distinct engineering
challenge from surface railways (COM-200), requiring tunneling, ventilation,
and drainage solutions surface construction never needs.

## Timing

London's Metropolitan Railway (1863) is the first underground urban
railway; the model spreads internationally with Istanbul's funicular
(1875), Buenos Aires's system (1913, the first in Latin America), and
Tokyo's subway (from 1927) among the earlier adopters outside the original
London context.

## Mechanism

Tunneling under an already-built, densely-occupied city adds an entire
layer of engineering challenge surface railways never face — ventilation
for smoke and later electric-train heat, continuous groundwater drainage,
and construction that must avoid undermining the building foundations
directly overhead — but the payoff is transport capacity added without
consuming any of the scarce, expensive surface land a comparable-capacity
surface rail line or road widening would require.

## Social consequence

Subway systems let a city's built-up core keep adding transit capacity
without further surface disruption or land acquisition, directly enabling
continued urban density increase and the daily commuting patterns that
industrial internal migration (MIG-170) depends on once a city's surface
street capacity is already saturated.

## Regional specifics

Istanbul's early funicular and Buenos Aires's early full system both show
the technology adopted and implemented well outside the London-Paris-
New York core comparatively quickly, complicating any simple center-to-
periphery diffusion timeline. The complete absence of subway construction
in Africa during this era reflects urban density and transit-demand
conditions that hadn't yet reached the threshold driving subway
investment elsewhere, rather than any lack of access to the underlying
tunneling engineering knowledge itself.

## Hook

Underground construction trades substantial additional engineering
complexity (ventilation, drainage, foundation-safe tunneling) for transit
capacity that consumes no scarce surface land — a tradeoff only worth
making once a city's surface density and transit demand cross a threshold,
which several non-Western cities reached and acted on comparatively early.
