Beyond meat, a domesticated animal population's full economic value is
only unlocked by a second technological shift — the "Secondary Products
Revolution" — that reorganizes herding around milk, wool, manure, and
traction instead of slaughter alone, multiplying the same animal stock's
lifetime economic yield without requiring any additional domestication
event.

## Timing

Initial livestock domestication (sheep, goat, cattle, pig in the Fertile
Crescent) proceeds from roughly -8500; the secondary-products shift —
animals kept alive and worked for renewable output rather than
slaughtered primarily for meat — follows over the next several
millennia, reaching clear archaeological visibility (wear patterns on
cattle bones consistent with plow traction, textile evidence of wool
processing) by around -4000 to -3500.

## Mechanism

A slaughtered animal yields one meat harvest; a milked, sheared, or
traction-worked animal yields renewable output across its entire
productive lifespan instead — the same domesticated stock supports a far
larger total economic return once herding practice reorganizes around
keeping animals alive and working rather than around meat extraction
alone, at the cost of the more intensive year-round management (feeding,
milking, harnessing) that renewable exploitation requires HLT-060's
dairying-specific nutritional and genetic angle is a downstream
consequence of this broader reorganization, not the whole of it.

## Social consequence

Herding built around renewable secondary products requires substantially
more continuous daily labor and closer human-animal proximity than
meat-focused herding, restructuring pastoral life around milking
schedules, wool-processing seasons, and draft-animal care as recurring
work rather than periodic slaughter — a labor-intensity shift that also,
per HLT-030's mechanism, raises sustained zoonotic exposure through
closer and more constant animal contact.

## Regional specifics

Chinese pig and chicken domestication, and the later addition of water
buffalo specifically for paddy traction, shows the secondary-products
logic recurring on an entirely independent species base rather than
being a Fertile-Crescent-specific development. The Andean llama/alpaca/
guinea pig package, domesticated with zero Old World contact, never
develops a comparably diversified secondary-products economy — pack
transport and wool, but no dairying tradition at all — showing a full
secondary-products revolution isn't a guaranteed consequence of
livestock domestication itself but depends on which specific traits the
locally available species happen to offer. Sub-Saharan Africa's tsetse-
constrained domesticate roster caps the whole mechanism at its source in
most of the continent's productive land, regardless of pastoralist
social organization.

## Hook

The secondary-products shift multiplies a domesticated animal stock's
lifetime economic yield without any new domestication event, at the cost
of year-round management intensity and (per HLT-030) sustained zoonotic
exposure — and which specific secondary products (milk, wool, traction,
all three, or none) a region's locally available species can actually
support is a hard ceiling set by species roster, not by herding
ambition.
