This entry owns the specific agronomic technique — swidden (slash-and-
burn) cultivation and its soil-fertility-cycling logic — that makes
farming-population expansion into new, often forested, ecological zones
agriculturally viable at all; MIG-020's demic-wave mechanism and MIG-090's
settler-risk mechanic own the migration process itself.

## Timing

Swidden cultivation techniques are attested from multiple independent
regions from roughly -7000 onward, remaining a primary frontier-expansion
technique through the long, slow farming waves this record tracks
elsewhere (MIG-020), continuing in some regions as a stable steady-state
practice through the 1800s and, in reduced form, beyond.

## Mechanism

Swidden cultivation clears forest by burning, which briefly boosts
`soil fertility` through ash-deposited nutrients before that boost
depletes over a small number of growing seasons faster than continuous
cultivation without fallow could sustain — the technique's core logic is
therefore cyclical rather than permanent: a plot is cleared, farmed
intensively while fertility is high, then abandoned to regrow forest and
rebuild fertility while a new plot is cleared elsewhere, with the
practice's sustainability entirely a function of whether population
density allows a long enough fallow-regrowth cycle before land is needed
again.

## Social consequence

Where population pressure shortens the fallow cycle below what local
forest regrowth can support, the same technique that supports a stable
steady-state farming population at low density instead drives continual
outward movement onto fresh, unexhausted land — converting swidden
cultivation from a sustainable local practice into the direct agronomic
engine behind farming-frontier population waves (MIG-020) and settler
expansion (MIG-090) wherever population growth outpaces the local
fallow-cycle ceiling.

## Regional specifics

Southeast Asian swidden traditions in several documented cases reach a
genuinely stable long-term equilibrium, with fallow cycles carefully
matched to local forest regrowth rates rather than functioning as a
one-way frontier-consuming process — the technique's sustainability is
a real, achievable outcome, not merely a euphemism for degradation.
Bantu-speaking farming populations combine swidden cultivation with
ironworking (AGR-160) and disease-ecology adaptation simultaneously while
crossing highly varied terrain, which plausibly explains the Bantu
expansion's markedly slower pace relative to the Fertile-Crescent-to-
Europe wave (MIG-020) — each new ecological zone required the whole
technological package to re-adapt together, not just the farming
population to relocate.

## Contested

Whether swidden cultivation is best modeled as an inherently sustainable
technique or an inherently frontier-consuming one is disputed, since the
same practice produces opposite long-run outcomes depending on fallow-
cycle length relative to local population density. Model `soil fertility`
recovery as a function of fallow-cycle length specifically, letting the
same technique support either a stable steady state or a continual
frontier-expansion driver depending on that one variable, rather than
assigning swidden cultivation a fixed sustainability verdict.

## Hook

Swidden cultivation's fallow-cycle-dependent `soil fertility` recovery
determines whether it functions as a stable steady-state farming system
or as the direct agronomic driver behind outward frontier expansion
(MIG-020, MIG-090) — the same technique, with population pressure alone
determining which of the two very different long-run outcomes a given
region actually gets.
