This entry owns the Columbian Exchange's agricultural side — the
transfer of American, European, and African crops across the Atlantic
and the resulting yield and land-use consequences on both sides — kept
separate from HLT-017's ownership of the same contact event's disease-
mortality mechanism.

## Timing

Beginning with Columbus's 1492 voyages, American crops (maize, potato,
cassava, tomato, among others) spread into the Old World over the
following two centuries, reaching significant cultivation scale in
Europe, Africa, and Asia by 1700; Old World crops and livestock spread
into the Americas on a parallel timeline, arriving alongside the
population collapse HLT-017 describes.

## Mechanism

Several American crops raise `yield ceiling` on land types Old World
staples handle poorly — the potato thrives on cool, wet, marginal land
unsuited to wheat; maize and cassava tolerate poorer soil and, in
cassava's case, drought better than many existing African and Asian
staples — meaning their adoption frequently expands total cultivable
area and population-carrying capacity rather than simply substituting
for an existing crop on the same land. On the American side, the same
contact event that transfers crops outward triggers population collapse
severe enough (HLT-017) that previously cultivated land is abandoned at
a scale large enough to show up as a documented land-use and vegetation-
cover shift.

## Social consequence

Where American crops raise yield ceiling on previously marginal land,
they support real population growth credited by historians as a
significant contributor to demographic expansion in parts of Africa,
China, and Europe over the following two centuries — a genuine
agricultural windfall running on a completely separate track from, and
arriving largely independent of, the same contact event's catastrophic
consequence for American populations themselves.

## Regional specifics

Maize and cassava spread across sub-Saharan Africa within a century of
contact, becoming major staples well ahead of formal European colonial
administration in most regions — an agricultural transfer that precedes
and is largely independent of the colonial period usually associated
with this era. American crops' spread into Qing China, especially onto
hill and marginal land unsuited to existing rice and wheat cultivation,
is credited by several historians as a meaningful contributor to the
substantial Qing-era population growth. The American side of the
exchange is instead defined by loss: population collapse severe enough
to abandon large areas of previously cultivated land, which reverts to
forest — a land-use collapse consequence distinct from, though sharing
its cause with, HLT-017's disease-mortality mechanism.

## Contested

Whether Americas-wide land abandonment was severe enough to function as
a measurable global atmospheric carbon sink, contributing to the
roughly contemporaneous Little Ice Age cooling (AGR-019), is an active,
disputed research question with varying estimates across studies. Model
the land-abandonment and reforestation event itself as well documented,
while treating its specific quantitative climate contribution as a
separate, more uncertain claim.

## Hook

American crops raise `yield ceiling` on marginal land across Africa,
China, and Europe largely independent of colonial administration, a
genuine agricultural windfall running on a wholly separate track from
the same contact event's catastrophic American land-use collapse
(sharing its cause with, but mechanically distinct from, HLT-017's
disease mortality) — gain and collapse from one event, on opposite
sides of the same ocean.
