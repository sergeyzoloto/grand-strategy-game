This entry owns the specific long-run agrarian-class-structure outcome
the Black Death's labor-scarcity shock (HLT-220) triggers — not the
immediate wage and habitus-rupture mechanism itself, which is HLT-220's,
but the durable institutional fork the same shock produces: serfdom's
decline in Western Europe against its intensification in the East.

## Timing

Following the Black Death's initial mortality shock (1347-1353, HLT-220),
Western European serfdom erodes progressively through the following two
centuries; Eastern Europe's "second serfdom" instead develops and
intensifies from around 1500 through the following century, on a
delayed and opposite trajectory from the same starting shock.

## Mechanism

The same labor-scarcity pressure (HLT-220) that raises Western European
peasants' bargaining position against a comparatively organized, custom-
protected village class structure instead lets Eastern European lords
re-impose and intensify hereditary labor obligation, because the
region's peasant class entered the shock with weaker village-level
organization and legal custom protecting existing tenancy terms — the
identical mortality-driven labor scarcity produces opposite institutional
outcomes depending entirely on which side's prior class-organizational
strength determines who actually captures the bargaining advantage labor
scarcity creates.

## Social consequence

Western Europe's serfdom decline converts agrarian labor progressively
toward wage tenancy and, eventually, enclosure-driven commercial farming
(AGR-200); Eastern Europe's second serfdom instead binds peasant labor
more tightly to large noble estates organized specifically around grain
production for export — the two regions' peasantries end up with
starkly different long-run social and economic positions despite having
experienced, within a comparable timeframe, the same originating
demographic catastrophe.

## Regional specifics

Eastern Europe's second serfdom is directly tied to a specific
commercial opportunity Western Europe's own transformation created: a
labor-scarce, wage-rising West increasingly able to pay for imported
grain gives Eastern noble estates a profitable export market their
intensified, re-bound peasant labor supplies — the two regions'
divergent outcomes aren't independent but directly linked, with Western
Europe's own labor-market transformation partly creating the market
conditions that make Eastern re-serfdomization commercially attractive
to its landowning class.

## Contested

The Brenner debate disputes why the identical mortality shock produced
opposite outcomes — Brenner's own argument centers prior peasant class-
organizational strength (stronger in the West, weaker in the East),
while other historians weight market-access differences (Western wage
economy versus Eastern grain-export opportunity) more heavily. Model the
divergence as branching on both prior peasant organizational strength
and available market alternatives together (design principle 1), rather
than crediting either factor alone as sufficient to explain the fork.

## Hook

Identical demographic shock (HLT-220), opposite institutional outcome:
Western Europe's stronger prior peasant organization converts labor
scarcity into serfdom's decline, while Eastern Europe's weaker prior
organization lets the same scarcity be answered with intensified,
export-oriented re-serfdomization — a clean design-principle-1 case
where the branch is set entirely by pre-shock institutional state, not
by the shock's magnitude.
