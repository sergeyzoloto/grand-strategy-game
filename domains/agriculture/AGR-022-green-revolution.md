This entry owns the Green Revolution's plant-breeding and land-tenure
side — the high-yield semi-dwarf wheat and rice varieties (Norman
Borlaug's breeding program foremost among them) and their uneven
distributional consequence across different landholding scales;
ENG-012 already owns the Haber-Bosch synthetic-fertilizer chemistry and
its own separate environmental-cost dispute in full.

## Timing

Norman Borlaug's semi-dwarf wheat breeding program begins in Mexico from
1943; the resulting varieties, alongside comparable high-yield rice
breeding (most prominently IR8, released in the Philippines in 1966),
spread rapidly across South and East Asian grain agriculture from the
mid-1960s, with the package's major yield and food-security consequences
substantially realized by 1980.

## Mechanism

Semi-dwarf wheat and rice varieties convert more of the nutrients a
plant absorbs into harvestable grain rather than into tall, easily-
wind-flattened stalks, which lets them respond to heavy synthetic
fertilizer application (ENG-012) with dramatically higher grain yield
than taller traditional varieties can safely absorb without collapsing —
the breeding innovation and the fertilizer input are jointly necessary,
since fertilizer alone on a traditional tall variety mostly produces
taller, more failure-prone stalks rather than more grain, and the
semi-dwarf trait alone without adequate fertilizer and water inputs
(AGR-005) doesn't reach anywhere near its full yield potential either.

## Social consequence

Because the full seed-fertilizer-irrigation package requires real
capital investment to access, its adoption and benefit concentrate
disproportionately among larger landholders and irrigated regions
already positioned to afford the inputs — converting a technology with
genuine aggregate food-security benefit into one that simultaneously
widens the yield and income gap between capital-advantaged and
capital-constrained farmers within the same country, a distributional
consequence separate from and additional to ENG-012's own already-
documented environmental and input-dependency costs.

## Regional specifics

India's adoption converts the country from food-aid dependency to
substantial self-sufficiency within roughly a decade, while adoption and
benefit concentrate heavily among larger and irrigated landholdings —
both outcomes real and simultaneously true. China's comparable grain-
yield gains run substantially through collective-era reorganization and
later household-responsibility decollectivization as much as through the
seed-and-fertilizer package itself, showing the breeding technology and
a society's land-tenure institutions combine as separable variables
rather than the technology alone determining the outcome.

## Contested

The Green Revolution's land-tenure consequence — that the seed-
fertilizer-irrigation package's capital requirements systematically
favored larger landholders and irrigated regions, widening rural
inequality even as aggregate national yield and food-security outcomes
improved — is documented, but its relative weight against the aggregate
food-security gain is debated, and is a distinct question from ENG-012's
separate contested dispute about the package's environmental and input-
dependency costs. Model the yield gain and the distributional/inequality
consequence as two separately-tracked effects (design principle 3's
authority/efficacy-style separation, applied here to aggregate gain
versus distributional consequence) rather than netting one against the
other into a single verdict.

## Hook

Semi-dwarf variety breeding and synthetic fertilizer (ENG-012) are
jointly necessary for the Green Revolution's yield gain, but the capital
cost of accessing the full package concentrates adoption and benefit
among larger, irrigated landholdings — model the aggregate `yield
ceiling` gain and its distributional/inequality consequence as two
separately-tracked effects rather than one combined verdict, since both
are real and point in different directions for different farmers within
the same country.
