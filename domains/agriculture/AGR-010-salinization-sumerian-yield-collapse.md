Sustained irrigation without adequate drainage slowly accumulates salt in
the topsoil, converting the same water-control technology that raises
`yield ceiling` (AGR-005) into a slow, compounding drain on `soil
fertility` if the salt it carries is never properly flushed back out.

## Timing

Southern Mesopotamian irrigation agriculture shows a documented shift
from wheat to more salt-tolerant barley cultivation, and declining
overall yield figures in surviving administrative records, across
roughly -2400 to -1700 — a multi-century process, not a sudden collapse.

## Mechanism

Irrigation water carries dissolved salts that concentrate in the topsoil
as the water evaporates or is taken up by crops, and without either a
naturally flushing flood cycle or active engineered drainage to carry
those accumulated salts back out of the field, salt concentration rises
with every irrigation cycle — a depleting-pool dynamic (design principle
4) where the same input (irrigation water) that sustains the crop also
steadily consumes the soil's own long-term productive capacity unless a
separate, ongoing drainage investment offsets it.

## Social consequence

Where salinization isn't actively managed, a region's farmers face a
slow-motion version of the yield-ceiling trade-off: continuing to
irrigate intensively sustains short-term output at the cost of steadily
rising long-term salt burden, while switching to more salt-tolerant but
lower-value crops (barley over wheat) trades some yield for slower
long-term soil degradation — a choice between two different rates of
decline rather than between decline and no decline, once salinization has
progressed far enough.

## Regional specifics

Egypt's Nile flood cycle naturally flushes accumulated field salts every
year as a byproduct of the same annual flooding that provides the
region's natural irrigation (AGR-005's Egyptian absence case), avoiding
Mesopotamia's salinization problem entirely without requiring comparable
active drainage investment — the same underlying salt-accumulation
mechanism, neutralized by favorable hydrology rather than engineered
effort, showing salinization risk is a consequence of specific
irrigation-system design and local hydrology, not an unavoidable cost of
irrigation as such.

## Contested

Whether salinization was the primary driver of Mesopotamia's documented
wheat-to-barley shift and yield decline, or whether political
fragmentation, warfare, and canal-maintenance breakdown contributed
comparably or more, is disputed, with the precise quantitative weight of
salinization specifically difficult to isolate from the same evidence.
Model salinization as a real, mechanistically well-understood drainage-
dependent risk, while treating its specific share of responsibility for
the Sumerian case, versus institutional and political causes, as
genuinely open.

## Hook

Unmanaged irrigation converts `yield ceiling` gains into a slowly
compounding `soil fertility` drain — a depleting-pool cost (design
principle 4) requiring ongoing drainage investment to offset, with a
region's only real choices, once salinization has progressed, being
which rate of decline to accept (continued intensive irrigation, or a
shift to lower-value salt-tolerant crops) rather than whether to decline
at all.
