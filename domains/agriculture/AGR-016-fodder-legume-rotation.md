Fodder crops (turnips, clover) grown in rotation with grain restore
nitrogen to the soil biologically — clover in particular fixes
atmospheric nitrogen through root-nodule bacteria — removing the need to
leave a third or half of a farm fallow and unproductive every cycle; the
same era's programmatic livestock selective breeding is a genuinely
separate mechanism sharing no chemical or causal relationship with this
one, and is AGR-027's territory rather than narrated here a second time.

## Timing

The Norfolk four-course rotation (wheat, turnips, barley, clover),
associated with Charles "Turnip" Townshend, spreads across English
farming from around 1730, diffusing and maturing across British
agriculture through the century's end.

## Mechanism

Clover's nitrogen-fixing root bacteria restore `soil fertility` that
continuous grain cultivation depletes, converting land that previously
had to lie fallow (unproductive) for part of every rotation cycle into
land that grows a useful fodder crop instead while still restoring the
soil — a genuine yield gain with no fallow-year tradeoff required,
distinct from and predating ENG-012's much later synthetic-nitrogen
solution to the same underlying nitrogen-limitation problem, and a
land-intensive alternative to AGR-023's earlier, labor-intensive manure-
and-night-soil route to the identical outcome.

## Social consequence

Removing the fallow-year yield loss supports higher effective
agricultural output per farm without requiring additional land or the
sustained collection labor AGR-023's manure-cycling route depends on,
contributing directly to the population growth and urban labor-supply
expansion that feeds the roughly contemporaneous early Industrial
Revolution (ENG-007, COM-023) — an agricultural productivity gain whose
downstream consequence runs well outside agriculture itself.

## Regional specifics

Chinese agriculture had sustained intensive crop rotation and organic
soil-fertility management (AGR-023) for centuries before this era's
British innovations, at a level of sophistication Western agronomists
visiting the period frequently noted with admiration — the "British
Agricultural Revolution" framing risks crediting Britain with inventing
a nitrogen-management sophistication that already existed elsewhere,
reached through a different labor-intensive rather than land-rotation-
intensive institutional path.

## Contested

Whether the traditionally-cited British rotation innovation represents a
sharp, datable productivity break or a slower continuous intensification
with earlier roots, retrospectively packaged into one revolutionary
narrative, is disputed among agricultural historians. Model the yield
gain as real and datable to this era, while treating the "sharp break
versus gradual continuity" framing question as open.

## Hook

Legume rotation restores `soil fertility` without a fallow-year yield
loss or AGR-023's ongoing collection-labor cost, the last major land-
intensive pre-industrial solution to agriculture's ancient nitrogen
ceiling before ENG-012's synthetic-nitrogen breakthrough removes the
ceiling altogether — a land-for-labor trade-off against AGR-023's
manure-cycling alternative, not a superior replacement for it.
