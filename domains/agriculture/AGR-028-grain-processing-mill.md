This entry owns grain processing's agricultural and labor consequence —
which population bears the cost of converting harvested grain into usable
flour, and what changes when that cost drops sharply; the mill's own
construction technique belongs to ENG's territory once an entry for it
exists there, which as of this record it does not, making this entry's
own scope necessarily broader than AGR-005's cleaner irrigation/ENG-001
split until that gap closes.

## Timing

Hand-operated grinding stones (saddle querns, later rotary querns) are in
use wherever grain agriculture exists from close to its earliest spread
following AGR-001; water-powered milling spreads across the Middle East,
East Asia, and Europe over the following millennia, reaching the status
of ordinary rural infrastructure — not a rare technology — by the time
England's Domesday survey counts roughly 6,000 watermills in 1086.

## Mechanism

Converting harvested grain into usable flour by hand-operated quern is
extraordinarily labor-intensive, requiring hours of grinding per
household per day and falling, across the great majority of documented
societies practicing it, overwhelmingly on women's labor specifically —
a processing-stage bottleneck distinct from AGR-007's field-preparation-
stage labor question, but a comparable case of a specific agricultural
task's labor burden concentrating on one gender by default rather than
by even distribution. Water- and wind-powered milling removes that
bottleneck by substituting mechanical power for human grinding labor,
freeing the hours previously spent at the quern for other work.

## Social consequence

Where milling mechanizes, the freed household labor — again, historically
falling disproportionately on women — becomes available for other
productive or domestic activity, while control over the mill itself
(often a lord's or monastery's asset, with tenants required to use it
for a fee) becomes a new, separately-controllable economic and social
lever over the surrounding agricultural population, distinct from land
tenure itself.

## Regional specifics

Domesday's roughly 6,000 English watermills by 1086 shows the technology
had already become ordinary, geographically dense rural infrastructure
rather than a rare or elite installation, well before this entry's
window closes. The pre-contact Americas' continued reliance on
hand-grinding throughout, with no water- or wind-powered milling ever
developing, is consistent with the broader absence of constructed-water-
power and draft-animal traditions documented elsewhere in this record —
leaving grain processing there a purely hand-labor-gated bottleneck the
whole period this entry covers elsewhere sees mechanized away.

## Hook

Mechanized milling converts a heavy, historically gendered hand-labor
burden into a much smaller one, freeing that labor for other work — a
modifier on effective household labor supply distinct from AGR-007's
field-preparation-stage gendered-labor mechanic, with control over the
mill itself becoming a separate economic lever over the surrounding
agricultural population once the technology concentrates into a single,
locally-controllable installation.
