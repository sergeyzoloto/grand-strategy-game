The ard (a simple, non-inverting scratch plow, pulled by a domesticated
draft animal rather than pushed by human labor alone) converts field
preparation from a task any able-bodied adult can perform to one
requiring both a trained draft animal and, per the Boserup thesis, a
different and more male-concentrated labor profile than hoe cultivation.

## Timing

The ard develops in the Fertile Crescent and spreads outward from
roughly -4000, reaching most of Eurasian dry-farming agriculture within
the following two millennia; the traction shift depends directly on
AGR-002's secondary-products reorganization of livestock keeping around
sustained working use rather than slaughter alone.

## Mechanism

An ard-drawn furrow prepares far more field area per unit of human labor
than hand-hoeing the same area, but requires sustained control of a
harnessed draft animal across a full plowing session — a task profile
Boserup argues correlates cross-culturally with a shift toward more
male-concentrated field labor, since plowing is both more physically
demanding in upper-body strength terms and harder to interrupt for
simultaneous childcare than hoe cultivation's more flexible, interruptible
task structure.

## Social consequence

Where the Boserup shift takes hold, field agriculture's labor-value
credit concentrates more heavily on men specifically, with real
downstream consequence for property, inheritance, and status
arrangements built on top of who is understood to produce a
household's primary agricultural surplus — a technological shift in
field-preparation method translating into a durable habitus-level
division of labor (design principle 2) rather than remaining a narrow
question of who happens to hold the plow handle on a given day.

## Regional specifics

Chinese traction agriculture reaches a broadly comparable draft-cattle
plow adoption on a similar timeline to the Fertile Crescent, later
adding water-buffalo paddy traction as a distinct regional application.
Sub-Saharan Africa's persistence of hoe-based cultivation, constrained
by the same tsetse-belt draft-animal ceiling noted in AGR-002, sustains
a structurally different and, in many documented societies, more
female-central agricultural labor pattern — not a delayed transition
toward the plow model, but a stable alternative resting on a different
technological base entirely.

## Contested

Boserup's thesis linking plow adoption to a more male-concentrated field-
labor division is supported by cross-cultural correlation but disputed
on causal mechanism and on how much later social/legal structure
(inheritance norms, citizenship's gendered scope, LAW-002) actually
traces back to this specific technological shift versus other
contributing social factors. Model plow adoption as correlated with and
a plausible contributor to more male-concentrated field labor, while
treating the causal strength of that link as itself contested.

## Hook

Ard/plow adoption plausibly shifts field-labor's habitus-level gender
division toward men relative to hoe cultivation's more flexible task
structure (Boserup's contested thesis) — a technological choice gated
on draft-animal availability (AGR-002) whose downstream social
consequence, where it occurs, runs through the same habitus-forming
mechanism (design principle 2) as any other durable practice-driven
disposition shift in this record.
