Three distinct technologies — the heavy mouldboard plow (which turns and
inverts heavy clay soil rather than merely scratching it, unlike the ard,
AGR-007), the padded horse collar (which lets a horse pull without
choking, unlocking horse traction's superior speed over oxen for field
work), and three-field crop rotation (which fallows only a third of land
each year instead of half, per two-field rotation) — are traditionally
bundled together as one package credited with a substantial rise in
northern European agricultural yield.

## Timing

The heavy mouldboard plow and three-field rotation both spread across
northern Europe over an extended period from roughly 600 through 1200,
with the padded horse collar's wider adoption following a broadly
similar, though independently debated, timeline within the same window.

## Mechanism

Northern Europe's heavier, wetter clay soils resist the ard's light
scratching action and drain poorly without being properly turned — the
mouldboard plow's soil-inverting action specifically solves this local
problem, at the cost of requiring far more draft power to pull than an
ard does, which is where the horse collar's efficiency gain becomes
relevant (a properly-harnessed horse plows faster than an ox team,
letting a farm cover more ground per day once both plow and collar are
in place). Three-field rotation independently raises the average
`yield ceiling` by keeping two-thirds rather than half of a farm's land
under cultivation each year, at the cost of somewhat more complex crop
and fallow-cycle planning.

## Social consequence

Where the full package takes hold, a farming household can work more
land, more effectively, per unit of labor than the ard-and-two-field
system it replaces — a real per-farm productivity gain contributing to
the population growth and expanding cultivated area northern Europe
documents across this period, alongside the region's own frontier
expansion (AGR-008) into newly viable heavy-soil and forested land
(AGR-009).

## Regional specifics

Mediterranean and Middle Eastern dry farming's continued reliance on the
lighter ard rather than the heavy mouldboard plow reflects genuine local
soil and climate fit rather than technological lag — the heavier plow's
soil-inverting advantage matters far less, and its higher draft-power
cost matters more, on the thinner, drier soils dominant across most of
that region, showing the north European package's advantage is
specifically soil-and-climate-conditional rather than a universal
upgrade any farming region would benefit from adopting.

## Contested

Lynn White Jr.'s influential bundling of these three technologies into
one unified "medieval agricultural revolution" package directly causing
northern Europe's population growth has been substantially revised by
later medievalists, who argue the technologies diffused on more
independent timelines and that population growth was multiply-determined
(climate, reduced disruption, expanding cultivated area) rather than
explained by one clean technological package. Model the three
technologies as mutually reinforcing where they co-occur, while treating
their tight bundling into a single sufficient causal package as contested
rather than settled.

## Hook

The mouldboard plow, horse collar, and three-field rotation each raise
`yield ceiling` through a distinct, soil-and-climate-conditional
mechanism rather than one unified universal upgrade — northern Europe's
heavy clay soils reward all three where the Mediterranean's lighter,
drier soils reward none of them, making the "package's" actual adoption
and payoff a function of local soil fit, not just of technological
availability or contact.
