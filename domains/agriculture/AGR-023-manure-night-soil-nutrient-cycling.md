Before any land-rotation solution to agriculture's nitrogen ceiling
exists, returning an already-available waste stream — animal manure,
human night-soil, crop residue — to the field it came from restores
`soil fertility` directly, at the cost of the sustained collection and
transport labor that stream requires rather than at the cost of land
lying fallow.

## Timing

Manuring practice is attested wherever settled agriculture and
domesticated animals coexist, from close to the earliest sedentary
farming communities around -6000 onward; systematic urban night-soil
collection in China reaches its fullest documented, institutionalized
form from around 1000 and continues as standard practice for centuries
afterward.

## Mechanism

A waste stream that would otherwise simply accumulate or wash away
carries the same nitrogen, phosphorus, and organic matter a crop draws
down from soil every growing season — returning it converts an ordinary
disposal problem into a fertility-restoration input, but only in
proportion to the labor spent collecting, transporting, and applying it,
which is why the technique's actual intensity varies enormously with
local labor availability relative to land: abundant labor and scarce land
(much of premodern East Asia) rewards intensive, continuous collection
and application, while abundant land and scarce labor rewards leaving
land fallow instead (AGR-016's later, land-intensive alternative to the
identical underlying problem).

## Social consequence

Sustained night-soil collection requires an ongoing, unglamorous, often
low-status labor system connecting a settlement's waste output directly
back to its surrounding farmland — a genuine circular economy, but one
resting on continuous human labor that a land-rotation-based system
doesn't require, converting soil-fertility maintenance into a visible,
daily social division of labor rather than a background feature of the
farming calendar.

## Regional specifics

China's night-soil system reaches a scale and institutional
consistency, sustained across many centuries, that Western agronomists
visiting the region in the era covered by AGR-016 specifically singled
out for admiration — a labor-intensive nutrient-cycling sophistication
existing well before and independent of the much later British
rotation-based solution to the same nitrogen problem. South Asian dung's
persistent competition between fuel and fertilizer use shows the same
waste-stream resource can face a genuine allocation trade-off unrelated
to farming technique itself. The pre-contact Americas' thin domesticate
roster (AGR-002, AGR-003) caps manure-based cycling at the source in a
way no amount of labor availability alone can overcome.

## Contested

How much of sustained high pre-industrial yield in labor-abundant East
Asian agriculture specifically should be credited to manure/night-soil
nutrient cycling alone, versus other contributing factors (multi-
cropping, irrigation intensity, crop selection), is debated among
agricultural historians, given the difficulty of isolating one input's
contribution from a tightly integrated intensive farming system using
surviving records. Model labor-intensive nutrient cycling and land-
intensive fallow/rotation (AGR-016) as two independently viable routes
to comparable soil-fertility outcomes, rather than crediting either as
the more fundamentally correct solution to agriculture's nitrogen-
limitation problem.

## Hook

Manure and night-soil cycling restores `soil fertility` through a
labor-gated rather than land-gated path — a region with abundant labor
and scarce land, and a region with abundant land and scarce labor, reach
comparable soil-fertility outcomes through opposite input trade-offs,
with the specific technique a farming system adopts (AGR-016's later
rotation-based alternative among them) determined by which resource is
locally scarcer, not by which technique is objectively superior.
