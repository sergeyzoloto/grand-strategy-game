A sustained, multi-decade cooling period lowers `yield ceiling` and
raises harvest-failure frequency across large parts of Eurasia
simultaneously, and the resulting recurring agrarian stress is
implicated — with real historiographical dispute over how much weight
to give it — in a roughly synchronized cluster of political crises
across otherwise unconnected societies.

## Timing

Temperature reconstructions place the Little Ice Age's coldest and most
agriculturally disruptive phase across roughly 1560 to 1720, with
harvest-failure clusters and associated political crises concentrated
particularly in the early-to-mid 1600s across multiple Eurasian regions.

## Mechanism

Sustained lower average temperatures shorten growing seasons and
increase the frequency of crop-failing frost and drought events,
directly lowering `yield ceiling` across affected latitudes regardless
of prevailing agricultural technology — a climatic input existing
irrigation (AGR-100), rotation (AGR-180, AGR-240), and traction (AGR-130)
technology can partially buffer but not fully offset, since all of them
operate on a growing-season length and temperature range the cooling
itself compresses.

## Social consequence

Recurring harvest failure raises grain prices, strains state fiscal
capacity (tax revenue falling as agricultural output falls, precisely
when relief spending needs rise), and increases both peasant unrest and
elite competition over a shrinking surplus — a stress pattern that,
this era's historiography argues, contributes to political
crises ranging from open revolt to full dynastic collapse across
multiple, otherwise unconnected societies experiencing the same
underlying climatic pressure within a similar window.

## Regional specifics

Northern China's severe 1630s-40s drought cluster contributes directly
to the fiscal and social breakdown accompanying the Ming dynasty's 1644
collapse, a case of the same climate-linked stress pattern operating
independently on the opposite side of Eurasia from Europe's
contemporaneous crises. Ottoman Celali revolts intensify through the
same early-1600s window alongside documented harvest instability and
fiscal strain, part of the same broadly synchronized pattern. Sub-
Saharan Africa's far less consistently documented cooling signal in
this period plausibly reflects genuine climatic asymmetry (tropical
agriculture is less exposed to temperate-zone cooling) as much as a
thinner surviving record for the region and era.

## Contested

The "General Crisis" thesis linking Little Ice Age cooling to a
synchronized cluster of mid-17th-century political crises across
Eurasia is influential but disputed, with historians differing on how
much weight a shared climatic trigger deserves against region-specific
political, fiscal, and dynastic causes that plausibly would have
produced crises regardless. Model elevated harvest-failure frequency as
a real, trackable climatic input across the affected regions, while
treating its sufficiency to explain each specific regional political
crisis as contested rather than settled.

## Hook

Little Ice Age cooling lowers `yield ceiling` and raises harvest-
failure frequency across a wide, otherwise unconnected swath of Eurasia
simultaneously — existing agricultural technology (irrigation, rotation,
traction) buffers but can't fully offset the climatic compression,
leaving the resulting fiscal and social strain as a real, shared
contributing input to political crisis whose sufficiency, against
region-specific causes, stays a genuinely open branch rather than a
fixed multiplier.
