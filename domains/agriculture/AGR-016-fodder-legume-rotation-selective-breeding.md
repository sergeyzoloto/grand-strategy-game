Fodder crops (turnips, clover) grown in rotation with grain restore
nitrogen to the soil biologically — clover in particular fixes
atmospheric nitrogen through root-nodule bacteria — removing the need to
leave a third or half of a farm fallow and unproductive every cycle,
while systematic livestock selective breeding converts animal
improvement from incidental variation into a deliberate, repeatable
breeding program.

## Timing

The Norfolk four-course rotation (wheat, turnips, barley, clover),
associated with Charles "Turnip" Townshend, spreads across English
farming from around 1730; Robert Bakewell's systematic selective
livestock breeding program develops from the 1760s; both practices
diffuse and mature across British agriculture through the century's end.

## Mechanism

Clover's nitrogen-fixing root bacteria restore `soil fertility` that
continuous grain cultivation depletes, converting land that previously
had to lie fallow (unproductive) for part of every rotation cycle into
land that grows a useful fodder crop instead while still restoring the
soil — a genuine yield gain with no fallow-year tradeoff required,
distinct from and predating ENG-012's much later synthetic-nitrogen
solution to the same underlying nitrogen-limitation problem. Bakewell's
selective breeding applies the same deliberate-selection logic that
originally produced domesticated species (AGR-001) to systematic,
programmatic improvement of an already-domesticated population,
converting livestock improvement from passive accumulated variation into
an actively managed breeding program with measurable, repeatable
results.

## Social consequence

Removing the fallow-year yield loss and increasing livestock
productivity together support higher effective agricultural output per
farm without requiring additional land, contributing directly to the
population growth and urban labor-supply expansion that feeds the
roughly contemporaneous early Industrial Revolution (ENG-007,
COM-023) — an agricultural productivity gain whose downstream
consequence runs well outside agriculture itself.

## Regional specifics

Chinese agriculture had sustained intensive crop rotation and organic
soil-fertility management for centuries before this era's British
innovations, at a level of sophistication Western agronomists visiting
the period frequently noted with admiration — the "British Agricultural
Revolution" framing risks crediting Britain with inventing a nitrogen-
management sophistication that already existed elsewhere, reached
through a different labor-intensive rather than land-rotation-intensive
institutional path.

## Contested

Whether the traditionally-cited British innovations represent a sharp,
datable productivity break or a slower continuous intensification with
earlier roots, retrospectively packaged into one revolutionary narrative,
is disputed among agricultural historians. Model the yield and
livestock-productivity gains as real and datable to this era, while
treating the "sharp break versus gradual continuity" framing question as
open.

## Hook

Legume rotation restores `soil fertility` without a fallow-year yield
loss, the last major pre-industrial solution to agriculture's ancient
nitrogen ceiling before ENG-012's synthetic-nitrogen breakthrough removes
the ceiling altogether — paired with Bakewell's programmatic livestock
breeding, both applying deliberate, repeatable selection logic
(AGR-001's original mechanism) to already-established domesticates rather
than to new ones.
