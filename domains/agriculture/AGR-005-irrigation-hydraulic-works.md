This entry owns irrigation's agronomic and social consequence — the yield
increase, and the water-rights control it concentrates in whoever
administers the system; ENG-001 owns the construction technique itself
(channels, levees, reservoirs, dams).

## Timing

Following small-scale Mesopotamian canal irrigation from roughly -6000
(ENG-001), irrigated agriculture's yield and social consequences mature
alongside state-scale hydraulic works across multiple independent
traditions through the first millennium BCE.

## Mechanism

Reliable irrigation raises a plot's `yield ceiling` well above what
rain-fed farming on the same land could sustain, converting agricultural
output from a function of unpredictable rainfall into a function of
engineered water access — but because that access runs through a shared
canal or reservoir network no individual farmer controls alone, irrigated
land's productivity becomes conditional on continued access to a
resource administered by whoever runs the network, a lever of control a
rain-fed farmer's landlord or state simply doesn't have available.

## Social consequence

Water-rights administration becomes a distinct, separately-controllable
resource from land ownership itself — a farmer can hold clear title to
irrigated land and still be entirely dependent on an external
authority's water allocation decision for that land's actual
productivity, a dependency temple (REL-001) and state fiscal (FIN-001)
institutions historically use as direct administrative leverage over an
otherwise autonomous landholding population.

## Regional specifics

Mesopotamian irrigated agriculture's yield advantage over rain-fed
farming is large enough to make water-rights control a first-order
political resource in its own right, distinct from land tenure. Egypt's
Nile flood cycle achieves a comparable irrigated-agriculture yield
through favorable natural hydrology rather than comparable constructed
canal infrastructure, showing the yield benefit and the constructed-
infrastructure-dependent control lever are separable: a region can get
one without needing to build the other.

## Contested

Wittfogel's "hydraulic despotism" thesis — that irrigation dependency at
scale causally produces centralized despotic state control — is
influential but disputed, with documented counter-cases of irrigation
managed by decentralized communal institutions rather than centralized
state bureaucracy. Model irrigation-driven water-rights concentration as
a real, trackable control lever, but let whether that control
centralizes at state or communal level depend on prior institutional
context (design principle 1) rather than following automatically from
irrigation scale.

## Hook

Irrigation raises a plot's `yield ceiling` past rain-fed farming's
limit, but makes that yield conditional on continued access to a
shared, externally-administered water network — concentrating a real
control lever in whoever runs that network, with the resulting authority
centralizing at state or communal level depending on what institutions
were already in place before the irrigation system was built, not on
irrigation scale alone.
