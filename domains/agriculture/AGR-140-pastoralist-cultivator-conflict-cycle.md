This entry owns the recurring, structural land-use competition between
mobile pastoralism and fixed-field cultivation as two agricultural modes
with genuinely incompatible spatial needs — WAR-080 owns cavalry as a
battlefield doctrine and MIG-110 owns steppe political-military
confederation; this entry owns the everyday, non-military land-use
conflict the two modes generate wherever they share an ecological zone,
independent of whether either side ever mobilizes militarily at all.

## Timing

The conflict recurs wherever pastoralist and cultivator populations
share a border zone from as early as organized pastoralism and settled
agriculture both exist in the same region (from roughly -3000 in parts
of the Middle East), continuing as an active pattern in several regions
into the present.

## Mechanism

Pastoralism requires extensive grazing land and, for many herding
systems, seasonal transhumance routes moving herds between pastures —
land uses that are spatially extensive and mobile by design. Cultivation
requires fixed, bounded fields under continuous, exclusive use for an
entire growing season — a spatial claim mobile grazing routes
structurally conflict with wherever the two overlap, since a herd
crossing or grazing on standing crops directly destroys a cultivator's
season, while fenced or claimed cropland blocks a pastoralist's
established route or seasonal pasture access. Neither side's land use is
irrational; the conflict is structural to the two modes sharing the same
land rather than a failure of either party's judgment.

## Social consequence

Where population growth on either side compresses the available land —
cultivators expanding fields into former pasture, or pastoralist herds
growing faster than available grazing range — the conflict intensifies
predictably, converting what can be a stable, even mutually beneficial
coexistence (pastoralist herds grazing post-harvest stubble, providing
manure that restores cultivated `soil fertility`, cultivators supplying
grain in seasonal exchange) into active competition and, recurrently
across history, violence once the land-use overlap can no longer be
locally negotiated.

## Regional specifics

West African Fulani pastoralist-farmer conflict persists as an active
pattern into the present, intensified by contemporary population growth
and rangeland compression rather than being a resolved historical
phenomenon. China's substantial and sustained state investment in
frontier fortification specifically to separate steppe pastoralist
territory from the agrarian core shows the conflict cycle can be
suppressed, though not eliminated, through state-level infrastructure
investment — a genuinely different resolution strategy from the ongoing
local land-use negotiation the cycle runs on absent that investment.

## Hook

Pastoralist grazing routes and cultivator fixed fields make structurally
incompatible spatial claims on the same land, with the balance between
productive coexistence (stubble-grazing exchange restoring `soil
fertility`) and open conflict tracking available land relative to
population pressure on either side — a cycle state-level frontier
infrastructure investment (the Chinese case) can suppress but not
eliminate, and which recurs independent of whether either side's
political organization ever escalates to the military scale WAR-080 and
MIG-110 describe.
