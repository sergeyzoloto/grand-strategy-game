Enclosure converts land held under open-field, common, or customary-use
arrangements — where multiple households hold traditional but often
legally unwritten rights to graze, glean, or cultivate strips of a
shared field system — into consolidated, privately-fenced, individually-
titled holdings, a legal and physical land-tenure transformation
distinct from LAW-005's feudal service-bond question of who owes what to
whom on the land.

## Timing

English enclosure proceeds in two broad waves — an earlier, more
piecemeal phase from around 1450 driven substantially by landlords
converting arable to more profitable sheep pasture, and a later,
more systematic and legally formalized wave of Parliamentary enclosure
acts through the 18th and early 19th centuries, substantially complete
by 1850.

## Mechanism

Consolidating scattered open-field strips and formerly common land into
single, fenced, individually-managed holdings lets a landholder apply
crop rotation (AGR-013, AGR-016), selective breeding, and other yield-
raising techniques at a scale and with a degree of centralized control
open-field systems' distributed, custom-bound management structure
doesn't easily permit — raising commercial `yield ceiling` at the direct
cost of extinguishing the traditional grazing, gleaning, and
subsistence-use rights a substantial share of the rural population
depended on without holding formal legal title to.

## Social consequence

Populations dispossessed of customary land-use rights through enclosure
face a stark choice between remaining as wage laborers on the newly
consolidated commercial farms (at a lower and less secure standard of
living than customary-use rights had provided) or migrating toward
urban wage labor and, in many documented cases, toward the frontier and
overseas settlement (AGR-008, MIG-005) — enclosure functioning as a
direct, traceable push factor behind several of this record's later
migration waves, not merely a domestic agricultural policy question.

## Regional specifics

Qing China's land-tenure structure moves in the opposite direction
across the same broad era — partible inheritance fragments rather than
consolidates holdings, sustaining a predominantly smallholder agrarian
class structure rather than developing toward large commercial estates
the way English enclosure does, a durable structural difference from the
same period's Western European transformation. Communal and lineage-
based land tenure across much of precolonial sub-Saharan Africa closes
off the specific enclosure mechanism from the outset, since land rights
vested in a kin-group can't be individually fenced and privatized the
way an English commoner's traditional-use rights could be legally
extinguished.

## Contested

Whether enclosure's net effect on the dispossessed rural population was
primarily positive (freeing labor for more productive commercial and
later industrial use) or primarily negative (destroying a customary
subsistence safety net with inadequate compensation) is a long-running
historiographical dispute. Model enclosure as raising commercial `yield
ceiling` while separately displacing customary-use rights, with the net
welfare sign of that trade left genuinely contested rather than resolved
in either direction.

## Hook

Enclosure raises commercial `yield ceiling` by concentrating land under
centralized management, at the direct cost of extinguishing customary
subsistence-use rights the dispossessed held no formal title to — a
land-tenure transformation that functions as a real, traceable push
factor behind wage-labor migration and frontier settlement (AGR-008,
MIG-005) wherever it occurs, with its net welfare effect on the
displaced population left as a genuinely open, contested variable.
