Flooded paddy cultivation intensifies rice yield well beyond dry-field
rice farming's ceiling by itself, and the introduction of a fast-
ripening, drought-tolerant rice variety (Champa rice) into that already-
intensified system unlocks double-cropping — two harvests from the same
paddy in a single year — raising the region's effective `yield ceiling`
a second time on top of paddy cultivation's own earlier gain.

## Timing

Wet-rice paddy cultivation in the Yangzi valley intensifies from early
rice domestication (AGR-001) onward; Champa rice, developed in what is
now central Vietnam considerably earlier, is formally introduced to Song
China in 1011-1012 specifically because its short growing season and
drought tolerance let a second crop be planted and harvested within the
same year the first crop already occupied the paddy.

## Mechanism

Standing water in a paddy field suppresses weeds, moderates soil
temperature, and delivers dissolved nutrients continuously in a way dry-
field cultivation can't match, which is what lets wet-rice paddies
sustain higher per-hectare yield than dry cereal farming in the first
place — Champa rice's specific contribution on top of that is timing,
not yield-per-crop: its shorter growing season and drought tolerance
(it needs less standing water and less time to mature than prior rice
varieties) let a paddy produce two harvests in the same growing season
where only one fit before, multiplying effective annual yield without
requiring any additional land.

## Social consequence

Double-cropping's yield multiplication directly supports the
substantial Song-dynasty population growth and southward economic
center-of-gravity shift documented in this era — a paddy region that
could support one harvest's worth of population now supports
meaningfully more from the same land, land-tenure and tax-assessment
systems (FIN-001) built around a single-harvest assumption having to
adjust to a genuinely higher sustainable population density than before.

## Regional specifics

Champa rice's own origin lies in what is now central Vietnam, where its
drought-tolerant and fast-ripening traits were selected locally well
before the variety's Song-dynasty introduction to China — the
intensification credited to Chinese agricultural history in this era
rests directly on a Southeast Asian agronomic innovation, transmitted
specifically through tributary diplomatic exchange rather than
independently developed within China. Northern Chinese dry farming never
adopts wet-rice cultivation at all, keeping the Champa-rice-driven
intensification geographically confined to the Yangzi valley and points
south rather than becoming a nationwide Chinese phenomenon.

## Hook

Champa rice's short growing season converts an already-intensified
wet-rice paddy system's single annual harvest into two, multiplying
effective `yield ceiling` without requiring additional land — a
population-supporting gain whose origin traces to a Southeast Asian
variety selected outside China entirely, arriving as a diplomatic
tributary transfer rather than a domestic Chinese innovation.
