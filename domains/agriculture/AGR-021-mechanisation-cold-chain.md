This entry owns farm mechanization specifically — the reaper, thresher,
and eventually the tractor and combine harvester replacing human and
draft-animal labor (AGR-007) in field preparation and harvest; COM-025
already owns the cold chain's refrigerated-transport side of this era's
agricultural transformation in full, referenced here rather than
re-derived.

## Timing

Mechanical reapers and threshers spread across American and European
grain farming from around 1830; the gasoline tractor progressively
displaces draft-animal traction from the early 20th century, with
mechanization reaching its fastest and most consequential adoption pace
across American and European agriculture between 1930 and 1960.

## Mechanism

Each mechanization step removes a specific labor bottleneck — the reaper
and thresher speed up harvest processing that previously required large
seasonal labor crews, while the tractor replaces draft-animal traction
(AGR-007) with an engine that doesn't need pasture land, doesn't tire at
the same rate, and can pull heavier implements — the cumulative effect
across all these steps is a sharp fall in the labor required per unit of
cultivated land, converting farm labor from the binding constraint on
how much land a household could work into a comparatively minor one.

## Social consequence

As mechanization cuts labor-per-hectare requirements sharply, a given
rural population becomes able to farm the same or greater total land
area with dramatically fewer people — the direct agricultural-side
driver of the sustained rural-to-urban migration this record's
industrial-era migration entries describe, converting what used to be
essential farm labor into a surplus rural population pushed toward urban
wage employment.

## Regional specifics

American farm mechanization proceeds fast enough between 1930 and 1960
to be a direct, traceable contributor to the era's substantial rural
exodus. South Asian mechanization lags well behind the American and
European pace through the same era, animal traction remaining dominant
on most smallholdings — a gap rooted in capital availability and average
farm size (mechanization economics favoring large consolidated holdings
over small fragmented ones, AGR-020) rather than in unavailability of the
technology itself.

## Hook

Each mechanization step cuts the labor required per unit of cultivated
land, converting farm labor from a binding constraint into a shrinking
one and directly feeding rural-to-urban migration wherever adoption
proceeds fast enough — a transformation that pairs with, but runs on an
entirely separate infrastructure track from, COM-025's cold-chain
extension of perishable goods' viable trade range.
