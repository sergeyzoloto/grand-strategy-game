AGR-004 names an unresolved question and leaves it open: why do only
some Holocene populations, once climate stability removes the hard prior
constraint on agricultural investment, actually take up domestication
and its later intensification while others don't. This entry owns the
leading candidate answer economists and historical demographers actually
propose: population pressure itself.

## Timing

The mechanism this entry describes recurs wherever population density
rises against a fixed land base, from the earliest post-domestication
population growth (following AGR-001, from roughly -7000) through the
early modern era; Ester Boserup's own theoretical articulation of the
mechanism dates to 1965, considerably later than the phenomenon itself,
in the same way this record treats other cases where a theorist names a
pattern long after it first recurs historically.

## Mechanism

The domain's entries otherwise default to treating yield-raising
innovation as the independent variable and population growth as its
downstream consequence — a founder-crop package (AGR-001) or an
irrigation system (AGR-005) raises `yield ceiling`, and population grows
to fill the new capacity. Boserup's thesis inverts this: population
growth, once it outpaces what existing extensive, low-labor techniques
can support, itself forces a society to adopt more labor-intensive
methods — shorter fallow cycles, denser planting, more thorough weeding
and manuring (AGR-023) — that no population operating below that
pressure threshold would have any reason to bear the additional labor
cost for, since the extra effort only pays off once land, not labor,
is the binding constraint.

## Social consequence

Where the Boserupian mechanism fires, a society's shift toward more
labor-intensive agriculture isn't experienced as a chosen improvement
but as an imposed necessity — working harder for a comparable or only
marginally better standard of living per capita, since the intensification
converts population pressure into higher total output without
necessarily raising output per worker by a comparable margin, a real and
often-overlooked welfare cost embedded in the intensification story
AGR-013's and AGR-016's own yield-focused framings don't foreground.

## Regional specifics

Chinese and South Asian agriculture's historically very high labor input
per unit of land, reaching land-productivity levels far beyond what
comparably land-abundant regions ever pursue, form the core evidentiary
case for the thesis — exactly the outcome Boserup's model predicts where
population density is high and additional land isn't available. Sub-
Saharan Africa's continued reliance on extensive swidden cultivation
(AGR-008) through most of this era is, on this same reading, not an
unexplained lag behind the intensive-agriculture cases but the correctly
predicted outcome of comparatively lower population density relative to
available land — the thesis explains the absence as directly as it
explains the presence.

## Contested

Whether population growth follows agricultural intensification
(Malthus) or forces it (Boserup) is a genuinely live, unresolved debate
in agricultural economics and historical demography. Model both causal
directions as available, with the specific historical case — population
growth already outpacing yield, or a yield-raising innovation arriving
before population pressure existed — determining which one actually
fired in a given instance, rather than assuming a single universal
causal arrow applies everywhere in this record.

## Hook

Population pressure and yield-raising innovation can run in either
causal direction — a branch, not a fixed sequence — with AGR-008's
swidden fallow-cycle compression already implicitly running on the
Boserupian reading (population pressure forces shorter fallow) without
this record previously naming the general mechanism that explains why,
or under what conditions the reverse (Malthusian) direction applies
instead.
