This entry owns computation as a theoretical and scientific object —
algorithms and computability — kept distinct from the physical hardware
(ENG-330's semiconductors) that eventually executes it, and from the
separate mathematical question of quantifying information itself
(NAT-240).

## Timing

Al-Khwarizmi's algorithmic treatise dates to around 820; the theoretical
foundation for what a computer can and cannot compute in principle is
established by Turing's 1936 work on computability, completing the
conceptual lineage from a rigorously defined procedure to a precise
statement of that procedure's own limits.

## Mechanism

An algorithm is a precisely specified, step-by-step procedure guaranteed
to reach a result in a finite number of steps — a concept that requires no
machine at all to exist and matter, since it's fundamentally about what
counts as a valid, mechanically-followable procedure rather than about any
particular device executing one. Turing's theoretical contribution goes
further, precisely defining what any possible computing device could ever
compute in principle, establishing hard limits (undecidable problems) that
apply regardless of how sophisticated the physical hardware eventually
becomes.

## Social consequence

Separating the theory of computation from any particular physical
implementation is what lets computer science develop as a field with its
own internal logic and open problems well before, and independent of,
whatever hardware (ENG-330's semiconductors, and their predecessors)
happens to be available at a given moment — theoretical computer science
asks and answers questions about computation in the abstract that remain
valid regardless of which generation of physical machine eventually runs
the resulting algorithms.

## Regional specifics

Al-Khwarizmi's work is significant enough that it gives the entire field
its name, a rare and direct linguistic trace of a foundational non-European
contribution that survives explicitly into modern technical vocabulary
rather than being quietly absorbed and reattributed the way several other
cases in this record have been. East Asian abacus and calculating-device
traditions achieve highly effective practical arithmetic without an
accompanying formal computational theory, showing effective calculating
technology and a theoretical science of computation are separable
achievements — mechanical calculation can work extremely well without
anyone formalizing what it means for a procedure to be "computable" at all.

## Hook

The theory of computation — algorithms and computability — exists and
develops as its own coherent discipline independent of any particular
physical hardware, tracing back to a formal step-by-step-procedure concept
over a millennium before an executing machine exists, and remaining the
theoretical foundation ENG-330's semiconductor hardware eventually
executes rather than something that hardware itself generates. NAT-240
splits off the separate mathematical question of quantifying information
itself, a distinct achievement resting on probability rather than on
computability.
