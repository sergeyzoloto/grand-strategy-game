A knowledge-producing institution's accumulated capacity does not only
fade through neglect or succession — it can be destroyed outright, in a
single violent event, with recovery, where it happens at all, requiring
renewed investment from an entirely different institutional base rather
than a simple resumption of what existed before.

## Timing

The Mongol siege and sack of Baghdad in 1258 ends the House of Wisdom's
centuries-long translation-and-extension project (NAT-050) — which
NAT-050's own stated date range runs only to 1200, stopping just short of
its own central institution's actual, violent ending.

## Mechanism

The House of Wisdom's accumulated scientific corpus — the manuscripts,
the trained scholars, the standing patronage relationship with the
caliphate — is a form of capital that took generations of sustained
investment to build (NAT-050's own mechanism) and that the 1258 conquest
destroys in weeks: the city's libraries and archives are damaged or
looted, a substantial portion of the scholarly community is killed or
scattered, and the specific institutional structure (caliphal patronage
of centralized translation and research) that produced and sustained the
corpus ceases to exist in Baghdad itself.

## Social consequence

The destruction of a specific institutional home for accumulated
knowledge does not necessarily mean the knowledge itself vanishes
entirely — surviving scholars, manuscripts already copied and dispersed
to other cities, and independent centers of learning in Cairo, Persia,
and later Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal courts continue and in places
extend the same broader scientific tradition — but the specific, unified,
centrally-patronized institution NAT-050 describes does not survive, and
recovery is a matter of rebuilding capacity elsewhere from a different
political and institutional base rather than restoring what Baghdad
specifically had.

## Regional specifics

Baghdad's fall is a single, well-dated, unambiguous political and military
event; the scale of the resulting intellectual loss is the genuinely
disputed part, given both the popular account's later exaggeration and
the fact that other regional centers of Islamic scholarship were already
established or growing before 1258 and continued producing serious work
for centuries afterward.

## Contested

The popular account of catastrophic, near-total loss (the ink-blackened
Tigris story) significantly postdates the event and likely overstates
both the volume of material actually destroyed and how singularly
devastating it was for the Islamic scientific tradition overall, since
scholarship continued vigorously elsewhere. Model the institutional
destruction in Baghdad itself as certain, while treating the true
magnitude of net knowledge loss — as opposed to capacity that had already
relocated or continued in other centers — as an open, probabilistic
question rather than a settled catastrophic total.

## Hook

A depleting-pool event: the `accumulated scientific corpus` a
knowledge-producing institution like the House of Wisdom builds up over
generations (NAT-050) can be destroyed outright in a single violent
event rather than only eroding gradually through neglect, with any
recovery requiring fresh investment from a different institutional base
entirely rather than simple continuation of what existed before.
