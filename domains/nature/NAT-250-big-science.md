Research at a scale where the instrument, not the investigator, is the limiting
factor. Once an experiment needs a facility that costs more than a university can
raise, funding becomes political, authorship becomes collective, and access
becomes a chokepoint. All three change together, and none of them reverses.

## Timing

From Lawrence's Berkeley cyclotrons of the 1930s, through the Manhattan Project
from 1942, the Soviet atomic and space programmes, and CERN from 1954, to Apollo
through 1969, the Human Genome Project of 1990-2003 and the Superconducting Super
Collider's cancellation in 1993. India's programme runs from 1945, China's from
1956, and Japan's reconstruction from the 1950s. The form continues past 2000 in
ITER, LIGO and the Large Hadron Collider.

## Mechanism

**The instrument sets the limit.** Above a certain facility cost, the question of
who can do a piece of research stops depending on who can think of it and starts
depending on who can build and run the machine. That cost is an `instrument
threshold`, the minimum capital needed to take part in a field at its frontier at
all. Below it, talent buys nothing. Above it, talent works only through access to
the facility. Three things change together once a field crosses it.

**Funding becomes political.** A cyclotron, a reactor, a telescope or a collider
is a line in a state budget, so whoever appropriates the money sets part of the
research agenda. This is where science acquires its permanent relationship with
military and national-prestige funding. The Manhattan Project and the Soviet
atomic project were weapons programmes, and Apollo and the Soviet space programme
were prestige contests, which at their peak took around four per cent of the
American federal budget. Vannevar Bush's _Science, the Endless Frontier_ of 1945
argued for continuing wartime state funding in peacetime, and it became the charter
for a permanent arrangement. The price is that a field's direction can be changed
by a legislature rather than by evidence. The Super Collider was ended by a vote,
not a result.

**Authorship becomes collective.** NAT-150's society assigns credit to individual
investigators, and priority disputes are fought between named people. A detector
paper with hundreds or thousands of authors breaks that system. The individual is
no longer the unit that did the work. As a result:

- **Careers** are built inside collaborations.
- **Graduate students** train as members of subsystem teams.
- **Recognition concentrates on leaders**, while the prizes themselves still
  go to at most three people.

The scientific institution's reward structure was built for a scale of work that
no longer exists in these fields.

**Access becomes a chokepoint.** There is one facility, or a very few, so a
national committee or an international consortium decides who runs which
experiment. Scientific capability in the field becomes a function of access to
infrastructure rather than of individual talent. That is a concentration
mechanism with geopolitical consequences. A state or consortium holding the
facility decides which foreign scientists are admitted. States below the threshold
either pool, as CERN's members did, or send their scientists abroad. The resulting
pull is the migration MIG-260 describes, driven by facilities rather than by
salaries.

**Secrecy severs checking.** The publication norm that NAT-150's society rests on
works only if results can be seen. Big Science grew up inside weapons programmes,
and the American Atomic Energy Act of 1946 treated nuclear information as "born
secret", classified from the moment it exists. A result that cannot be published
cannot be checked, which removes outside scrutiny exactly where the stakes are
highest. Argentina's Huemul project shows the failure plainly. A secret facility
announced fusion, and no one outside could test the claim until the state's own
physicists were sent in. MTH-072 records the parallel in cryptography: GCHQ
invented public-key encryption and could not publish it, so credit went to others
and the work was checked only by people inside.

**The opportunity cost is the entry's best mechanic.** A state that funds one
collider does not fund a thousand small laboratories, and the two strategies have
completely different return profiles:

- **Concentration** buys discoveries in the few fields that sit above an
  instrument threshold, and nothing in the rest.
- **Distribution** buys broad, incremental, often surprising progress across
  many fields. Lasers, molecular biology's early techniques and much of
  materials science came from small groups. But it can never reach a field
  whose threshold it does not cross.

Neither dominates. The right mix depends on which fields a state needs to reach,
and the historical argument is still running.

## Social consequence

At the scale of a working life, Big Science changes what a scientist is. A
physicist at Berkeley in the 1930s, Los Alamos in the 1940s or CERN in the 1980s
works in a group organised like an engineering firm. There are project managers,
shift rotas and subsystem responsibilities, and the laboratory director runs a
budget larger than most universities'. Security clearance becomes a condition of
employment in the weapons-linked fields, and with it come loyalty investigations.
The same political pressure that deported Qian Xuesen to China handed China the
leader of its missile programme.

The scientist's relationship to the state changes too. Facility science is paid
for by governments and justified to them in terms of defence, prestige or
economic return, so leading scientists become political actors who testify to
legislatures and negotiate budgets. Bhabha in India, Kurchatov in the Soviet
Union and Lawrence in the United States each moved between laboratory and
government. Scientific standing now includes the ability to raise and defend a
facility budget. That is a different skill from doing science, and it is rewarded
separately.

## Regional specifics

This is not a Manhattan Project entry with appendices.

- **The United States** supplies the origin of the laboratory as organisation
  at Berkeley, the demonstration of industrialised research at Los Alamos,
  and the public-private race over the genome.
- **The Soviet Union** runs the parallel state model through closed cities
  and design bureaus, with total secrecy and no university at its centre.
- **CERN** is the consortium solution: states that could not cross the
  threshold alone crossing it together.

The non-Western cases carry real weight:

- **India** treated facility science as a condition of sovereignty from
  1945, building a reactor, a space agency and eventually a weapons
  capability on a poor country's budget, with a private industrial family
  funding its founding institute.
- **China** completed a weapons-and-satellite programme after its Soviet
  patron withdrew, staffed partly by scientists pushed back from the United
  States, then turned to civilian facilities.
- **Japan** rebuilt from the deliberate destruction of its cyclotrons into
  a leading host of large facilities, with no weapons programme at all.
  Kamiokande's neutrino results are among the most consequential facility
  discoveries of the period.

Argentina's Huemul project shows the secrecy failure. The United States' cancelled
Super Collider shows a legislature ending a field's direction. Sub-Saharan Africa
is the genuine absence. Outside South Africa no state crossed any instrument
threshold in the window, so capability in facility-scale fields was zero however
good the scientists were, and those scientists could reach the frontier only by
emigrating. WAR-330 carries the deterrence strategy the weapons programmes served,
and WAR-315 carries radar, the other wartime programme that showed science could
be organised at industrial scale.

## Contested

Whether facility science is a good use of research money is disputed.
Weinberg named the phenomenon and warned of it. The Super Collider's cancellation
divided physicists by subfield, and critics argued many small laboratories would
return more for the same money. There is no general answer, because concentrated
and distributed funding have different return profiles. Model the choice as a
trade-off conditional on which fields a state needs to reach, with neither
strategy dominating, and treat blanket claims of superior productivity for either
as contested.

## Hook

Fields have an `instrument threshold`, the facility capital needed to take part
at the frontier. Below it, talent produces nothing in that field. Crossing it
changes three things at once, irreversibly:

- **Funding:** research becomes a state budget line that a legislature can
  redirect or cancel.
- **Authorship:** credit becomes collective and the individual-priority reward
  system stops fitting.
- **Access:** the facility becomes a chokepoint that decides who can do the
  work, and so becomes a source of geopolitical leverage and of scientific
  migration toward it, since the facility gap widens the `skill differential`
  that pulls researchers abroad.

States below a threshold can pool into a consortium or lose their scientists to
emigration. Weapons-linked facilities impose secrecy that raises `information
sovereignty` over results and removes outside checking, so claims made inside them
can be wrong without anyone able to find out.

The central decision is an opportunity cost. A budget spent on one facility reaches
a threshold field and funds nothing else, and the same budget spread across many
laboratories produces broad progress but cannot reach that field. Neither strategy
dominates, and which pays depends on which fields the state needs.
