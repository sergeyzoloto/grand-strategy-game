Placing the sun rather than Earth at the center of planetary motion — a
model that resolves specific technical problems in Earth-centered
astronomy at the cost of a religious and intuitive challenge far larger
than the mathematics alone would suggest.

## Timing

The Maragha observatory school's key mathematical innovations date from
1259 through the following two centuries; Copernicus publishes his
heliocentric model in 1543; Galileo's telescopic observations (from 1609,
enabled by the optical theory of NAT-016) and Kepler's refinements supply
the decisive observational support before Newton's 1687 mechanical
synthesis (NAT-004) makes it the mathematically compelling default rather
than merely one competing model among several.

## Mechanism

Ptolemaic Earth-centered astronomy requires increasingly complex
mathematical patches (epicycles upon epicycles) to match observed planetary
motion accurately; heliocentrism removes much of that complexity at a
stroke by placing the sun at the center instead, but the specific
mathematical devices needed to make the geometry work cleanly (the "Tusi
couple" among them) had already been worked out by the Maragha school
generations before Copernicus applied them within a genuinely
heliocentric rather than Earth-centered framework. The model's decisive
empirical confirmation comes later still, once Galileo's telescope
(NAT-016) reveals Jupiter's moons and the phases of Venus — evidence a
purely mathematical comparison of the two models' relative elegance could
never have supplied on its own.

## Social consequence

Heliocentrism directly contradicts a literal reading of scripture in
multiple religious traditions and displaces humanity's cosmically central
position, which is why its acceptance provokes institutional religious
resistance (most famously Galileo's trial) far exceeding what the
underlying mathematical improvement alone would predict — the model's
technical merits and its cultural reception follow substantially different
logics.

## Regional specifics

The close correspondence between Maragha-school mathematical devices and
Copernicus's own model is one of the more striking documented parallels in
the history of science, strong enough that most historians consider some
transmission link likely even without a confirmed direct documentary
chain. Aryabhata's independent proposal of Earth's axial rotation
centuries earlier is a genuine partial anticipation that solves a related
but distinct problem without extending to full heliocentrism, and doesn't
connect to the later Maragha-to-Copernicus lineage at all — an
independent near-miss rather than a missing link in that specific chain.

## Contested

Whether Copernicus had direct access to Maragha-school texts or diagrams,
or independently arrived at mathematically identical solutions to the same
underlying geometric problem, is unresolved given the absence of
definitive surviving documentation either way. Model the Maragha work as an
established prior solution to the same technical problem, with the
transmission question itself treated as open and probabilistic rather than
settled in either direction.

## Hook

Heliocentrism's mathematical core was substantially worked out by the
Maragha school two centuries before Copernicus applied it within a fully
sun-centered model, and its decisive empirical confirmation required the
telescopic observation NAT-016's optical theory makes possible — evidence
mathematical elegance alone could never supply. The resulting religious
and cultural resistance to the finished model, once it displaced Earth's
cosmic centrality, follows a logic largely independent of and
disproportionate to its actual mathematical merit.
