A merchant community that settles abroad while keeping strong kin, religious,
or ethnic ties to its origin and to sister communities elsewhere becomes,
functionally, a long-distance trust and information network that no
contract-enforcement institution of the era could otherwise provide.

## Timing

Recurring independently across the whole span of this record wherever a
minority merchant community establishes durable nodes abroad: Phoenician and
later Jewish (see MIG-010), Armenian, and Greek networks in the Mediterranean
and Middle East from roughly -1000 onward, Gujarati/Tamil and Hausa/Dyula
networks from roughly 1000.

## Mechanism

Cross-border trade in this era faces a hard trust problem: no shared legal
system reliably enforces a contract between merchants of different polities.
A diaspora network solves this by substituting kin, ethnic, or religious
bonds and reputational enforcement (cheating a fellow network member gets you
excluded from the entire network, not just one counterparty) for
state-backed contract enforcement — the same function `contract enforcement`
plays elsewhere in this record, delivered here by social structure instead of
by a state.

## Social consequence

Diaspora merchant communities occupy a distinctive, often precarious social
position in every host society they settle in: economically valuable and
often locally indispensable, while remaining culturally and legally distinct
from the surrounding population in ways that make them simultaneously useful
to and vulnerable under local rulers — a tension that recurs across nearly
every diaspora case in this record regardless of the specific ethnic or
religious identity involved.

## Regional specifics

Gujarati/Tamil Indian Ocean networks and Hausa/Dyula Sahelian networks both
reach a comparable diaspora-trust architecture entirely independently of the
Mediterranean tradition and of each other, on caste and kin bases rather than
religious-minority status, showing the underlying trust-substitution mechanic
is generic to any sufficiently bounded, reputation-tracking community, not
tied to any one culture's particular history. Overseas Chinese networks
staying comparatively modest in this era despite an available and workable
kin-trust mechanism shows the limiting factor can be home-state policy
(restrictions on emigration and private overseas trade) rather than anything
about the network mechanism itself.

## Hook

A merchant diaspora substitutes network reputation for state contract
enforcement across borders, producing a `diaspora reach` stat that scales
trade-network trust and information flow with the diaspora's size and
cohesion — a trade-enabling asset for its host economies and a standing
vulnerability for the community itself.
