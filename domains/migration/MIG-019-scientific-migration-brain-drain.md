Scientists and other highly trained specialists migrate toward wherever
funding, equipment, and peer density are concentrated, a distinct migration
trigger driven by career and institutional opportunity rather than by land,
resource discovery, or subsistence pressure.

## Timing

The 1933 expulsion and emigration of scientists (many Jewish) from Nazi
Germany is the paradigm forced case; a much larger, mostly voluntary wave
from developing and middle-income countries toward North America and Western
Europe builds from the 1960s through the end of the century.

## Mechanism

A sufficiently large gap in research funding, equipment access, and peer
density between a scientist's home institution and available destinations
creates a migration pull keyed specifically to trained human capital rather
than to general wage differentials — the trigger is professional
opportunity and institutional capacity, not the broader economic
push/pull driving ordinary labor migration (MIG-016, MIG-022), which is why
this migration type can run strongly even from otherwise not-especially-poor
sending countries if their specific research infrastructure lags.

## Social consequence

The sending country typically bears the full cost of training (often
state-subsidized higher education) while the destination country captures
the resulting productive career, a transfer of investment that shows up
nowhere in ordinary trade or migration accounting and that sending-country
governments have historically struggled to either prevent or meaningfully
recoup.

## Regional specifics

The 1933 case shows this mechanic can also be forced rather than purely
opportunity-driven, with a receiving country (the United States particularly)
gaining enormous long-term scientific capacity from a sending country's
politically-driven expulsion. India's sustained, decades-long outflow from
the 1960s onward, tied initially to a specific US policy shift favoring
skilled immigrants, shows the mechanic can persist as an ongoing structural
drain rather than a one-time shock. African skilled emigration compounds
rather than simply scales down the same effect, because a smaller domestic
trained-population base to begin with means the same proportional outflow
represents a much larger relative capacity loss.

## Contested

Whether skilled emigration is a pure sending-country loss or part of a more
complex "brain circulation" that also returns remittances, investment, and
diaspora scientific networks is disputed, with real cases supporting both
readings depending on the sending country's own institutional capacity to
engage its diaspora. Model the emigration event as an unconditional loss of
trained capacity at time of departure, with a separate, conditional
circulation-return flow that only activates for sending countries maintaining
enough institutional capacity to receive it — not an automatic, universal
offset to the initial loss.

## Hook

A large enough research-capacity `skill differential` between a scientist's
home institution and available destinations triggers migration keyed
specifically to trained human capital, transferring the sending state's
education investment to the receiving state's productive economy — an
uncompensated capital transfer unless the sending state specifically
maintains diaspora-engagement capacity to claw some of it back as circulation.
