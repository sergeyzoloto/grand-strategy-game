A state reverses a prior migration flow by policy, ranging from voluntary
return incentives to coercive mass removal — a distinct mechanic from the
forced-transfer-between-states pattern in MIG-018, since here the state
acting is typically either the migrants' own origin state pulling them back
or a destination state pushing its own resident population out.

## Timing

Mexican repatriation programs of the 1930s (removing several hundred
thousand Mexican-origin residents, including many US-born citizens, from the
United States) are an early large case; postwar Japanese repatriation
(1946-1949) and postcolonial European settler returns from Africa and Asia
(clustering around the 1950s-1960s decolonization wave) follow.

## Mechanism

Repatriation converts a standing resident population — sometimes migrants
themselves, sometimes their descendants who have never lived in the
"origin" state at all — into a target for removal or return based on a
state's redefinition of who belongs, using the same documentation and
border-control apparatus (MIG-017) built for managing entry to instead
manage a mandated or incentivized exit.

## Social consequence

Because repatriation frequently targets people for ancestry or settler
status rather than for any individual choice or recent migration decision,
it can uproot populations with deep, multi-generational local roots (US-born
citizens in the Mexican case, settlers born in the colony in the African
cases) exactly as forcibly as it moves recent migrants, collapsing any
distinction between "recent migrant" and "long-settled resident" once a
state decides the relevant category is ancestry or colonial-era origin.

## Regional specifics

Postwar Japan's repatriation of several million overseas settlers and
soldiers proceeds under organized state and occupation administration within
a few years, a comparatively orderly execution of the same removal-by-
category mechanic. Algeria's pied-noir exodus around independence in 1962
runs a far more compressed and violence-driven timeline despite nominally
belonging to the same "postcolonial settler repatriation" category, showing
the same policy label can produce wildly different real-world processes
depending on whether the transition it accompanies is negotiated or violent.

## Hook

A state can redefine who belongs by ancestry, settler status, or colonial-
era category and repurpose its documentation/border apparatus to enforce
mandated departure — a `displacement pressure` event whose severity tracks
the surrounding political transition's own level of negotiation versus
violence, not the repatriation policy's stated administrative intent.
