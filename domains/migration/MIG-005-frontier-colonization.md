The second of two structurally distinct colonization mechanics: a large
founding population claims and works land directly, trading substantial
personal risk (travel, initial-years subsistence failure, conflict with
existing occupants) for land tenure that would have been unavailable at home.

## Timing

Greek agricultural colonization of Magna Graecia and the Black Sea coast runs
alongside the Mediterranean maritime-trading pattern from roughly -700;
large-scale frontier settlement recurs repeatedly and independently across
this record through the 19th-century American and Siberian frontiers.

## Mechanism

Frontier colonization requires a much larger founding population than a
trading post, because the entire value proposition is direct control and
working of land rather than a commercial relationship with existing
occupants — the cost is front-loaded personal risk (a large share of early
frontier populations across multiple documented cases fail to survive the
first several years) in exchange for land tenure and social mobility a
settler's origin society could not offer. The mechanism only works where
land is available faster than it can be legally or violently contested by
whoever already occupies or claims it.

## Social consequence

Frontier settlement reliably produces a distinct social profile at the
frontier itself — younger, more risk-tolerant, more socially mobile than the
origin population, and frequently in active or latent conflict with existing
occupants of the land being claimed — a pattern that recurs across eras and
regions specifically because it follows from the mechanic's own risk/reward
structure rather than from any one culture's particular character.

## Regional specifics

Chinese state-sponsored frontier colonization channels the same land-for-risk
bargain through direct state administration and military-agricultural
colonies rather than leaving it to individual initiative, showing the
mechanic can be either a bottom-up or a state-directed process while keeping
the same basic risk/reward structure. Sub-Saharan Africa's comparative scarcity
of this pattern before the colonial era plausibly reflects land-tenure systems
that don't convert easily into individually-claimable frontier plots, and
disease ecology constraining the livestock-based settlement package this
pattern usually depends on elsewhere — a structural absence, not a
population-pressure or mobility deficit.

## Hook

Frontier colonization trades high upfront settler risk and a large `settler
footprint` for land tenure unavailable at origin — mechanically the inverse
of maritime/trading-post colonization's low-footprint, low-risk profile in
MIG-004, and prone to direct conflict with prior occupants in a way the
trading-post pattern usually isn't.
