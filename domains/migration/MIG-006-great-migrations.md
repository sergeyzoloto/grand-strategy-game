A large state's weakening frontier meets an unrelated chain of population
displacement pressure originating further away, and the two processes
compound into a period of large-scale, cascading population movement that
neither side fully controls.

## Timing

Roughly 300-800, with the most intense phase in Europe running from the
Hunnic push into Gothic territory (370s) through the Lombard settlement of
Italy (568) and beyond; the roughly contemporary Chinese case runs through the
same centuries following the collapse of unified Han/Jin authority.

## Mechanism

A displacement shock (steppe political consolidation, climate-driven pasture
stress, or both) pushes one population into contact with, and often into the
territory of, another, which if that second population is also displaced
propagates the pressure onward in a chain — the mechanic that matters is
propagation, not any single group's origin story: a chain reaction requires a
first push and then a system with more populations than available uncontested
land to absorb it into.

## Social consequence

Frontier and receiving populations experience this simultaneously as invasion,
negotiated settlement, and opportunity, often in shifting proportions with the
same group over time — foederati arrangements (displaced groups formally
settled as frontier troops in exchange for land) sit on the same spectrum as
outright conquest, with the actual social outcome for both migrants and
existing occupants depending heavily on the receiving state's remaining
capacity to negotiate rather than simply collapse.

## Regional specifics

The roughly contemporaneous Chinese "Wu Hu" migrations follow a structurally
similar logic — steppe pressure meeting a fragmenting unified state — entirely
independently of the European case, suggesting the mechanic (state weakness
plus steppe-origin displacement pressure) is generic rather than tied to Rome
specifically. South Asia's absence of a comparable cascading event in the same
era, despite real steppe contact on its northwest frontier, shows the chain
only propagates at this scale when the receiving state's capacity is already
critically weakened — pressure alone isn't sufficient without that
precondition.

## Contested

Whether this period is better modeled as invasion or as a more gradual,
often-negotiated absorption of frontier peoples into an already-transforming
imperial system is a genuine, still-live historiographical dispute. Model
each specific group's entry into formerly Roman (or Han) territory with its
own coercion/negotiation ratio — some groups enter primarily through military
conquest, others through negotiated settlement as frontier auxiliaries — so
the period's overall character emerges from many different individual
outcomes rather than being scripted as uniformly one or the other.

## Hook

A displacement shock at one end of a contact chain propagates `displacement
pressure` onward through every population it touches, with each hop's outcome
(conquest, negotiated settlement, absorption) branching on the receiving
polity's remaining institutional strength rather than resolving the same way
every time.
