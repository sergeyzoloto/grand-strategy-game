The legal instrument and its diplomatic recognition are LAW/DIP's; this
entry owns what a state-issued, cross-recognized identity and travel
document does to the migration mechanic itself once it becomes standard —
converting movement from a physical/financial-cost activity into one also
gated by legal permission.

## Timing

Passport and visa requirements existed in earlier, patchier, and less
systematized forms for centuries, but World War One (from 1914) triggers
the specific shift to near-universal, standardized, state-issued travel
documentation across the major powers, with the practice becoming durably
entrenched rather than a temporary wartime measure over the following
decades.

## Mechanism

A passport/visa regime adds a second, independent gate to migration on top
of physical/financial cost (MIG-015's territory): even a migrant who can
afford passage and survive the journey can now be stopped by a state's legal
refusal to issue or recognize the necessary document. This converts
migration control from something a receiving state could only exercise
weakly (patrolling a long land or coastline) into something exercisable
administratively and in advance, at the point of departure or embarkation
rather than only at physical arrival.

## Social consequence

The regime creates, for the first time at this scale, a durable legal
distinction between documented and undocumented movement — the same
physical act of crossing a border now carries entirely different legal and
social consequences depending on paperwork status, a distinction with no
equivalent in any earlier migration mechanic in this record, where the
main constraints were physical and financial rather than administrative.

## Regional specifics

Europe's own pre-1914 experience of comparatively unrestricted cross-border
movement, followed by a sharp, largely wartime-security-driven tightening
that then persists in peacetime, shows this is a real historical regime
change rather than borders simply always having worked this way — the
"freedom of movement" baseline genuinely existed and was actively closed.
Post-Ottoman mandate borders and documentation regimes imposed by external
powers, cutting across pre-existing social and tribal geography rather than
emerging from it, add a further complication specific to that region: the
border-and-document institution itself, not just its enforcement, can be
externally imposed in a way that doesn't track any local administrative
tradition.

## Hook

A passport/visa regime adds legal-permission `border friction` as a second,
independent gate on top of migration's physical/financial `migration cost` —
a state can now block movement administratively regardless of whether the
migrant could otherwise afford and survive the journey, and can do so
selectively by document-issuing policy rather than needing to physically
enforce a border everywhere at once.
