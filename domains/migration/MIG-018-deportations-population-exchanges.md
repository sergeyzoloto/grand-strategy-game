Once the passport/border institution (MIG-017) establishes that a state can
administratively define and control who belongs within its territory, that
same administrative capacity becomes usable to forcibly move populations
judged not to belong — a state actively producing forced migration rather
than merely regulating voluntary movement.

## Timing

The Greek-Turkish population exchange (1923) is an early, unusually
formalized instance; the mid-20th century sees a cluster of further cases
including South Asian Partition (1947) and various European post-World War
Two forced population transfers, through roughly 1950.

## Mechanism

Once a state has the administrative apparatus to define, register, and
document who belongs to which national/ethnic category (a direct extension
of the passport/border-institution mechanic in MIG-017), that same apparatus
can be turned to forced relocation as a deliberate tool of national
homogenization or territorial consolidation — the underlying capability is
the same, but the trigger flips from managing voluntary movement to
producing involuntary movement by state decision.

## Social consequence

Forced population transfer imposes the sudden, often violent loss of home,
property, and community on a scale and timeline the affected population has
no ability to negotiate or delay, converting what a receiving or sending
state may record administratively as an orderly "exchange" into what the
displaced population experiences as sudden catastrophic loss — the two
framings coexist because they describe the same event from opposite ends of
the state's administrative control over it.

## Regional specifics

The Greek-Turkish case's unusually formal, internationally-supervised
treaty structure makes it something close to a controlled instance of this
mechanic — negotiated, documented, administered — useful as a contrast case
against Partition's far larger and far less controlled version of the same
underlying process, where comparable or greater displacement occurs through
communal violence and rushed boundary-drawing rather than a negotiated
bilateral framework. The same broad mechanic (state-driven forced population
movement to match territory to a defined national category) produces
outcomes ranging from relatively orderly treaty implementation to
uncontrolled mass violence depending on how much administrative and
diplomatic machinery actually governs the transfer.

## Hook

Once a state holds the administrative capacity to define and document
national belonging (MIG-017), that capacity becomes a lever for state-
triggered forced migration — a treaty-mediated `displacement pressure` spike
if a bilateral diplomatic framework governs the transfer, or a much larger
and more violent one if it doesn't.
