Former colonial ties and active postwar labor recruitment jointly lower the
effective `border friction` and `migration cost` between a former colony
and its former metropole, or between a labor-scarce oil economy and nearby
labor-surplus states, producing a distinct migration channel shaped as much
by prior political relationship as by wage differential alone.

## Timing

British postwar labor recruitment and Commonwealth-citizenship-linked
migration (from the 1948 Empire Windrush voyage onward) runs through the
1960s-1970s; Gulf labor migration scales sharply after the 1973 oil-price
shock and continues through 1980 and well beyond this entry's formal window.

## Mechanism

Postcolonial ties (shared citizenship categories, language, existing
administrative and social networks) function as a `border friction` discount
specific to the pair of countries involved, on top of whatever wage
differential is separately driving the migration — the same underlying labor
migration mechanic as MIG-016, but with an additional, non-economic
lowered-friction channel that a purely wage-driven model would miss. Gulf
labor migration instead runs on active demand-side recruitment under a
sponsorship system that ties a migrant's legal status directly to a specific
employer, a different friction-and-control structure achieving comparable
volume without the citizenship-linked channel.

## Social consequence

Postcolonial migrants moving on the citizenship-linked channel typically
arrive with at least nominal legal standing and an eventual path to
permanent settlement, provoking the receiving society's political
reaction over integration and belonging on those terms. Gulf-model guest
workers under kafala-type sponsorship arrive with no such path regardless of
tenure, producing a large resident non-citizen labor population by design —
the same underlying labor-migration volume, structured toward two very
different long-run social outcomes by the legal-status design choice alone.

## Regional specifics

British postwar recruitment and Commonwealth citizenship rules produce
substantial, eventually permanently-settling migration from South Asia and
the Caribbean specifically because of the citizenship-category discount those
prior colonial ties created. Gulf-state labor migration achieves comparable
or greater volume through active economic recruitment under a
no-settlement-path sponsorship model instead, showing large labor migration
volumes can be produced by either channel independently. African labor
migration in this same era running predominantly intra-continentally rather
than toward former European metropoles shows postcolonial political ties
don't automatically produce a Europe-directed migration channel everywhere
they exist — the channel depends on active recruitment and citizenship
policy specifically, not just colonial history alone.

## Hook

Prior political relationship (postcolonial citizenship ties, or active
demand-side recruitment under a sponsorship model) can lower `border
friction` and `migration cost` for a specific country-pair independent of
raw wage differential, and the legal-status design of that channel — path to
settlement versus permanent guest-worker status — is a separate policy choice
that shapes the migration's long-run social outcome as much as its volume.
