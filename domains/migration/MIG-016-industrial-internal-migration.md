Manufacturing employment concentrated in cities pulls rural population
toward it at a pace and scale distinct from either the slow demic
wave-of-advance (MIG-002) or resource-rush spikes (MIG-014) — a sustained,
demand-driven pull rather than either a slow diffusion or a sudden trigger.

## Timing

From the early phases of British industrialization (mid-18th century)
through the classic Western urbanization wave into the early 20th century,
recurring at a much larger scale in China from roughly 1980.

## Mechanism

Concentrated manufacturing employment creates a sustained urban wage
differential over rural agricultural income, pulling migration at a rate set
by the pace of industrial job creation itself rather than by any single
triggering event — the migration flow is a direct, continuously-adjusting
function of the urban-rural wage and opportunity gap, rising and falling with
industrial demand rather than spiking once and dispersing.

## Social consequence

Industrial internal migration produces the urban-graveyard-adjacent
overcrowding and public-health strain covered from the receiving side in
HLT-002 and HLT-006, alongside a genuine, if often precarious, upward
economic mobility for migrants relative to rural conditions — the same
movement is simultaneously a public-health and urban-planning problem for
the receiving city and a real economic opportunity for the people making the
move, with both readings valid at once.

## Regional specifics

China's late-20th-century version of this migration reaches a scale that
dwarfs the original Western industrial wave while operating under a
household-registration system that formally restricts migrants' access to
urban public services relative to registered residents — a legal
constraint layered on top of the same underlying wage-differential mechanic,
producing large-scale migration alongside a large, legally-distinct
semi-permanent migrant population rather than full urban absorption.
Sub-Saharan Africa's substantial rural-to-urban movement without a comparably
large formal-industrial pull shows the wage-differential mechanic isn't the
only thing that can drive this migration type — rural push factors alone can
produce comparable movement without the same receiving-city economic base.

## Hook

Sustained urban-rural wage differential from concentrated manufacturing
employment drives a continuous migration flow that tracks industrial demand
directly, straining a receiving city's `urban absorption capacity` and
potentially triggering a formal legal-status distinction (as with hukou)
between long-settled and migrant urban residents rather than full
integration.
