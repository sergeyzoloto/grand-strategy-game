Once the passport/border institution (MIG-017) establishes that a state
can administratively define and control who belongs within its
territory, that same administrative capacity becomes usable to forcibly
move populations judged not to belong — whether pulling a state's own
citizens or ancestry-linked diaspora back within its borders, pushing a
resident population out by redefining who belongs, or exchanging
populations bilaterally with another state — a state actively producing
forced migration rather than merely regulating voluntary movement.

## Timing

The Greek-Turkish population exchange (1923) is an early, unusually
formalized bilateral instance; Mexican repatriation programs of the
1930s remove several hundred thousand Mexican-origin residents from the
United States; the mid-20th century sees a cluster of further cases
including South Asian Partition (1947) and postwar Japanese repatriation
(1946-1949); postcolonial European settler returns from Africa, most
prominently Algeria's pied-noir exodus around 1962, extend the same
underlying mechanic through the decolonization wave and beyond.

## Mechanism

Once a state has the administrative apparatus to define, register, and
document who belongs to which national, ethnic, or ancestry-linked
category (a direct extension of the passport/border-institution mechanic
in MIG-017), that same apparatus can be turned to forced relocation as a
deliberate tool of national homogenization, territorial consolidation, or
ancestry-based exclusion. The underlying capability is identical whether
the state acting is pulling back its own citizens or diaspora
(repatriation), pushing out a resident population it no longer recognizes
as belonging (settler expulsion), or exchanging populations with another
state under treaty (bilateral exchange) — what changes is only the
trigger and the direction of movement, not the administrative mechanism
producing it.

## Social consequence

Forced population transfer imposes the sudden, often violent loss of
home, property, and community on a scale and timeline the affected
population has no ability to negotiate or delay — and because the
relevant category is frequently ancestry, settler status, or colonial-era
origin rather than any individual choice or recent migration decision, it
can uproot populations with deep, multi-generational local roots (US-born
citizens in the Mexican case, settlers born in the colony in the African
cases) exactly as forcibly as it moves recent migrants, collapsing any
distinction between "recent migrant" and "long-settled resident" once a
state decides the relevant category is ancestry or origin rather than
residence.

## Regional specifics

The Greek-Turkish case's unusually formal, internationally-supervised
treaty structure (administered under League of Nations auspices) makes it
something close to a controlled instance of this mechanic — negotiated,
documented, administered — useful as a contrast against Partition's far
larger and far less controlled version of the same underlying process,
where comparable or greater displacement occurs through communal violence
and rushed boundary-drawing rather than a negotiated bilateral framework.
Postwar Japan's repatriation of several million overseas settlers and
soldiers proceeds under organized state and occupation administration
within a few years, a comparatively orderly execution of the same
removal-by-category mechanic. Algeria's pied-noir exodus around
independence in 1962 runs a far more compressed and violence-driven
timeline despite nominally belonging to the same broad category, showing
the same underlying mechanic can produce outcomes ranging from orderly
treaty implementation to uncontrolled mass violence depending on how much
administrative and diplomatic machinery actually governs the transfer —
not on which state initiates, or which direction the population moves.

## Hook

Once a state holds the administrative capacity to define and document
national or ancestry-based belonging (MIG-017), that capacity becomes a
lever for state-triggered forced migration in any direction — pulling its
own diaspora back, pushing out a population it redefines as foreign, or
exchanging populations bilaterally — producing a treaty-mediated
`displacement pressure` spike where a diplomatic framework governs the
transfer, or a much larger and more violent one where it doesn't,
regardless of which state initiated the movement or why.
