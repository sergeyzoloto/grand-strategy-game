The longest continuously-documented instance of the diaspora-trade-network
mechanic in MIG-070, and the clearest recurring case of a diaspora's
economic value to host societies coexisting with, and periodically inflaming,
its legal and social precarity within them.

## Timing

From the Babylonian exile (-586) through Second Temple-era dispersal across
the Mediterranean and Persian worlds, into the specifically European medieval
and early modern pattern of shifting legal status (expulsions, restricted
occupations, periodic protection) that the 19th-century "Jewish Question"
debate crystallizes around, through to 1948.

## Mechanism

The diaspora-trust-network mechanic from MIG-070 applies with unusual
longevity and geographic spread here, but interacts with a specific
additional pressure: host states periodically restrict Jewish communities
from land ownership or guild membership while simultaneously relying on
Jewish moneylending and commercial networks precisely because Christian and
some Islamic prohibitions on usury created a niche few others could legally
fill — a state-created economic dependency that coexists with, and doesn't
prevent, legal marginality and periodic expulsion.

## Social consequence

The community's occupational concentration in finance and trade, driven
substantially by external restriction rather than free choice, becomes in
turn a recurring justification for further restriction or expulsion — a
self-reinforcing cycle where the same legal exclusion that pushes the
community into a specific economic niche later gets cited as evidence
against it, visible across multiple, otherwise very different host societies
and eras.

## Regional specifics

The Second Temple-era Mediterranean diaspora and North African/Ethiopian
communities develop under sufficiently different legal and social conditions
from each other, and from the specifically European medieval pattern, that
"the Jewish diaspora experience" is really several distinct regional
trajectories sharing a common origin and network structure rather than one
uniform story — the European pattern that the 19th-century "Jewish Question"
specifically responds to is itself only one branch of a much longer and more
geographically varied diaspora history.

## Contested

Whether the 19th-century "Jewish Question" reflects a genuine, if badly
handled, structural tension between diaspora-network social organization and
the new nation-state's assumption of ethnic homogeneity, or is better read as
a retrospective label papering over what was substantially opportunistic
scapegoating with no real "question" being asked in good faith, is disputed.
Model the underlying tension (diaspora network structure meeting a
homogenizing state) as real and structural, but let a specific host state's
institutional strength and economic conditions determine whether that
tension resolves toward emancipation, continued restriction, or violent
expulsion — not scripting any one outcome as automatic.

## Hook

A long-duration diaspora's `diaspora reach` value (economic usefulness to
host states) and its legal precarity move on largely independent tracks and
can both be high simultaneously — the community's usefulness doesn't buy
legal security, and its legal marginality doesn't reduce its economic
importance, an authority/vulnerability split with no automatic correction
mechanism.
