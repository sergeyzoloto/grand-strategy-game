This entry owns only the settler-migration waves themselves — who moved, in
what numbers, under what legal status, to which parts of the Americas. The
conquest (WAR), the resulting extraction economy and bullion trade (COM/FIN,
see `COM-011`), the demographic collapse of indigenous populations (HLT-017),
and colonial legal systems (LAW) are each their own story, cross-referenced
rather than duplicated here — this is large enough across enough domains
that it's a strong future candidate for a `_meta/threads/` page once those
other domains have entries of their own to link together.

## Timing

From the earliest sustained Spanish settlement (1490s-1500s) through the
main wave of British, French, and continued Iberian colonization across the
16th to 18th centuries.

## Mechanism

Settler migration to the Americas runs through several distinct legal and
economic channels simultaneously: free family settlement aimed at land
tenure (the frontier-colonization mechanic, MIG-005), indentured servitude
(a fixed-term labor contract trading passage cost for years of bound labor,
functionally a financed-migration mechanism for people who couldn't
otherwise afford the crossing), and smaller administrative/military/religious
settler populations governing and extracting from a much larger surviving or
enslaved labor base. Which channel dominates in a given colony strongly
shapes that colony's subsequent social structure.

## Social consequence

Indentured servitude in particular restructures migration itself into a
financial instrument: a passage that would otherwise be unaffordable becomes
accessible by mortgaging several years of the migrant's own labor, extending
the reach of long-distance migration well down the economic ladder from
where frontier colonization's own upfront-risk-bearing settlers usually sit,
at the cost of a period of near-unfree status on arrival.

## Regional specifics

British North America's disproportionate skew toward family agricultural
settlement produces a demographically self-sustaining, land-hungry settler
population reproducing the frontier-colonization dynamic of MIG-005 at large
scale. Spanish and Portuguese America's smaller, more male-heavy
administrative and extractive settler population sits on top of an existing
large indigenous (and later enslaved) labor base instead, a structurally
different settlement profile achieving political and economic control with a
much lower settler-to-existing-population ratio. French New France's
persistently thin settler numbers and closer fur-trade relationship with
indigenous nations show the same colonizing power choosing an essentially
different colonization type (closer to MIG-004's trading-post model) in a
different part of the same continent — colonization strategy is a choice made
per-colony, not a fixed national character.

## Hook

Settler migration to a colonized region can run through free-settlement,
indentured, or thin-administrative-elite channels simultaneously within the
same broad colonization event, each producing a different long-run `settler
footprint` and colony social structure — the same colonizing state can and
does run more than one channel at once in different territories.
