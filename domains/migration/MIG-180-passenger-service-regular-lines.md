Scheduled, commercially-operated steamship passenger service converts
transoceanic migration from a risky, one-off, self-organized undertaking
into a predictable, ticketed, mass-market service — the single biggest drop
in the effective cost and uncertainty of long-distance migration in this
record.

## Timing

Regular transatlantic steamship passenger lines scale from the 1840s-1850s
onward, reaching peak mass-emigration volume in the decades before 1914;
comparable Indian Ocean indentured-labor shipping lines operate on a similar
commercial-scheduling logic from 1834.

## Mechanism

A scheduled line converts migration cost from a large, uncertain, often
one-time expenditure (chartering or building passage informally, as earlier
colonization waves required) into a predictable ticket price competitively
set by multiple operating companies, while steam power's speed and
reliability sharply cut both the ticket-holder's transit-mortality risk and
the calendar time a migration decision commits a household to — all three
changes compound to pull migration decisions within reach of a much larger
and less wealthy population than before.

## Social consequence

Mass, affordable, scheduled passenger service is what makes late-19th-century
transatlantic emigration a genuine mass phenomenon (tens of millions of
people) rather than the elite or state-organized undertaking earlier
colonization waves mostly were, extending the practical option of
transoceanic migration for the first time down to ordinary laboring
households making an individual economic calculation rather than joining a
state-sponsored or communally-organized settlement project.

## Regional specifics

Indian indentured-labor shipping lines apply the same commercial-scheduling
logic to a population moving under bound-labor contract terms rather than
free emigration, showing the infrastructure innovation (regular, ticketed,
scheduled service) is separable from the legal status of who travels on it.
Sub-Saharan Africa's continued channeling primarily through forced rather
than commercial-passenger movement in this same era, despite the same
steamship technology being available, underlines that infrastructure
availability alone doesn't determine which migration mechanic a region's
population actually experiences.

## Hook

Regularized, competitively-priced passenger-line service sharply lowers
`migration cost` and raises effective `migration range` for a much larger
population than could previously afford or risk transoceanic movement,
converting mass emigration from an elite or state-organized undertaking into
an individual household's ordinary economic decision.
