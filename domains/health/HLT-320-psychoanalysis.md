A theoretical system for the self and its disorders that acquires enormous
cultural authority and diagnostic vocabulary while its specific clinical
efficacy claims remain persistently, and to this day, contested.

## Timing

Freud's foundational work dates from the mid-1890s; the framework's peak
cultural and institutional influence, especially in the United States, runs
through the first half of the 20th century.

## Mechanism

Psychoanalysis offers a comprehensive, internally coherent theory of mental
life (unconscious drives, repression, developmental stages) that functions
much like the premodern codified medical systems in HLT-150: coherence and
explanatory reach drive its adoption and prestige largely independent of
controlled evidence for its specific therapeutic claims, which remained
difficult to test rigorously for decades and unfavorable to it once outcome
studies became more rigorous.

## Social consequence

The framework's vocabulary (repression, the unconscious, projection) diffuses
into general educated discourse and popular culture — especially in the
United States — far beyond the population that ever undergoes analysis
itself, making it a cultural-authority phenomenon at least as much as a
clinical-practice one; this is close to the same authority/efficacy split
described below for premodern medicine, playing out again in a supposedly
more scientific era.

## Regional specifics

Its American reception amplifies the framework's cultural reach well past its
European origin's scale, embedding it in areas (advertising, media,
middle-class self-understanding) far from clinical practice. Its entanglement
with French colonial psychiatry in North Africa produces a different and more
adversarial reception: applied by colonial institutions while simultaneously
critiqued by figures within that same system as an instrument that could
pathologize colonized subjects' resistance to colonization itself — reception
as contested tool, not neutral import.

## Contested

Clinical-efficacy critiques of psychoanalysis specifically (starting
prominently with Eysenck's mid-century outcome-comparison work) are long-
running and remain unresolved as a clean verdict, even as the framework's
broader intellectual and cultural influence is not seriously disputed. Model
`medical legitimacy` (high, durable, culturally diffuse) and `medical
efficacy` (contested, plausibly modest relative to its own claims) as
independent stats, mirroring HLT-150's premodern medicine treatment rather
than assuming a more "modern" framework must have closed that gap.

## Hook

Psychoanalysis grants a large, durable `medical legitimacy`/cultural-authority
bonus for a self-understanding framework whose `medical efficacy` stays
contested and comparatively low — the same authority/efficacy split as
premodern codified medicine, recurring in a nominally scientific era.
