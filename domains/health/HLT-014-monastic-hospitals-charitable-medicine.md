The institution, endowment, and religious motivation behind charitable
medical care are REL's territory; this entry owns what the resulting
hospital actually does as a health-delivery mechanism, and how unevenly
that mechanism is distributed by tradition and region.

## Timing

Ashokan state-endowed hospitals (~-250, South Asia) are the earliest clear
case; Christian monastic infirmaries scale up across Europe from roughly the
6th century; Abbasid bimaristans appear from around 800 and become the most
clinically sophisticated model of the three for several following centuries.

## Mechanism

Charitable medicine substitutes an institution's endowment (religious
donation, state grant, or both) for the patient's own ability to pay, which
is what lets it reach populations market medicine never would. The actual
health payoff, though, still runs through whatever `medical efficacy` the
underlying medical system possesses (see HLT-011) — a well-endowed, well-
staffed hospital built on a low-efficacy theoretical medicine still delivers
mostly custodial care (shelter, food, comfort, a place to die attended) rather
than cures, which is not nothing, but is a different mechanic from actual
treatment efficacy.

## Social consequence

A charitable hospital is as much a visible monument to institutional piety and
prestige — for the religious order, the ruler, or the endowing merchant — as
it is a health-delivery mechanism, which is part of why
investment in hospital buildings and endowments doesn't track particularly
closely with investment in the medical knowledge that would make them more
effective.

## Regional specifics

Ashokan hospitals predate the European monastic model by roughly a
millennium, unsettling any assumption that institutional charitable medicine
is a European or even a Christian invention. Bimaristans meaningfully
outperform contemporary European monastic infirmaries on clinical
sophistication — teaching function, specialized wards, salaried physician
staff — making the Islamic world the more advanced node in this specific
mechanic for a long stretch of the period. China's reliance on state and
lineage welfare channels rather than a dedicated hospital building shows the
same charitable impulse can route through an entirely different institutional
container without implying less capacity or concern.

## Hook

Charitable-medicine endowment buys population health-access coverage
independent of, and typically outpacing, the underlying `medical efficacy` of
the system it delivers — a coverage stat and an efficacy stat that move on
separate tracks and don't have to agree with each other.
