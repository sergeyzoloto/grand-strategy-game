Mortality falls first (public health, nutrition, medicine improving across
this entire domain's earlier entries), population surges as fertility lags
behind the mortality drop, and then fertility itself falls — in the "second"
transition, often below replacement — as the economic and social value of
having many children changes.

## Timing

The first transition begins in Europe from roughly 1800, spreading unevenly
worldwide over the following two centuries; a second, sub-replacement-fertility
phase becomes visible in the most advanced cases (Europe, East Asia) from
around 1960 onward and is still unfolding.

## Mechanism

The first transition is a lag effect: mortality-reducing interventions
(sanitation, vaccination, antibiotics — this domain's own earlier entries)
arrive and take hold faster than the fertility-relevant social norms and
economic calculus around child-rearing adjust, producing a population boom in
the lag window before fertility eventually falls to match the new, lower
mortality reality. The second transition is a further shift past mere
replacement adjustment: once child mortality is reliably low and children
shift from net economic contributors (agricultural labor) to net economic
costs (extended education, urban living costs), the economically rational
number of children keeps falling, in some cases well below replacement.

## Social consequence

Societies mid-transition experience a large, historically unusual working-age-
heavy population bulge (the "demographic dividend") before population growth
slows — a temporary economic tailwind tied to the transition's timing, not a
permanent state. Societies that complete the second transition face the
opposite structural problem a generation or two later: a shrinking working-age
base supporting a growing elderly population, an entirely different set of
pressures than the ones that shaped this domain's earlier, high-mortality-era
entries.

## Regional specifics

East Asia's compressed transition — reaching first-transition completion
faster than Europe's original, slower case, then proceeding to some of the
lowest fertility rates ever recorded — shows the whole process can run much
faster once mortality-reducing technology is importable rather than needing
to be invented locally. India's sharp within-country regional divergence
shows the transition's pace is sensitive to sub-national factors (female
education and healthcare access especially) fine-grained enough that a single
national policy environment can produce dramatically different regional
outcomes. Sub-Saharan Africa's mortality-ahead-of-fertility pattern persisting
into the present is the clearest live case of the transition's well-established
lag structure still working itself out in real time, not an exception to it.

## Contested

Whether the second demographic transition's below-replacement fertility is
driven mainly by economic incentives (cost of children, opportunity cost of
women's foregone labor-force earnings) or by an independent values/ideational
shift (individualism, secularization, declining normative pressure to marry
and reproduce) is unresolved in the demographic literature. Model fertility
decline as the sum of two independent, separately-triggerable functions —
economic-cost-of-children and secular-individualist-values — so that a region
with rapid economic development but a slower values shift (or vice versa)
produces a visibly different fertility trajectory rather than everyone
following one universal curve keyed to income alone.

## Hook

Falling mortality creates a population-growth lag window before fertility
catches down to match it, and — once child-rearing's economic logic flips from
net-contributor to net-cost — the same downward fertility pressure can
overshoot replacement entirely, flipping a population-growth mechanic into a
population-aging one within the same causal chain.
