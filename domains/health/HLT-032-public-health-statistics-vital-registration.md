Systematic mortality recording and, later, spatial disease mapping convert
disease from a locally observed, anecdotally understood phenomenon into a
population-level, quantified, comparable one — the knowledge-
infrastructure precondition that lets a society notice this domain's own
patterns deliberately rather than only living through them.

## Timing

John Graunt's 1662 analysis of London's Bills of Mortality is the earliest
systematic attempt to extract population-level patterns from raw death
records; civil vital-registration systems formalize and spread across
European states through the following two centuries; John Snow's 1854
spatial mapping of a London cholera outbreak to a single contaminated
pump is the clearest early case of the method directly producing an
actionable, correct causal inference ahead of the underlying germ theory
that would later explain why it worked.

## Mechanism

Aggregating individual deaths and their recorded circumstances (age,
cause, location, occupation) into a systematic register converts disease
from something each household or physician experiences and interprets
locally into a pattern visible only at the population level — recurring
seasonal mortality spikes, occupation-linked risk, or, in Snow's case, a
literal spatial cluster around one water source — none of which any
single case, however carefully observed, could reveal on its own.

## Social consequence

Once a state or institution can see disease as a measurable population-
level pattern, intervention can in principle target the actual
statistical driver rather than proceed on inherited theory or authority
alone, as HLT-011's codified systems mostly did — Snow's pump-handle
removal is the paradigm case of an intervention justified by pattern
recognition succeeding well before, and independent of, any accepted
causal theory explaining why it worked.

## Regional specifics

This entry's development is concentrated in England specifically,
building on an unusually early and continuous tradition of urban
mortality recordkeeping (the Bills of Mortality) that gives Graunt's and
later Snow's analyses a data foundation not comparably available
elsewhere in this period.

## Hook

A gate: converts disease from a locally anecdotal experience into a
measurable, comparable, population-level pattern, raising a state's
epidemiological `legibility` and unlocking deliberate, evidence-targeted
public health intervention (HLT-006's later sanitary reforms) as opposed
to intervention guided by inherited theory or authority alone.
