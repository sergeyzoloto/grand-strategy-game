Mild stimulants — caffeine, coca, and their regional equivalents — become
durable social and economic institutions well before anyone has a
pharmacological account of why they work, valued simultaneously as a
sociability technology and a labor-endurance one.

## Timing

Tea in China is a mature, elaborated practice by 900; coffee's spread from
Ethiopian/Yemeni origins into a broader Middle Eastern and then European
social institution runs from roughly 1450 to 1700; coca and cacao in the
Americas predate all of this by at least a millennium on their own
independent timeline.

## Mechanism

A mild, reliably-sourced stimulant lets an individual sustain alertness and
output past what unaided physiology manages, at a small, recurring
consumption cost — a direct, tradeable productivity input centuries before
anyone frames it that way. The coffeehouse specifically adds a second
mechanism on top: a physical venue built around a stimulant that keeps
patrons alert and sociable for extended periods becomes a uniquely effective
place for information to circulate, functioning as an informal exchange and
news venue as much as a drinking establishment.

## Social consequence

Coffeehouses in the Ottoman world and then Europe become genuinely
significant sites of political and commercial gossip, negotiation, and
information exchange — regarded suspiciously by more than one authority
precisely because the same mechanic (alert, sociable people staying in one
place for hours) that makes coffeehouses good for business and information
also makes them good for the political conversations rulers wanted less of.
Tea in China develops instead primarily as an elaborated status and etiquette
practice, showing the same stimulant substance can anchor quite different
social institutions depending on the surrounding culture.

## Regional specifics

The independent American stimulant complex (coca, cacao) shares the same
underlying logic — status, ritual, and endurance value bundled onto a mild
stimulant — while developing with zero contact with the Old World tradition,
suggesting convergent rather than diffused social technology. South Asia's
comparatively late adoption of tea and coffee, despite sitting geographically
between the two Old World centers of stimulant culture, shows proximity alone
doesn't guarantee diffusion — an absence worth its own explanation rather than
an assumed inevitability.

## Hook

A stimulant-anchored public venue (coffeehouse-equivalent) raises local
`information speed` and commercial deal-flow as a side effect of the
socializing it was built around, making it attractive and occasionally
threatening to authority for the same underlying reason.
