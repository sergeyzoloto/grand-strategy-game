A mortality shock large enough (30-50% in the worst-hit regions) doesn't just
kill people, it breaks the taken-for-granted daily assumptions — about labor's
price, about status display, about the reliability of the world — that a
generation had absorbed as simply how things are.

## Timing

1331 onward from a Central Asian origin point, reaching the Black Sea and
Mediterranean by 1347 and most of Europe and the Middle East by 1353.

## Mechanism

A sudden, massive reduction in labor supply relative to land and capital
sharply raises the bargaining position of surviving laborers — wages rise,
labor mobility increases as lords compete for scarce tenants, and the prior
equilibrium that fixed labor's price and obligations at a customary,
habitual level breaks because the old assumptions no longer match the new
scarcity. This is a habitus rupture rather than an ordinary price adjustment:
the change isn't just in wages but in what people expect is normal or owed.

## Social consequence

Surviving laborers in the hardest-hit regions gain real bargaining power and,
often, real income — visible in the sumptuary-law backlash that follows in
several European jurisdictions, explicitly aimed at stopping newly-flush
lower-status people from dressing or spending above their old station. Elites
experience the same event as a threat to a social order they'd taken as fixed,
which is exactly why the legal backlash is aimed at appearance and spending
rather than at wages directly — the fight is over the marker, because the
underlying economic shift is harder to legislate away.

## Regional specifics

Mamluk Egypt and Syria suffer comparable mortality without producing anywhere
near as developed a "rising peasant bargaining power" narrative in the
historiography — this may reflect a genuinely different agrarian-labor
starting structure (different land tenure and coercive-labor baseline) rather
than a smaller true effect, but the asymmetry in how well each region's
version of this event has been studied is itself worth noting. China's
plague-era mortality is entangled with the Yuan-Ming transition closely enough
that isolating a comparable labor-market effect from the general chaos of
dynastic collapse hasn't been done with confidence.

## Contested

Whether the well-known post-plague European outcomes (rising real wages,
weakening serfdom) were caused mainly by the mortality shock itself, or by
longer-running agrarian trends already in motion that the plague only
accelerated, remains disputed. Model the immediate labor-scarcity wage/status
effect as real and scaled directly to local mortality rate, but branch its
durable institutional consequence — serfdom actually erodes, versus lords
successfully re-impose the old terms once the immediate shortage eases — on
the prior strength of lordly coercive and legal institutions in that region,
per design principle 1, rather than having high mortality auto-resolve into
one fixed outcome everywhere.

## Hook

A large enough mortality shock ruptures the existing labor-price/status
habitus and forces a re-negotiation whose outcome (workers keep the gains, or
elites claw them back via law) branches on prior institutional strength
rather than on the mortality number alone.
