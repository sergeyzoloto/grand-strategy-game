Codified rules about what may be eaten, prepared by whom, and shared with
whom do two jobs at once: they plausibly reduce exposure to some specific
foodborne risks, and they reliably mark and enforce social boundaries — this
entry stays on the health-functional reading, with the fuller institutional
and theological treatment belonging to REL.

## Timing

Middle Eastern kosher law is textually attested from roughly -1200 onward;
comparable South Asian caste-commensality norms solidify by roughly -600;
neither is a single dateable event so much as a long codification process
across centuries.

## Mechanism

Two mechanisms run in parallel under one set of rules. The disease-avoidance
mechanism is narrow and real where it applies: certain prohibited foods or
mandated preparation methods (thorough slaughter and drainage rules,
pork avoidance in regions with endemic trichinosis) measurably reduce specific
foodborne risks, without the societies enforcing them having any germ-theory
understanding of why. The boundary mechanism is much broader: any rule about
who may prepare, serve, or share food with whom creates a legible, constantly-
re-enacted test of in-group membership every single meal — `commensality` as a
permeability control on the social boundary, independent of whether the
specific food rule has any health payoff at all.

## Social consequence

Shared meals become a recurring, low-stakes-seeming but socially load-bearing
checkpoint: accepting or declining food, or being served differently, performs
group membership constantly and routinely rather than at rare formal moments.
Caste-linked commensality in South Asia makes this especially fine-grained,
regulating not just what is eaten but who may cook it and in what vessel, which
extends the boundary-marking function far past anything a simple
permitted/forbidden food list could achieve on its own.

## Regional specifics

The Middle Eastern kosher/halal model centers boundary-marking on the food
itself (which animals, which preparation). The South Asian caste model
centers it on the human relations around the food (who touched it, in what
sequence, using what vessel) more than on the food's identity, achieving a
finer-grained social technology from a similar starting need. East Asia's
comparative lack of codified sacred dietary law, substituting medical
(humoral/qi) and etiquette frameworks for boundary-marking around food, shows
the underlying need — marking group membership through eating — doesn't
require a purity-code solution at all; it's one solution among several.

## Contested

Functionalist explanations (pork prohibition as trichinosis avoidance, kosher
slaughter as proto-hygiene) are popular but contested against readings that
treat any disease-avoidance payoff as coincidental to codes whose actual
content and persistence are better explained by boundary-marking and identity
alone, with no need to invoke health reasoning at all. Model both as true
simultaneously rather than competing: purity codes carry a modest, real
disease-avoidance effect where the specific prohibition lines up with an
actual local risk, stacked with a full-strength commensality/boundary effect
that applies regardless of whether the health payoff is present — the two
effects don't need to share a common cause to both be real.

## Hook

Dietary/purity codes seed a `commensality` boundary-permeability effect on
every shared meal, plus a small, prohibition-specific disease-avoidance
modifier where the forbidden food actually correlates with a local zoonotic
risk — two independent effects riding on one rule set.
