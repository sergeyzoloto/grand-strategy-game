A family member, neighbor, or local herbalist with orally transmitted,
experientially accumulated knowledge treats the overwhelming majority of
illness episodes in any population at any point in this record, entirely
outside the codified, credentialed, or institutionally endowed channels
this domain otherwise concentrates on.

## Timing

Continuous and effectively undatable as a discrete origin point — the
practice long predates written records and persists throughout this
domain's entire timeframe alongside, rather than displaced by, every later
institutional development (codified medicine, hospitals, hospitals'
eventual clinical efficacy gains), remaining the dominant care channel by
sheer volume of illness episodes treated well into the 19th century in
most regions.

## Mechanism

Folk medicine accumulates and transmits practical knowledge — which
plants treat which symptoms, how to set a bone, how to manage a fever —
through direct apprenticeship and oral tradition rather than through
formal theory or institutional credentialing, which is exactly what
HLT-011's codified systems substitute a coherent theoretical framework
for: the folk channel has no comparable mechanism for building durable
social prestige from its knowledge, but also carries none of a codified
system's risk of theory-driven intervention (bloodletting, aggressive
purging) actively working against a genuinely effective accumulated
practice.

## Social consequence

Because folk medical knowledge typically concentrates in specific social
roles — often women, often a specific lineage or recognized local
practitioner — without formal credentialing, it sits structurally outside
whatever institutional medical authority exists in a given society at any
given time, low in `medical legitimacy` regardless of its actual clinical
record, a mirror of HLT-016's midwifery case generalized to the whole of
ordinary illness rather than childbirth specifically.

## Regional specifics

European folk-medical knowledge, concentrated in women's hands under the
"wise woman" or cunning-folk tradition, comes under unusually severe and
targeted legal and religious suppression during the early modern witch
persecutions specifically where it overlapped with or was mistaken for
suspected malefic magic — a direct, violent attack on this substrate not
comparably documented in the other major traditions this entry spans,
showing the folk channel's own survival wasn't simply assumed everywhere
but was actively contested and, in this one case, partially destroyed by
institutional and legal power rather than merely ignored by it.

## Contested

How much systematic overlap existed between folk remedies and genuinely
effective compounds (willow bark, various antimicrobial plant
preparations), versus how much folk practice was inert or actively
harmful, is disputed and highly remedy-specific, with modern
ethnobotanical research confirming real efficacy for some traditional
preparations while finding none for others superficially similar. Model
individual folk techniques as rolling their own independent, mostly
uncorrelated efficacy value — exactly the design-principle-3 treatment
HLT-011 already applies to elite codified medicine — rather than assuming
folk status itself predicts either effectiveness or its absence.

## Hook

A modifier: population-level health outcomes track this substrate's
coverage and quality far more than they track any codified system's own
prestige, since folk care is what most people actually receive regardless
of what formal medical authority exists in their society at all — the
same authority/efficacy split HLT-011 formalizes for elite medicine,
running here with the polarity often reversed.
