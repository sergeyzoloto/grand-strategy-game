Rising income and globalized, industrialized food supply chains shift a
population's diet toward more calorie-dense, processed, fat- and sugar-heavy
food, trading historical undernutrition risk for a new chronic-disease burden.

## Timing

Begins in the United States and Western Europe by the mid-20th century,
reaching East Asia and parts of the Middle East on a compressed timeline from
the 1970s-1980s, and remaining incomplete or double-burdened in large parts of
Africa and South Asia through the present.

## Mechanism

Industrialized food processing and global agricultural trade (see HLT-050's
older fermentation/processing lineage and its commerce-side supply chains)
sharply lower the price and increase the availability of calorie-dense
processed food relative to traditional whole-food diets, shifting the
binding constraint on population health from calorie sufficiency to calorie
excess and nutritional imbalance — the same cost-of-food-access lever that
once caused undernutrition now, run in the opposite direction, causes a
different chronic-disease burden.

## Social consequence

The health burden of the transition doesn't fall evenly even within a single
population: it typically hits lower-income groups hardest first (cheapest
calories are the most processed and calorie-dense), inverting the older
historical pattern where affluence reliably meant better nutrition — in a
completed nutrition transition, affluence increasingly buys access to
better, less processed food instead, while poverty is newly associated with
obesity rather than protecting against it.

## Regional specifics

East Asia and the Gulf states compress a transition that took the United
States and Europe most of a century into a single generation, riding
globalized food-supply infrastructure that simply didn't exist during the
original Western transition — the same endpoint reached much faster because
the enabling infrastructure was already built elsewhere and could be
imported wholesale. Much of Africa and South Asia instead show a genuine
double burden — undernutrition and diet-linked chronic disease coexisting
rather than one cleanly replacing the other — breaking the classic linear
transition model's assumption of a single ordered sequence.

## Hook

Falling food-access cost is the same lever in both directions: below a
threshold it drives undernutrition, and pushed past a different threshold by
cheap processed-calorie abundance it drives a new `toxic burden` chronic-
disease pattern instead — the same stat, opposite failure modes at opposite ends.
