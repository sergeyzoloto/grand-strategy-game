Three substances move from limited, often ritual or medicinal use into
mass-market commodities carrying real addiction and health cost, each time
following an expansion in supply and commercial promotion rather than a
spontaneous shift in demand.

## Timing

Tobacco's transformation from American ritual substance to global commodity
runs from 1500; the Qing opium crisis develops mainly from the mid-1700s
through the 19th century as British commercial supply scales up.

## Mechanism

In each case, a substance with a real physiological dependency mechanism
becomes newly available at mass-market scale and low price through a
commercial actor with a strong profit incentive to expand consumption well
past whatever level existing social or religious controls had kept it at —
the addictive property itself functions as a demand-side lock-in that makes
the market unusually resistant to price or moral-suasion-based limits once
established at scale.

## Social consequence

Mass addiction at the societal level externalizes much of its cost onto
households and public order rather than onto the seller, which is what
eventually motivates state intervention (Qing prohibition attempts,
later temperance and control movements elsewhere) — intervention that
recurringly fails or is fought militarily (the Opium Wars) when the supplying
power has more coercive capacity than the state trying to restrict
consumption.

## Regional specifics

Qing China's opium crisis is unusual among the three in being driven by
deliberate, sustained foreign commercial supply expansion into a market a
state was actively and repeatedly trying to restrict — a supply-side-forced
social epidemic rather than one that grew from internal demand alone.
Tobacco's rapid decoupling from its American ceremonial origin into a
value-neutral global commodity is close to total within a couple of
centuries. The Middle East's durable formal alcohol prohibition shows a
region successfully maintaining a genuinely different consumption baseline
long-term, rather than every region converging on the same substances given
enough contact time — coffee and tobacco absorb much of the social-substance
role alcohol plays elsewhere in that specific regional configuration.

## Hook

A commercially-supplied addictive substance accumulates `toxic burden` and
household-level social cost as a function of market penetration, and — once
established at mass-market scale — resists state suppression in proportion to
the supplying actor's relative coercive and commercial power, not just the
restricting state's willpower.
