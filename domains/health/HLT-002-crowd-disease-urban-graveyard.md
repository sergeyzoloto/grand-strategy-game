Cities kill faster than they can be born into, for most of urban history — net
population growth in premodern cities depends on continuous rural in-migration,
not on births exceeding deaths within the city itself.

## Timing

From the first Mesopotamian and Indus cities (~-3000) through to the sanitary
reforms of the 19th century, which are the first interventions to durably close
the gap in the cities that adopt them.

## Mechanism

Population density above a threshold sustains crowd diseases (measles,
smallpox, various enteric infections) that require a large susceptible pool to
stay endemic rather than burning out — a pool only a city, not a village,
provides. The result is an urban death rate that structurally exceeds the urban
birth rate: cities are, demographically, population sinks that only grow because
migrants keep arriving from the (healthier, lower-density) countryside. The cost
that moves is effectively a standing "immigration tax" every city must keep
paying just to hold its population steady, let alone grow.

## Social consequence

Urban life is, for most of this era, something people move to and often die
sooner in — a bargain made for opportunity, trade, or displacement from rural
land, not for health. This produces a permanent underclass of recent migrants
disproportionately exposed to the disease pool before any acquired immunity
catches up, and a demographic churn that keeps urban culture perpetually
replenished by newcomers rather than growing organically from within.

## Regional specifics

The Indus cities are a striking possible exception: comparable density to
Mesopotamian contemporaries, but paired with covered drains and near-universal
household water access, which — if the graveyard effect scales with pathogen
load rather than density alone — should have pushed their threshold later. Firm
mortality data doesn't survive to confirm this directly, so the case rests on
inference from infrastructure. The Americas show the cleanest natural
experiment: enormous pre-contact urban density (Tenochtitlan likely exceeded
most contemporary European cities) without the crowd-disease roster that makes
Old World cities lethal, because the animal-reservoir diseases themselves
weren't present — the graveyard effect only appears in the Americas after 1492,
imported wholesale rather than generated locally.

## Contested

Exactly when a given city crosses from net demographic sink to self-sustaining
depends on reconstructed birth and death rates that are rarely well-attested
before modern registration, so the "closing date" of the urban graveyard is
argued city-by-city and scholar-by-scholar rather than settled. Model this as a
threshold city-level function of population density against water/waste
infrastructure quality (see HLT-006), rather than a single Old-World-wide
calendar date — letting well-engineered cities cross the threshold earlier than
equally dense but less-engineered ones.

## Hook

Every settlement above a density threshold accrues a standing urban mortality
drag that can only be offset by in-migration or by later investment in
water/waste infrastructure — cities are a population deficit wearing a growth
mechanic's clothes until something pays that deficit down.
