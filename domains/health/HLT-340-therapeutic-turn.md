Psychological self-examination and professional emotional support shift, over
the second half of the 20th century, from a marginal or stigmatized practice
to a default, even expected, response to ordinary life difficulty — mainly in
the United States, with real but uneven diffusion elsewhere.

## Timing

Emerges as a mass phenomenon in the United States from roughly the 1950s
(building directly on psychoanalysis's earlier cultural diffusion, HLT-320),
reaching broad institutionalization (workplace counseling, self-help
publishing, daytime television) by the 1980s-1990s.

## Mechanism

As kin, congregation, and fixed community networks shrink or thin as the
default source of emotional support and moral guidance for a growing share of
the population (itself downstream of urbanization, mobility, and
secularization trends elsewhere in this record), a professionalized
market-based substitute expands to fill the gap — the underlying demand for
processing distress doesn't disappear, it relocates from an unpaid communal
channel to a paid professional and media one.

## Social consequence

Personal distress becomes something to be individually diagnosed, discussed,
and worked on with a professional or through mass-marketed self-help material,
rather than primarily interpreted through communal, familial, or religious
frameworks — a genuine privacy/individualism-linked habitus shift (design
principle 2) that also, not incidentally, commercializes a function that
previously ran largely outside the market.

## Regional specifics

Japan's later and more selective adoption layers therapeutic vocabulary onto
persistent existing frameworks (workplace and family obligation structures)
rather than displacing them, producing a hybrid rather than a straightforward
import of the American pattern. Much of Africa continues routing the
underlying need for support through community, extended family, and religious
institutions that never thinned out to the same degree the American pattern
presumes, making the "therapeutic turn" as a mass institutional phenomenon
much less visible there without implying the underlying human need is any
less met.

## Hook

As communal support networks thin, a market-based therapeutic-support sector
expands to fill the gap, raising individual `privacy`/introspection at the
cost of commercializing a function that previously ran outside the market
entirely — a substitution, not a net-new capability.
