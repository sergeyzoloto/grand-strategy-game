Sustained caloric deprivation directly causes death, but the larger share
of famine mortality historically comes from starvation's suppression of
immune function cascading into ordinary infectious disease becoming
lethal in a population it wouldn't otherwise kill — a compounding
mechanism causally distinct from any single pathogen's own transmission
dynamics.

## Timing

Famine mortality is documented from the earliest written records
(Egyptian and Mesopotamian famine accounts by roughly -1800) and recurs
throughout this record's entire span; the 1770 and 1943 Bengal famines
under British colonial administration are among the best-documented cases
for isolating policy failure's specific contribution to total mortality,
with the 1943 case closing this entry's window.

## Mechanism

A sustained caloric shortfall directly kills through prolonged
starvation, but a substantially larger share of total famine mortality
historically comes through a second channel: malnutrition suppresses
immune function broadly, converting ordinary endemic infections
(dysentery, typhus, measles) that a well-fed population survives
routinely into a mass-mortality event in a starving one — the same
pathogens, the same population, a radically different outcome once
caloric intake crosses a threshold, making famine mortality a compounding
rather than a single-cause mechanic.

## Social consequence

Famine mortality falls hardest on exactly the population with the least
capacity to weather it — children, the elderly, and the already
malnourished — while a receiving state's actual administrative response
(grain reserves released or withheld, transport capacity mobilized or
not, continued export despite local shortage) determines how much of the
underlying harvest shortfall actually converts into deaths, meaning
total mortality is never a fixed function of crop failure alone.

## Regional specifics

The Bengal famines occur under a colonial administrative structure with
real logistical capacity to move grain and intervene, making specific
policy choices (continued grain export during the 1943 famine
prominently) as significant to eventual mortality as the underlying
harvest shortfall itself — a clear case where governance failure, not
just crop failure, is doing real causal work. Sub-Saharan Africa's
comparatively thinner documentary record for premodern famine mortality
plausibly reflects both more drought-resilient diversified subsistence
strategies in some regions and weaker surviving administrative records in
others, rather than a genuine absence of the underlying risk.

## Contested

The exact split between direct starvation mortality and starvation-
amplified infectious mortality is disputed and varies enormously by
famine, further complicated by how much total mortality reflects the
harvest shortfall itself versus the receiving state's distribution and
relief-policy choices. Model total mortality as the sum of a direct-
starvation channel and a larger, more variable infectious-amplification
channel, with state capacity and policy applied as a separate multiplier
on both rather than folded into the harvest-shortfall number alone.

## Hook

An event: a food-supply collapse converts a population's ordinary,
survivable background disease load into a mass-mortality event by
suppressing immunological resistance rather than introducing anything
new — the same population, the same pathogens, a radically different
outcome once caloric intake crosses a threshold, with a receiving state's
own administrative capacity and choices acting as a real, trackable
multiplier on the final death toll.
