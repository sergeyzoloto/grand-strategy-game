This entry covers only the disease-management function of burial practice —
who may touch a corpse, how fast, and by what method — not burial as religious
institution, which is a REL entry in its own right.

## Timing

Deliberate corpse treatment predates written history by tens of thousands of
years; the specific practices compared here (cremation, exposure, and
restricted-contact inhumation) are attested from roughly -1500 onward in the
traditions with enough material or textual record to date confidently.

## Mechanism

A corpse is a spike in local pathogen load at exactly the moment survivors are
most likely to be physically close to it (washing, mourning, handling). Burial
practice functions, whatever its stated purpose, as a control on who contacts
the body, how quickly it's removed from the living space, and by what method it
is neutralized — burial (isolates and slow-releases), cremation (immediate,
near-total neutralization at fuel cost), or exposure (delegates neutralization
to scavengers, at the cost of no control over timing or location).

## Social consequence

Rules about who may or must handle the dead — specific kin, a dedicated
caste or guild, ritual specialists — concentrate whatever residual exposure
risk remains onto a defined subset of the community rather than spreading it
across everyone who grieves, which is also, not incidentally, usually the same
group whose status the role marks as distinct (higher or lower, depending on
the tradition).

## Regional specifics

Cremation traditions (South Asia from ~-1500) trade a fuel-supply constraint
for near-complete removal of the corpse-reservoir problem. Exposure traditions
(Zoroastrian towers of silence, Central Asian and some circumpolar practices)
minimize direct human handling at the point of highest infectivity by
delegating the work to scavengers, at the cost of surrendering control over
where and how fast decomposition happens. Low-density foraging and pastoralist
societies across Africa and elsewhere often formalize very little here at all
— not neglect, but a reflection of population density too low for corpse-borne
transmission to be a meaningful recurring threat worth building fixed
infrastructure or specialist roles around.

## Hook

Burial method sets a one-time toxic-burden/exposure cost at time of death,
sized differently by method (cremation lowest, delayed inhumation highest,
exposure variable) — a quiet modifier on local disease dynamics that most
players will never consciously notice they're managing.
