Four major literate traditions — Greek/Galenic, Ayurvedic, Chinese, and later
Islamic medicine — each independently or semi-independently build a
comprehensive theory of the body, disease, and treatment, and each acquires
enormous social authority almost entirely uncoupled from how often its
treatments actually worked.

## Timing

Greek humoral medicine and Ayurveda systematize in roughly the same centuries
(~-400), Chinese medical theory shortly after (~-200), and Islamic medicine
from ~900 onward, substantially building on and extending the Greek corpus
rather than starting fresh.

## Mechanism

Each system bundles a coherent, internally-consistent theory of disease
causation with a matching set of diagnostics and interventions, which is what
lets it function as a stable social institution (predictable, teachable,
defensible) regardless of clinical outcomes. The cost that moves is not
disease itself but legitimacy: once a theory is coherent and prestigious
enough, it becomes the default reference point patients and rulers turn to,
crowding out competing folk or empirical practices whether or not those
alternatives actually perform better.

## Social consequence

Trained physicians in each tradition acquire real social status, income, and
often state patronage (court physicians, hospital appointments under Islamic
medicine especially) that tracks their command of the theoretical corpus and
its rhetoric, not their patients' survival rates — authority and efficacy
run on separate tracks for the entire period this entry covers, with almost no
mechanism available to the societies themselves to notice the gap.

## Regional specifics

Greek, Ayurvedic, and Chinese systems arrive at structurally similar
humoral/elemental theoretical architectures (three or four or five basic
qualities in dynamic balance) with no plausible contact between the Greek and
Chinese cases, suggesting this kind of theory is a close-to-inevitable way for
a literate society to systematize medicine, not a specific cultural accident.
Islamic medicine's role as an extending and transmitting bridge — absorbing
Greek theory, adding substantial original clinical and pharmacological
material, and passing the combined corpus back into Europe — makes it the
hinge of the entire Old World medical-authority network for centuries.
African traditions carrying comparably deep herbal/diagnostic knowledge without
comparable textual codification illustrates that codification, not knowledge
itself, is the variable driving durability and cross-regional influence here.

## Contested

It's genuinely disputed how much of any system's actual therapeutic effect was
real versus purely the placebo/authority effect of a confident, prestigious
practitioner — some individual interventions across all four traditions
plausibly had real efficacy (specific herbal compounds, wound hygiene, dietary
moderation), while signature theory-driven interventions (bloodletting,
aggressive purging) plausibly did net harm on average. Model `medical
legitimacy` and `medical efficacy` as fully independent stats per system, with
individual interventions rolling their own small, mostly-uncorrelated efficacy
value rather than inheriting the parent system's prestige — letting a
high-authority system contain both genuinely useful and actively harmful
components at once.

## Hook

Codified medical systems grant high, durable `medical legitimacy` essentially
decoupled from `medical efficacy`, which stays low almost everywhere until a
much later entry (antisepsis, germ theory) finally starts pulling efficacy up
independently of authority.
