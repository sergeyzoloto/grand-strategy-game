Once a population expects prices to keep rising, that expectation itself
accelerates the rise — workers and sellers price in future inflation now,
which pushes current prices up faster, which validates and intensifies the
expectation, a feedback loop that can spiral far beyond whatever fiscal
pressure originally triggered it.

## Timing

Weimar Germany's hyperinflation (1921-1923) is the paradigm early case,
driven by war-reparations obligations and large-scale money creation to
cover them; comparable spirals recur wherever a government finances a large
fiscal deficit through money creation faster than the real economy can
absorb it, as in Zimbabwe from 2007.

## Mechanism

Once expected inflation exceeds a threshold, rational economic actors
stop holding the depreciating currency at all, spending it immediately or
converting to foreign currency or barter, which collapses the currency's
velocity-driven value even faster than the underlying money-supply growth
alone would — the spiral's self-reinforcing character (rising `price level`
driving expectations driving further price rises) is what separates it from
ordinary elevated inflation, and once expectations decouple this severely,
stopping the spiral typically requires a drastic, credible regime change
(a new currency, external monetary anchor) rather than a marginal policy
adjustment.

## Social consequence

Hyperinflation wipes out the real value of fixed-income savings, pensions,
and wage contracts faster than institutions can renegotiate them, producing
an arbitrary and often brutal redistribution — debtors whose obligations are
fixed in the collapsing currency benefit enormously while creditors and
anyone holding cash savings lose almost everything, a wealth transfer with
no relationship to merit or planning on either side.

## Regional specifics

Zimbabwe's hyperinflation reaches one of the highest monthly rates ever
recorded, ultimately forcing complete abandonment of the domestic currency
for foreign alternatives — an extreme case of the spiral's self-reinforcing
logic running to its natural conclusion (total currency abandonment) rather
than being arrested partway. Postwar Japan's comparatively controlled
inflation despite real fiscal pressure shows a spiral isn't an automatic
consequence of high inflation risk — sufficient policy coordination and,
critically, external demand and support can keep expectations from
decoupling into a self-reinforcing spiral in the first place.

## Hook

Once inflation expectations decouple from underlying fiscal fundamentals, the
resulting spiral in `price level` becomes self-reinforcing and typically
requires a drastic credibility-restoring regime change to stop rather than
a marginal policy fix — and the same underlying fiscal pressure can either
spiral (Weimar, Zimbabwe) or stay controlled (postwar Japan) depending
entirely on whether that decoupling actually occurs.
