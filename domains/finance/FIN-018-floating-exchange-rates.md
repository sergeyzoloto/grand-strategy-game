The 1971 end of dollar-gold convertibility (the "Nixon Shock") collapses
the Bretton Woods fixed-rate system (FIN-011) built after World War Two,
letting major currencies' relative value float according to market supply
and demand rather than being fixed to gold or to each other by international
agreement.

## Timing

The Bretton Woods system operates from 1944 (FIN-011) until the United
States suspends dollar-gold convertibility in 1971; major currencies
transition to floating rates through the mid-1970s, with the regime
becoming the dominant global default by the early 2000s even as some
states continue choosing fixed or managed alternatives.

## Mechanism

A floating rate lets a currency's value adjust continuously to reflect
trade balances, interest-rate differentials, and market sentiment, restoring
the independent domestic monetary policy the gold standard (FIN-002) and
Bretton Woods both traded away for fixed-rate predictability — this removes
the credibility anchor a fixed rate provides, so a floating currency's value
depends entirely on ongoing market confidence in the issuing state's policy,
directly exposing it to the same panic dynamics FIN-010 describes if that
confidence collapses suddenly.

## Social consequence

Floating rates reintroduce exchange-rate risk into everyday international
trade and investment that fixed regimes had suppressed, spurring the growth
of currency-hedging instruments (a direct extension of FIN-015's derivative
mechanism into currency risk specifically) as businesses and investors seek
to manage a volatility fixed rates previously handled for them by design.

## Regional specifics

Gulf oil states' deliberate dollar-peg choice, trading away independent
monetary policy specifically to keep currency stability with the market
their oil revenue is denominated in, shows fixing rather than floating
remains a live rational choice for particular economic structures, not
merely a holdover practice. Several East Asian economies' continued managed
or pegged exchange rates into the 1990s directly set up the vulnerability
the 1997 crisis (FIN-010) exposes once currency speculators test whether
those pegs can actually be defended under capital-flight pressure.

## Hook

Floating rates restore independent domestic monetary policy at the cost of
the fixed-rate credibility anchor (FIN-002, FIN-011) — a real tradeoff some
economies (Gulf oil states) still deliberately decline in favor of pegging,
and one whose abandonment (managed pegs under speculative pressure) is
exactly the mechanism behind currency crises like 1997's (FIN-010).
