An asset's price detaches from any plausible estimate of its underlying
future earnings or utility, sustained purely by the expectation that
someone else will pay even more for it later — a self-reinforcing dynamic
that continues exactly until new buyers stop appearing.

## Timing

Dutch tulip mania (peaking 1636-1637) is the earliest well-documented case;
the South Sea Bubble (1720, directly involving the joint-stock instrument
of FIN-006) and Britain's railway mania (1840s, see COM-024) recur across
the following two centuries, with Japan's real estate and equity bubble
(peaking around 1989-1990) showing the pattern persisting in a fully modern
financial system.

## Mechanism

A bubble requires a genuine, not-unreasonable growth story (a new
commodity, a new company structure, a transformative new technology like
railways) to get started, but sustains itself past any level that story
can actually justify through a self-reinforcing feedback loop: rising
prices attract new buyers, whose buying pushes prices higher still,
validating the original buyers' decision and drawing in more — the dynamic
continues exactly as long as new buyers keep entering, and reverses
catastrophically once that inflow stops or reverses, since the price was
never supported by anything except the expectation of further buying.

## Social consequence

Bubbles redistribute wealth dramatically and somewhat arbitrarily between
whoever exits before the peak and whoever is still holding the asset when
it collapses, with the timing of that exit only weakly correlated with any
participant's actual skill or information — a bubble's collapse frequently
falls hardest on the last, often least-sophisticated wave of buyers drawn
in by the same rising prices that made the earlier, more knowledgeable
participants their profit.

## Regional specifics

Japan's late-1980s bubble developing within one of the world's most
technologically sophisticated financial systems is a clean demonstration
that market maturity doesn't inoculate against collective mispricing — the
same self-reinforcing herd dynamic operates regardless of how advanced the
surrounding financial infrastructure is. The comparative scarcity of
documented equivalent bubbles in Middle Eastern markets of the same eras
plausibly reflects both smaller public equity market scale and Islamic
finance's structural constraints on purely speculative leveraged
positions, rather than any lesser tendency toward optimism or herd behavior
generally.

## Contested

Whether bubbles reflect rational pricing of a real, if ultimately
unrealized, growth possibility, or genuine collective irrationality
departing from any defensible valuation logic, is disputed between
efficient-market and behavioral-finance perspectives. Model bubble
formation as requiring both a genuinely plausible growth narrative and a
separate, self-reinforcing herd dynamic that pushes valuation well past
what even optimistic realization of that narrative would support — treating
neither ingredient as sufficient alone.

## Hook

A genuine growth narrative plus a self-reinforcing herd-buying dynamic
inflates an asset's price far past its underlying earnings potential, in a
process that continues exactly as long as new buyers keep entering and
collapses catastrophically the moment that inflow reverses — a pattern
recurring identically across four centuries regardless of the surrounding
financial system's sophistication.
