The labor-organization and mechanization history of industrialization
belongs to `COM-100` and `COM-170`; this entry owns a distinct problem those
don't cover — mechanized factory production requires capital investment at
a scale and time-horizon no craft workshop or manufactory ever needed, and
that capital has to come from somewhere.

## Timing

Industrial-scale capital requirements grow sharply from the 1830s onward as
factory mechanization (COM-170) spreads, met through varying financing
models across major industrializing economies through the outbreak of
World War One in 1914.

## Mechanism

Building and equipping a mechanized factory requires large upfront capital
committed well before any revenue arrives, a fundamentally different
financing problem than a craft workshop's or manufactory's much smaller,
faster-recouped capital needs — this gap gets filled through some
combination of joint-stock equity (FIN-070), bank lending against future
output, or direct state investment, and which combination a given economy
relies on shapes who ends up controlling the resulting industrial capacity
as much as the underlying technology itself does.

## Social consequence

Where industrial finance flows through private banks and equity markets,
industrial capacity concentrates in the hands of whoever can access that
capital — often an existing propertied or merchant class — while where it
flows through direct state investment, the state itself becomes a direct
industrial owner and operator, with correspondingly different implications
for who captures the resulting profits and who bears the risk of failed
ventures.

## Regional specifics

Meiji Japan's state-led financing model, building model factories directly
with state capital before transferring them to favored private conglomerates,
produces a fundamentally different industrial ownership structure (the
zaibatsu system) than Britain's more market- and bank-financed path — the
same underlying mechanization technology, arrived at through structurally
different capital-formation routes with lasting consequences for each
economy's industrial power structure. Colonial India's constrained capital
access, with available investment channeled toward British-owned enterprise
and railway financing rather than Indian-controlled industry, compounds the
trade-policy constraints already covered in this record's commerce domain
into an additional, specifically financial barrier to indigenous industrial
development.

## Hook

How industrial-scale capital actually gets raised — private equity and bank
lending versus direct state investment — determines who ends up owning and
controlling the resulting industrial capacity as much as the underlying
mechanization technology (COM-170) does, with the same technology producing
very different ownership structures (Japanese zaibatsu versus British
private industry) purely as a function of the financing path taken.
