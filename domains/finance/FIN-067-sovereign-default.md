A state can borrow, and it can refuse to repay. Sovereign lending is the only major
credit relationship with no enforcement mechanism: a creditor cannot foreclose on a
state, seize its territory, or compel a court judgment against it. Everything about
sovereign debt follows from that, including why lending happens anyway.

## Timing

From Philip II's suspension of payments in 1557 and the serial defaults of Habsburg
Spain and Bourbon France, through the Latin American defaults of the 1820s, the
defaults and foreign debt administrations of Tunisia, the Ottoman Empire, Egypt and
Greece from 1869 to 1898, gunboat collection in Venezuela in 1902 and American
customs receiverships, the defaults of the 1930s, the Soviet repudiation of 1918, the
Latin American debt crisis from 1982, Russia in 1998, Argentina in 2001, the Greek
restructuring of 2012 and Zambia in 2020.

## Mechanism

**The boundary with FIN-110.** FIN-110 carries how states convert future revenue into
tradable bonds, and how default risk is priced into them. This entry carries default
itself: what happens when a state stops paying, why it can, and why creditors lend to
it knowing it can.

**No enforcement.** Ordinary credit (FIN-030) rests on a court above the borrower. A
private borrower who does not pay loses collateral through a court. A sovereign is its own court, and foreign courts long treated it as immune. Its
territory cannot be foreclosed and its assets at home cannot be seized. A sovereign
repays because repaying is worth more to it than defaulting, and three things
substitute for collateral:

- **Reputation and exclusion.** A defaulter pays more to borrow, or cannot borrow at
  all, for years afterwards. `default history` is the lenders' memory, and
  `sovereign credit capacity` falls with it. Lenders distinguish excusable defaults,
  in war or a commodity bust, from wilful ones.
- **Seizure of a revenue stream.** A state's territory cannot be taken, but a customs
  house can. Creditors and their governments took over identifiable revenues: the
  Ottoman Public Debt Administration collected salt and tobacco taxes, the Caisse de la
  Dette ran Egypt's finances, American officials ran Dominican customs, and foreign
  inspectors administered China's customs and salt.
- **Force and law.** Gunboats blockaded Venezuela in 1902-03, and debt administration
  became occupation in Egypt in 1882 and Tunisia in 1881. After the Second World War
  force gave way to law. The United States' Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976
  and Britain's State Immunity Act of 1978 let creditors sue over commercial debts, and
  holdout funds such as Elliott Associates against Peru in 2000 and NML Capital against
  Argentina in 2012 used court injunctions to block payments to other creditors. The
  premise of no enforcement weakened for debt issued under foreign law.

**Serial default.** Because default is survivable, some states default again and
again. Reinhart and Rogoff (This Time Is Different, 2009) count thirteen external
defaults for Spain, ten for Venezuela and nine for Ecuador. A state that has
defaulted borrows on worse terms, carries less debt before it defaults again, and
tends to default in the same circumstances — war, a commodity bust, a rise in world
interest rates. For early modern Spain, default was a known feature of the contract:
suspension when the silver fleets failed, a negotiated conversion, and new lending
(COM-140). The loans paid armies abroad in the coin that mercantilist policy tried
to accumulate (FIN-065), so default and the bullion premise answered the same fiscal
problem.

**Domestic and external default are different.** A state that owes in its own currency
can pay by printing money, and inflation then transfers value from creditors to the
state without a formal default. Weimar Germany erased its domestic debt this way, and
FIN-220 carries the spiral that can follow. A state that owes in a foreign currency
has no such escape. It must earn or borrow the currency it owes, and when its own
currency falls, its debt rises. `foreign-currency debt share` is the trap:

- **Latin America in 1982** had borrowed dollars at floating rates, and the rise in
  American interest rates multiplied what it owed.
- **Argentina in 2001** had pegged its peso to the dollar and borrowed in dollars, so
  devaluation made the debt unpayable.
- **Greece in 2012** owed euros it could not print, which made its debt a foreign
  currency debt in effect.

Eichengreen and Hausmann called the inability of most developing states to borrow
abroad in their own currency "original sin". It removes the inflation escape and
converts every currency crisis into a debt crisis.

**Resource booms and interest-rate cycles set the timing.** Defaults cluster. Lenders
pour money into borrowers when commodity prices are high and world interest rates are
low, and pull it out together when either turns. Peru borrowed against guano and
defaulted in 1876; Mexico borrowed against oil and defaulted in 1982. FIN-205 carries
the volatility that makes resource states pro-cyclical borrowers.

**Receivership and adjustment.** The creditor's answer to no enforcement changed shape
but not purpose. In the nineteenth century creditors took over revenue: the foreign
debt administrations of the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, Tunisia and Greece, and the
customs receiverships of the Caribbean, all instruments of the coerced diplomacy
DIP-140 and DIP-150 carry for East Asia and the Ottoman capitulations. In the late
twentieth century IMF programmes made new lending conditional on austerity and
structural reform (FIN-260). The foreign official in the customs house and the IMF
mission in the finance ministry answer the same question: how to secure repayment from
a borrower no court can bind. Rating agencies (FIN-210) made default risk publicly
legible in between.

**Odious debt has never held.** The doctrine that a successor regime need not repay
debts a despot borrowed against the people's interest was formulated by Sack in 1927.
It draws on the United States' refusal in 1898 to let Cuba assume the debts Spain had
charged to it, and the Soviet government repudiated all tsarist debts in 1918. But no
international court has applied it, and states that invoked it paid in exclusion.
Iraq's debts after 2003 were reduced by negotiation through the Paris Club, not by the
doctrine, and Ecuador's 2008 default on bonds it called illegitimate was treated by
markets as an ordinary default. Legitimacy has not proved to be a defence.

## Social consequence

At human scale default redistributes a state's losses:

- **A Genoese banker in 1575.** They see short-term loans converted into long-term juros
  at lower interest, and lend again because the crown's business is too large to leave.
- **A French rentier in 1797.** They lose two thirds of the capital of their government
  bonds by decree.
- **An Ottoman tobacco farmer after 1883.** They sell their crop under a monopoly run for
  foreign bondholders by a debt administration with its own inspectors.
- **An Egyptian peasant in the 1870s.** They pay taxes collected in advance to meet the
  khedive's coupons.
- **A Mexican worker after 1982.** They watch real wages fall under austerity while the
  government pays its banks.
- **An Argentine saver in December 2001.** They find their dollar deposits frozen by the
  corralito and then converted into devalued pesos.

The costs of default and of avoiding it both fall on taxpayers and wage earners. The
choice between them is a choice about which domestic group pays: foreign bondholders
through default, or the population through austerity to keep paying. Default
administrations also turned creditors into rulers, which made debt a cause of
nationalist revolt, from Urabi in Egypt to the protests against IMF programmes.

## Regional specifics

Every region with borrowing states shows the mechanism, and the forms differ:

- **Europe.** Habsburg Spain supplies serial default as contract renegotiation, and
  Bourbon France repeated repudiation. Greece in 2012 is the modern foreign-currency
  trap inside a currency union.
- **The Middle East.** The Ottoman Public Debt Administration and Egypt's Dual Control
  are the fullest cases of creditor administration, with Egypt showing where it led.
- **The Americas.** Venezuela supplies gunboat collection and the Drago doctrine against
  it, the Caribbean the American customs receivership, and Mexico and Argentina the
  modern crises and holdout litigation.
- **Africa.** Tunisia shows receivership turning into protectorate. Late-twentieth-century
  debt relief replaced default with conditional write-down.
- **East Asia.** Qing China's revenues were administered by foreigners without a formal
  default, while Meiji Japan built credit by never defaulting.
- **South Asia.** India in 1991 pledged its gold to avoid a first default.

The absence is Tokugawa Japan, which borrowed nothing abroad and met fiscal crises
by debasement, forced loans and domestic repudiation of merchant debts.

## Contested

Why sovereigns repay is disputed:

- **Reputation.** Eaton and Gersovitz argued exclusion from future credit suffices, and
  Tomz found lenders forgive excusable defaults.
- **Sanctions.** Bulow and Rogoff argued reputation alone cannot sustain lending;
  Mitchener and Weidenmier found gunboats and receiverships raised repayment.
- **Contingent contracts.** Drelichman and Voth read early modern default as an agreed
  renegotiation clause.

Model repayment as the sum of reputation, sanction capacity and need for future credit.

## Hook

**No enforcement.** A sovereign repays only while repaying is worth more than
defaulting. Default is always an available action.

- **Reputation.** Default raises `default history`, which lowers `sovereign credit
  capacity` for years, less for excusable defaults in war or a commodity bust.
- **Sanctions.** A stronger creditor state can seize a revenue stream (receivership),
  use force, or later litigate against debt issued under its law.

**Domestic versus foreign currency.** Debt in the state's own currency can be inflated
away (FIN-220). A high `foreign-currency debt share` removes that escape, so a currency
fall becomes a debt crisis.

**Clustering.** Defaults cluster when commodity prices fall and world interest rates
rise, hitting states with high `revenue volatility` first.

**Creditor administration.** Receivership and conditional lending transfer fiscal
control to creditors and generate nationalist backlash.

**Odious debt.** A successor regime can refuse a despot's debt but should expect to be
treated as an ordinary defaulter.
