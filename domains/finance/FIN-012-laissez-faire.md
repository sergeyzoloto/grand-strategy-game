The systematic philosophical argument for minimal state intervention in
markets is PHI's territory; this entry owns its actual adoption as state
economic policy and what that adoption did to real economies once
implemented.

## Timing

Adam Smith's 1776 argument provides the doctrine's foundational case;
laissez-faire policy reaches its fullest practical implementation across
several major economies through the 1800s, receding sharply as a dominant
policy consensus after the Great Depression (FIN-016) discredits its
crisis-response adequacy by 1930.

## Mechanism

Laissez-faire policy removes tariff protection (COM-018), industry
subsidy, and most direct state economic direction, betting that markets
left alone allocate capital and labor more efficiently than state planning
would — this bet plausibly pays off well for an economy already competitive
at the technological frontier (which benefits from open competition and
trade access) while plausibly disadvantaging an economy trying to build
new industrial capacity against already-established foreign competitors,
who can simply undercut a nascent domestic industry before it reaches
competitive scale.

## Social consequence

Removing state economic direction shifts risk and reward onto private
capital and market outcomes directly, producing faster capital reallocation
toward profitable uses but also less buffering against market volatility
for workers and smaller producers who have no equivalent access to
diversification or exit that larger capital holders do — the doctrine's
distributional effects, not just its aggregate growth effects, are part of
why it remains politically contested wherever it's actually implemented.

## Regional specifics

Meiji Japan's explicit rejection of laissez-faire in favor of direct state
industrial sponsorship is a deliberate alternative strategy, not an
immature step toward an eventual liberal model — a choice that fits the
broader pattern of Japan selectively adopting or rejecting specific Western
institutional models based on its own strategic judgment, seen repeatedly
elsewhere in this record. The German historical school's explicit internal
European argument against laissez-faire for developing economies shows the
doctrine was contested from within Europe itself, not merely resisted by
economies outside the tradition that produced it.

## Contested

Whether laissez-faire maximized aggregate welfare or primarily benefited
already-advanced economies at developing economies' expense is disputed
between classical/neoclassical and historical-school/development-economics
traditions. Model the policy's growth effect as conditional on an economy's
existing competitive position relative to others — positive for an
established frontier economy, plausibly negative for one trying to catch
up against entrenched foreign competition — rather than as a fixed-sign
effect independent of starting conditions.

## Hook

Laissez-faire's real-world payoff is conditional on an economy's starting
competitive position, not fixed — removing tariff protection (COM-018) and
state direction rewards an already-frontier economy while plausibly
locking out development for one still trying to reach competitive scale,
which is exactly the split between Britain's adoption and Japan's and
Germany's rejection of the same doctrine in the same era.
