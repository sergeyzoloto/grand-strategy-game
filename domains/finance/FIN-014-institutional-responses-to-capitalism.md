The systematic theoretical argument against capitalism (Marx and the
broader tradition building on or contesting his work) is PHI's territory;
this entry owns what happened when states actually tried to build
institutions on top of that critique — labor law, welfare provision, and
full command economies alike.

## Timing

Organized labor and socialist political movements gain institutional
traction from the mid-1800s (the term "communism" entering wide use around
1848); comprehensive command-economy implementation follows the 1917
Russian Revolution and 1949 Chinese Revolution, with most such systems
either collapsing or undergoing major market-oriented reform by 1991.

## Mechanism

Critique-driven institutional responses to capitalism span a wide range of
actual implementation, from moderate reform (labor protection law, the
welfare state, progressive taxation layered onto an otherwise market
economy) to comprehensive replacement (state ownership of the means of
production, central economic planning replacing price signals entirely) —
the moderate versions generally persist and integrate into otherwise
market-based systems, while comprehensive command economies face a much
harder information problem, since central planners must somehow substitute
administrative calculation for the decentralized price-signal coordination
a market economy uses instead.

## Social consequence

Even where a comprehensive command economy is eventually judged to have
underperformed market alternatives on growth and consumer-goods
availability, the underlying critique's more moderate institutional
descendants — collective bargaining rights, unemployment insurance, minimum
labor standards — persist broadly across market economies that never
adopted central planning at all, showing the critique's institutional
influence and its most extreme implementation's ultimate track record are
separable outcomes.

## Regional specifics

China's post-1978 reform, retaining one-party political control while
substantially reintroducing market mechanisms, shows a critique-driven
system can revise its own economic institutions extensively without
abandoning the political framework built to implement the original
critique — a case complicating any simple "command economy succeeds or
fails wholesale" narrative. Post-independence African state-socialist
experiments tie critique-driven institutional change explicitly to
anti-colonial politics, a combined ideological framing distinct from either
the European labor-movement or the East Asian revolutionary cases.

## Contested

Whether command economies failed mainly due to external pressure and
specific implementation choices, or due to inherent, unavoidable
information and incentive problems in central planning itself, is
unresolved in comparative economic systems scholarship. Model command-economy
performance as a function of specific institutional design choices
(how much market mechanism is retained, information and incentive
structures) rather than assuming either that central planning is
categorically doomed or that better implementation would have fully
resolved its problems.

## Hook

Critique-driven institutional responses to capitalism range from moderate
reform that persists broadly across market economies to comprehensive
command-economy replacement that faces a much harder information-
coordination problem — and, per the Chinese case, a state can revise how
far along that range it sits without abandoning the political system built
around the original critique.
