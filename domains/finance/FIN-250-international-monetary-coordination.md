Once multiple states each issue their own paper currency (FIN-050), a further coordination problem emerges that no single state's own monetary policy can solve alone: without some agreed anchor, there is no natural way to value one state's currency against another's for trade and lending purposes.

## Timing

The Bretton Woods conference (1944) establishes a system pegging major currencies to the US dollar, which is itself pegged to gold, alongside the International Monetary Fund to oversee and support the arrangement; the system operates from 1946 until the United States suspends dollar-gold convertibility in 1971 (the "Nixon Shock," FIN-270), ending the fixed-rate coordination framework it established.

## Mechanism

Bretton Woods solves the multi-currency coordination problem by designating a single reference currency (the US dollar) as the anchor every other participating currency pegs to at a fixed rate, with the dollar itself remaining convertible to gold — this gives every participating state's currency a stable, predictable value relative to every other participating currency without requiring direct negotiation between each pair of states, at the cost of tying the entire system's stability to one country's own monetary discipline and gold reserves.

## Social consequence

Because the system concentrates so much structural importance in a single anchor currency and the state issuing it, that state gains an unusual, largely non-reciprocal influence over global monetary conditions — other states' own domestic monetary policy has to account for dollar-denominated trade and reserve holdings in a way the reverse isn't true, a systemic asymmetry that persists in modified form well past the system's own formal 1971 end.

## Regional specifics

Gulf oil-exporting states, not yet major independent actors in international monetary diplomacy when Bretton Woods was founded, become significant influences on the system only later, once oil revenue and dollar-pegging decisions (FIN-270) give their reserve holdings genuine systemic weight — a region's monetary influence arriving well after the system's founding architecture was already fixed by other states entirely.

## Hook

A civic mechanic: pegging every participating currency to one anchor currency solves the multi-state coordination problem FIN-050's paper money creates without requiring pairwise negotiation, at the cost of concentrating outsized, largely non-reciprocal systemic influence in whichever state issues the anchor — a concentration whose eventual unwinding (FIN-270) restores every state's independent policy at the cost of the stability the anchor provided.
