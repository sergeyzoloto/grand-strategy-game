A jurisdiction deliberately offers low or zero taxation and strong financial
confidentiality specifically to attract capital and corporate registration
from elsewhere — directly inverting the cadastre-and-tax legibility (FIN-020)
this whole domain otherwise builds on, by making assets deliberately harder
for any other jurisdiction to see or tax.

## Timing

Switzerland's 1934 banking secrecy law is an early formalization of the
model; the offshore-finance sector expands sharply from the 1950s-1960s
Caribbean and Channel Islands centers, with additional major hubs (Hong
Kong, Singapore) developing from the 1970s, continuing to grow through the
2000s despite periodic international transparency and information-exchange
initiatives.

## Mechanism

A small jurisdiction with limited domestic tax base has little to lose and
much to gain by offering near-zero taxation and strict confidentiality to
attract foreign capital and corporate registration — the haven captures
registration fees and a local financial-services industry, while the
origin country whose capital or corporate profits relocate there loses the
tax revenue and legibility (FIN-020) it would otherwise have had over that
same wealth, a direct transfer of fiscal capacity from large economies to
small ones structured specifically to attract it.

## Social consequence

Offshore haven use concentrates disproportionately among wealthy
individuals and large corporations with the resources to structure
international holdings, meaning the domestic tax burden in origin countries
shifts correspondingly more heavily onto less mobile forms of income and
wealth (ordinary wages, real property) that can't as easily relocate — a
redistribution of tax incidence achieved entirely through jurisdictional
arbitrage rather than through any explicit domestic tax policy change.

## Regional specifics

Hong Kong and Singapore's development as major Asian offshore centers shows
the haven strategy generalizes well beyond its European and Caribbean
origins to any sufficiently stable, well-governed small jurisdiction willing
to pursue it — the model is a replicable strategy, not a specifically
Western institutional inheritance. African capital flight into foreign
havens is a particularly stark version of this entry's cost mechanism,
since the diverted revenue represents domestic investment and public
service capacity the origin population never receives, compounding
existing resource constraints in exactly the economies least able to
absorb the loss.

## Hook

A tax haven inverts FIN-020's foundational legibility mechanic by design —
deliberately making capital harder for other jurisdictions to see and tax
— capturing registration and financial-services revenue for the haven while
shifting tax incidence in origin countries onto whatever wealth and income
can't as easily relocate, a strategy any small, stable jurisdiction can
adopt regardless of region.
