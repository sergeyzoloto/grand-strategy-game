A paper claim substituting for physical specie in cross-city settlement — the private-sector precursor to and long-running parallel of FIN-011's later state-issued paper money, and the specific instrument that makes long-distance medieval trade settlement practical without physically shipping gold or silver across dangerous routes.

## Timing

The Islamic suftaja, a written order allowing a merchant to draw funds from a correspondent in a distant city, is documented from the 700s within the same commercial framework that produces FIN-004's mudaraba and musharaka partnership structures; Italian merchant-banking houses develop comparable bill-of-exchange instruments through their own cross-city correspondent networks from the 1300s, maturing into a standard tool of European commercial settlement by 1400.

## Mechanism

A bill of exchange lets a merchant in one city settle a debt owed to a merchant in another by drawing on a balance or credit relationship with a correspondent there, rather than physically transporting coin or bullion across a route exposed to theft, shipwreck, and delay — the instrument's value rests entirely on trust that the correspondent will actually honor the draw, which is why the earliest reliable networks form within tightly-connected merchant communities (Islamic trading networks, then Italian banking-family correspondent branches) rather than between total strangers.

## Social consequence

Because a bill of exchange requires a functioning correspondent relationship to be worth anything, the instrument concentrates real commercial power in whichever merchant houses maintain the widest and most trusted network of correspondent branches — the same houses (the Medici prominent among them) that dominate this trust network also become the effective bankers to popes, kings, and other merchants who need cross-city settlement, converting a practical settlement technology into genuine political and financial influence.

## Regional specifics

Italian merchant-banking houses' correspondent-branch networks make the bill of exchange practically usable at real commercial scale across European trade cities, directly enabling exactly the kind of delegated, distance-spanning commercial trust FIN-005's double-entry method is partly developed to verify and audit. Chinese long-distance settlement achieving a comparable trust function through merchant-guild networks and, later, dedicated remittance houses rather than a freely circulating negotiable instrument shows the same underlying settlement problem again admits more than one institutional solution.

## Hook

A gate that removes physical bullion transport as a hard requirement for long-distance trade settlement — a private-credit network precondition medieval and early-modern commerce depends on well before formal banking (FIN-008) exists, whose value rests entirely on trust in a specific correspondent network rather than on any physical backing at all.
