Lending at interest solves a real economic problem (moving capital from
those who have it to those who can use it productively, compensating the
lender for risk and delayed consumption) that nearly every literate
tradition in this record regulates, restricts, or channels through an
alternative structure rather than leaving unregulated.

## Timing

Interest-bearing lending is documented from Mesopotamian temple and palace
records by roughly -1800; religious and legal restriction on it develops on
separate timelines across traditions, from Vedic-era Dharmashastra
provisions (~-600) through Islamic riba doctrine (from 610) and medieval
Christian usury prohibition, the last only substantially relaxing by the
1500s-1600s under mounting commercial pressure.

## Mechanism

Every tradition covered here recognizes the same underlying tension:
lending at interest is economically productive (it moves capital to where
it can be used) but also exploitable (a lender can extract crushing terms
from a desperate borrower with no other option) — traditions differ sharply
in how they resolve that tension, from an outright ban paired with an
alternative risk-sharing structure (Islamic partnership contracts) to
graduated rate caps (Dharmashastra) to a near-total prohibition that simply
pushes the activity into a marginalized niche (medieval Christendom) rather
than replacing its economic function with anything else.

## Social consequence

Where formal moneylending is banned outright without a legitimate
alternative structure replacing it, the function doesn't disappear — it
relocates to whichever population is both willing to bear the resulting
social stigma and excluded from other livelihoods anyway, concentrating an
economically essential but socially marginalized role onto a specific
group, exactly the dynamic MIG-100 traces for European Jewish communities
under Christian usury doctrine.

## Regional specifics

The Islamic riba framework is distinctive for building a complete
alternative capital-investment system (profit-and-loss-sharing partnerships)
specifically to preserve the underlying economic function while banning the
fixed-interest mechanism itself — a full substitute, not merely a
restriction. Dharmashastra's graduated, caste- and circumstance-sensitive
rate caps represent a third approach, regulating rather than banning or
replacing interest lending. Medieval Christendom's approach — near-total
prohibition with no institutional alternative offered — is the one most
directly responsible for pushing the function into a marginalized,
minority-occupied niche rather than resolving the underlying tension
another way.

## Hook

Every tradition manages the same lending-at-interest tension differently —
ban-and-replace (Islamic partnerships), regulate-and-cap (Dharmashastra), or
ban-with-no-replacement (medieval Christendom) — and only the last of these
reliably produces the marginalized-moneylender-caste social pattern MIG-100
traces, since banning a needed economic function without replacing it
doesn't eliminate the function, only relocates who performs it.
