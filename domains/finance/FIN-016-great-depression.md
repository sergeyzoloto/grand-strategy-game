This entry treats the Depression as the point where several of this
domain's separate mechanics — an asset bubble's collapse (FIN-007), a
banking panic (FIN-010), and gold-standard rigidity (FIN-002) — compound
into one severe, globally-transmitted crisis rather than resolving
independently.

## Timing

The 1929 U.S. stock market crash (FIN-007) triggers the crisis; banking
panics (FIN-010) and gold-standard-driven monetary contraction (FIN-002)
deepen and prolong it through the 1930s, with recovery timelines varying
sharply by country depending on specific policy responses, substantially
resolved only with wartime mobilization by 1939.

## Mechanism

A stock market crash alone need not become a prolonged depression, but
compounds severely once it triggers banking panics (depositors losing
confidence in banks holding crash-devalued assets) and once gold-standard
adherence (FIN-002) prevents affected governments from expanding money
supply or devaluing to ease the resulting credit contraction — each
mechanic independently covered elsewhere in this domain interacts here to
produce an outcome substantially worse than any one of them would produce
alone.

## Social consequence

Mass unemployment and business failure at this scale and duration
discredits laissez-faire policy's (FIN-012) perceived adequacy for crisis
response across most major economies simultaneously, directly motivating
the expanded state economic role (unemployment insurance, public works,
deposit insurance) that becomes standard in most industrial economies over
the following decades — a policy legacy that outlasts the crisis itself by
generations.

## Regional specifics

Colonial African commodity-export economies suffer disproportionately as
export prices collapse, directly exposing the price-volatility
vulnerability inherent in the commodity-trap structure (COM-017) — the
same colonial economic integration that delivered revenue in good years
transmits the crisis with equal or greater force during the bust. Japan's
earlier departure from the gold standard, combined with fiscal stimulus,
produces measurably faster recovery than economies that maintained gold
adherence longer, an early real-world test case directly relevant to
FIN-002's own contested debate about the standard's crisis-response costs.

## Contested

The relative weight of the crash, banking panics, gold-standard rigidity,
and contractionary policy responses (including tariff retaliation, COM-018)
in explaining the Depression's severity and duration is disputed among
economic historians. Model the crisis as driven by several compounding
factors rather than one dominant cause, with each affected state's specific
policy choices (how long it stayed on gold, how it responded fiscally,
whether it retaliated with tariffs) materially determining how much worse
or better that state fared relative to others facing the same initial
global shock.

## Hook

The Great Depression is what happens when an asset-bubble collapse
(FIN-007), a banking panic (FIN-010), and gold-standard rigidity (FIN-002)
compound simultaneously rather than resolving independently — and a state's
specific policy response to that compounding (gold-exit timing especially)
measurably determines its own relative severity and recovery speed within
the same global shock.
