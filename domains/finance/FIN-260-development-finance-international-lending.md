An international institution capitalized by wealthy member states lends to developing or crisis-hit economies on terms private capital markets wouldn't offer, often attaching policy conditions as the price of access — a lending relationship whose goals extend beyond return, and whose conditionality can constrain a borrowing state's own policy choices in ways an ordinary sovereign bond-market loan (FIN-110) doesn't.

## Timing

The Bretton Woods conference (1944) establishes both the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank as standing institutions for post-war reconstruction and, increasingly from the 1950s-1960s, for financing and stabilizing developing economies; lending to newly independent and crisis-affected states scales substantially through the 1970s.

## Mechanism

Where a private bond market (FIN-110) prices a loan purely on expected return and default risk, a development-lending institution's mandate includes broader stability or development goals, letting it lend to borrowers a purely private market might refuse or charge prohibitively — but typically at the cost of attached conditionality (fiscal targets, structural policy reforms) the borrowing state must accept to access or continue receiving the funds, converting the loan into a lever over the borrower's own domestic policy choices rather than a purely financial transaction.

## Social consequence

Conditionality attached to development lending can constrain an indebted state's own democratic or sovereign policy choices as directly as any occupying power's demands, provoking recurring domestic political friction between a government's obligations to its lenders and to its own electorate or population — a tension with no clean resolution built into the lending relationship itself, since both the loan and the conditions attached to it are usually the borrowing state's only realistic option in a genuine crisis.

## Regional specifics

Newly independent African states' heavy reliance on this lending channel from the 1960s directly intersects with the era's broader post-colonial sovereignty tensions, external conditionality sometimes running directly counter to a new government's own development priorities. Japan's and several other East Asian economies' unusually fast graduation from recipient to donor and creditor status within a few decades, relative to the multi-generational recipient status seen elsewhere, shows the same institutional relationship can be a genuinely temporary developmental stage rather than a durable structural dependency.

## Contested

Whether IMF and World Bank conditionality genuinely stabilizes struggling economies through needed discipline, or imposes counterproductive austerity that deepens the crises it's meant to resolve, is a live, unresolved debate, with real cases supporting both readings. Model conditionality's effect as dependent on the specific terms attached and the borrower's own institutional capacity to absorb them, rather than as a fixed-sign outcome true in every case.

## Hook

A branch: testing whether an indebted state's own priorities survive contact with its lenders' preferred prescriptions, with the outcome conditional on the specific conditionality attached and the borrower's institutional capacity to absorb it rather than resolving the same way in every case.
