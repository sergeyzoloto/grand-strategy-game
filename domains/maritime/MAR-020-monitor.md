A low-freeboard, heavily-armored vessel built around a small number of very
large guns in a rotating turret — a specialized ironclad type optimized for
coastal and riverine defense rather than open-ocean fleet action, named for
the vessel that popularized it.

## Timing

USS Monitor's construction and its 1862 Battle of Hampton Roads engagement
against CSS Virginia is the type's origin point; the design and its
international coastal-defense variants proliferate through the 1860s-1870s
before pre-dreadnought battleship designs (MAR-025) supersede it as the
dominant capital-ship concept.

## Mechanism

Concentrating armor and a small number of very large guns on a low, small
hull trades open-ocean seaworthiness (a low-freeboard monitor handles badly
in rough water and is a poor long-range cruiser) for a much cheaper way to
achieve a locally decisive concentration of armor and firepower — a rational
design specifically for the coastal-defense and riverine mission, where the
vessel doesn't need to survive open-ocean conditions and can instead
maximize armor and gun size relative to cost.

## Social consequence

The type's low cost relative to a full ocean-going ironclad lets
comparatively minor naval powers field a credible coastal-defense
deterrent against a wealthier rival's battle fleet without attempting to
match that fleet's open-ocean capability directly — a specific, narrower
version of the cost-asymmetry logic MAR-019's Jeune École later tries to
generalize to the open ocean.

## Regional specifics

European navies' rapid adoption of the monitor type specifically for
coastal defense, rather than fleet action, shows the design's narrow
mission-fit was well understood immediately rather than mistaken for a
general-purpose warship replacement. East Asian navies' preference for
purchasing conventional ironclad designs rather than developing a monitor-
type coastal specialization of their own is consistent with the broader
purchase-rather-than-develop technology adoption pattern noted in MAR-018's
Ottoman case.

## Hook

The monitor type buys a cheap, locally decisive concentration of armor and
firepower for coastal and riverine defense specifically by sacrificing
open-ocean capability — a narrow-mission-fit design choice, not a general
ironclad replacement, that lets a lesser naval power deter a stronger one's
coastal approach without matching its blue-water fleet.
