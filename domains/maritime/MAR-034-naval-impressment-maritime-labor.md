The sailor's own experience of the same standing-navy crewing problem
MAR-005 treats as a fiscal and institutional question: where voluntary
maritime labor supply falls short of what a standing fleet needs, a
state resorts to coercion, and coercion in turn generates its own costs
no budget line captures.

## Timing

English naval impressment is documented in organized form from the
1660s, expanding sharply during major wars through the 1700s and into
the Napoleonic era; American grievances over British impressment of
their sailors accumulate through the 1790s and 1800s before becoming a
declared cause of the War of 1812; large-scale impressment lapses after
1815 as the practice becomes politically and diplomatically untenable at
its former scale.

## Mechanism

A standing navy (MAR-005) requires far more trained sailors during
wartime mobilization than voluntary enlistment reliably supplies at the
wage a fiscally-constrained state is willing to pay — impressment (press
gangs seizing merchant sailors, often from the same ports whose merchant
shipping the navy is meant to protect) converts a wage problem into a
coercion problem, supplying crews at far below market wage at the cost of
chronic desertion, merchant-shipping labor shortages, and, where the
seized sailors belong to another state's shipping, direct diplomatic
friction.

## Social consequence

Impressment creates a maritime labor force serving under open coercion
alongside voluntarily-enlisted crew on the same ships, a tension that
surfaces directly in major fleet mutinies — the Royal Navy's 1797
Spithead and Nore mutinies, driven substantially by pay and conditions
grievances shared by pressed and volunteer sailors alike — showing a
standing navy's crew, unlike its officer corps, is not the loyal
professional class MAR-005's institutional account might suggest by
default, but a labor force with its own material grievances capable of
collective action against the institution itself.

## Regional specifics

British impressment of American sailors is the clearest case in this
domain of a purely domestic crewing-shortfall solution generating a major
external diplomatic and eventually military consequence, feeding directly
into the causes of the War of 1812 — a state's internal labor-supply
choice becoming another state's casus belli. Qing and Ming China managing
naval crewing shortfalls through general imperial labor-conscription
systems rather than a dedicated maritime impressment apparatus is
consistent with the broader absence of institutionalized standing-navy
administration already noted in MAR-005 — the underlying problem
(crewing a fleet beyond what voluntary supply provides) recurs, but the
institutional solution doesn't.

## Hook

Where voluntary maritime labor supply falls short of a standing navy's
wartime crewing needs, coercive impressment substitutes at the cost of
desertion, diplomatic friction, and — as the Spithead and Nore mutinies
show — the risk of collective action by a crew that experiences the same
institution MAR-005 describes as an imposition rather than a career.
