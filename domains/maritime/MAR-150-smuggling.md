Moving goods across a legally-restricted boundary without authorization —
mechanically similar to piracy (MAR-030) in exploiting weak enforcement
capacity relative to trade volume, but evading a tariff or prohibition
rather than taking cargo by force.

## Timing

Smuggling against customs and trade-monopoly restrictions is attested
wherever such restrictions exist; the cases compared here span Ming China's
1550s maritime prohibition enforcement and the height of European
mercantilist trade-restriction regimes through the 1800s.

## Mechanism

Smuggling is a direct function of the gap between a restriction's stated
scope and a state's actual capacity to enforce it along an entire coastline
— the same enforcement-capacity-versus-coastline-length problem that
determines piracy's prevalence (MAR-030) determines smuggling's, except the
"predation" here is on the state's tariff or monopoly revenue rather than on
other merchants' cargo directly.

## Social consequence

Where smuggling becomes pervasive and locally tolerated, it functions as an
informal, bottom-up correction to a trade policy the affected population
considers unreasonable, without requiring any formal political change —
merchants, consumers, and often local officials develop a shared interest in
non-enforcement that can persist for generations even as the law itself
stays on the books unchanged.

## Regional specifics

Ming China's haijin prohibition is a clean case of legal reclassification
alone converting previously ordinary commerce into criminalized smuggling,
with the direct, documented side effect of pushing displaced merchants into
the Wokou raiding networks that the prohibition was nominally meant to
suppress. Colonial American smuggling against British mercantilist
restrictions reaching a scale where enforcement becomes selectively
symbolic shows smuggling volume itself can function as a de facto policy
override, renegotiating enforced trade terms without any formal legal change.

## Hook

Smuggling scales with the gap between a trade restriction's legal scope and
a state's real coastal enforcement capacity — high enough volume can make a
restriction symbolic rather than binding in practice, and a sudden
restriction on previously legal trade (as with haijin) can manufacture large-
scale smuggling and associated raiding networks overnight.
