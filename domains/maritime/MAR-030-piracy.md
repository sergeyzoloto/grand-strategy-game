Unlicensed, non-state maritime predation on shipping — the negative
externality every coastal trade network (MAR-020) generates simply by
existing, and a recurring pressure toward either naval suppression or
protection-payment as the two available responses.

## Timing

Piracy is attested wherever organized seaborne trade exists, from the "Sea
Peoples" raiding of the eastern Mediterranean (~-1300) through the Barbary
corsair era (from 1518) and the Caribbean "Golden Age of Piracy"
(roughly 1650-1730), into the early 19th century before steam-powered naval
patrol capability (MAR-270 onward) makes endemic piracy far harder to sustain
at scale.

## Mechanism

Piracy is a direct tax on trade volume and route predictability: the same
route knowledge and traffic density that makes a shipping lane commercially
valuable (MAR-020) makes it a predictable, worthwhile hunting ground, and a
weak or absent naval patrol presence is what determines whether that
predation gets suppressed or tolerated as background cost. Where a
land-based political authority can extract comparable value by taxing or
licensing the same shipping instead (or by taking a cut of the piracy itself,
as with the Barbary regencies), piracy and state authority blend rather than
opposing each other cleanly.

## Social consequence

Endemic piracy imposes an insurable, budgetable cost on commercial shipping
(feeding directly into MAR-120's development) once it's persistent enough to
be predictable, while also creating a genuine alternative economic path
(plunder rather than production or trade) attractive enough to draw
participants from multiple, sometimes rival, political and ethnic
communities at once, as the Wokou case shows especially clearly.

## Regional specifics

The Barbary corsairs' three-century operation with quasi-state sanction
shows piracy and privateering (MAR-110) aren't always cleanly separable
categories — tribute payments from multiple European states functioned as an
ongoing protection-rent arrangement rather than a one-time suppression
victory for either side. Wokou raiding's multi-ethnic composition and its
intensification under Ming anti-trade policy shows restrictive state policy
can directly manufacture more piracy by closing off the legal trade
alternative participants would otherwise prefer. The Indian Ocean's relative
(not absolute) suppression of endemic piracy relative to its trade volume
suggests cooperative multi-polity interest in protecting a shared, valuable
trade calendar can substitute for naval suppression as a piracy-limiting
mechanism.

## Hook

Piracy taxes trade volume and route predictability directly, extracting
`protection rent` from shipping wherever naval patrol capability is weak
relative to trade density — suppressible by naval investment, but also
substitutable by land-based authorities simply taking their own cut of the
same predation instead of stopping it.
