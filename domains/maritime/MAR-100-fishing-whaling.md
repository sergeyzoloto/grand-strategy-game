Extraction of food or raw material directly from the sea rather than
movement of goods across it — the one major maritime economic activity
this domain's trade, war, and finance mechanics never touch, and the
domain's first genuine depleting resource pool.

## Timing

Basque whaling in the Bay of Biscay is attested from around the 11th
century; organized Japanese coastal whaling and fishing cooperatives
develop from around 1600; American deep-ocean sperm whaling scales from
Nantucket after 1712 and reaches its peak in the mid-1800s before
petroleum displaces whale oil as a lighting fuel by 1859.

## Mechanism

A fishery or whale population is a renewable resource under moderate
extraction pressure — it reproduces and replenishes between seasons —
but a genuinely exhaustible one under sustained heavy extraction, since
population recovery takes years the extraction rate can outpace
indefinitely if effort keeps scaling. This is a structurally different
economic relationship to the sea than anything else in this domain:
coastal trade (MAR-020) and ocean navigation (MAR-160) move an existing
good from one place to another, while fishing and whaling remove a
quantity that must first regenerate before it can be taken again.

## Social consequence

A fishing or whaling community's wealth is tied directly to a resource
stock its own success can deplete — sustained good catches draw more
effort and capital into the fishery, which can draw the stock down
faster than it replenishes, a boom-that-undermines-itself dynamic none
of this domain's trade or naval mechanics otherwise produce, and one that
recurs independently in the North Sea herring fishery, the Grand Banks
cod fishery, and the depletion of whale populations near established
whaling grounds.

## Regional specifics

American deep-ocean whaling's shift to multi-year blue-water voyages
specifically to reach whale populations closer coastal waters had
already thinned is itself a direct behavioral response to local
depletion — fleets moving further and staying out longer as the nearest
stock draws down, the depletion dynamic visibly shaping the industry's
own geography over time. Japanese coastal whaling and fishing
cooperatives sustain intensive extraction at village scale without the
Atlantic pattern's blue-water expedition model, showing organizational
scale and extraction technique are separable choices. The Indian Ocean's
comparative absence of an export-oriented industrial fishery alongside
its heavy participation in monsoon trade (MAR-020) suggests capital and
labor went where the relative return was higher, not that fishing
knowledge or technique was lacking.

## Hook

A depleting resource pool — a `fishery stock` (design principle 4) drawn
down by extraction effort and replenished only slowly between seasons —
the domain's first genuine pool mechanic, and a boom-that-undermines-
itself dynamic distinct from anything MAR-020's trade-in-existing-goods
model produces.
