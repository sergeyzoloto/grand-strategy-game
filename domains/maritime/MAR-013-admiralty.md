This entry deliberately covers two functions historically bundled under one
institution: fleet administration (a WAR-adjacent function this domain now
owns per its carve-out) and maritime jurisdiction — prize courts, salvage
disputes, jurisdiction over crimes at sea — which is a genuinely LAW-adjacent
judicial function riding along inside the same institution.

## Timing

English admiralty jurisdiction is documented from the 14th century, with the
institution's fleet-administration and judicial functions both maturing and
partly separating over the following centuries into the 1900s reforms that
eventually fold naval administration into a broader ministry structure.

## Mechanism

Bundling fleet logistics (supply, maintenance, dockyard administration) with
maritime legal jurisdiction (prize adjudication — who legally owns a
captured ship and cargo, salvage claims, crimes committed at sea) into one
institution makes sense specifically because both functions require the same
scarce specialized knowledge of maritime affairs that a general-purpose
court or treasury department wouldn't have — the institution exists because
the expertise, not the two functions themselves, is what's genuinely shared.

## Social consequence

Prize-court rulings under admiralty jurisdiction directly determine whether
privateering (MAR-012) and naval captures actually pay out, making the
admiralty's judicial function a critical, if under-appreciated, precondition
for wartime privateering to function as a reliable enough incentive to
attract private capital at all — a captured ship whose legal status stays
unresolved is worth nothing to the captors.

## Regional specifics

The Middle Eastern Diwan al-Ustul administrations combine comparable fleet-
logistics and maritime-jurisdiction functions on an earlier, independent
timeline, showing the specific bundling logic (shared specialized knowledge)
recurs wherever a state both maintains ships and needs to adjudicate
maritime disputes, not only in the English case most commonly cited. Qing
and Ming China's administration of naval affairs through the general
imperial bureaucracy rather than a dedicated maritime institution is
consistent with the broader pattern in MAR-005: the administrative capacity
existed, but wasn't organizationally concentrated the way the European and
Middle Eastern admiralty models concentrated it.

## Hook

An admiralty institution converts scattered maritime administrative and
judicial `legibility` into one specialized body — and its prize-court
function specifically is a hidden precondition for privateering (MAR-012)
to work as an incentive at all, since captured value means nothing without
a court to confirm the capture was lawful.
