For three centuries the sea was the standing example of something no one could
own. Then technology made the seabed and its fisheries valuable, and within four
decades more than a third of the ocean became national jurisdiction. This is
enclosure, the same mechanism applied to water that was applied to land.

## Timing

From Grotius's _Mare Liberum_ in 1609 and Selden's reply, through the cannon-shot
and three-mile rules of the eighteenth century, to the Truman Proclamation of 1945,
the Latin American 200-mile claims from 1947, the Geneva conventions of 1958, and
the Third United Nations Conference on the Law of the Sea from 1973. That produced
the convention signed in 1982 and in force in 1994. The deep-seabed agreement of
1994 and the Arctic continental-shelf submissions around 2000 are the entry's
current edge.

## Mechanism

**Why this is MAR and not LAW or DIP.** Boundary rules give LAW internal
obligation and DIP inter-polity relation, and hold a treaty to be DIP. The
MAR/(LAW, DIP) clause qualifies that. Regimes whose subject is the sea itself,
meaning jurisdiction over waters, seabed and vessels, are MAR, on the same
principle that places enclosure of land in AGR-200. MAR-140, which carries
admiralty and prize law, is the precedent. This entry's claim is about how the
marine domain was appropriated, not about how states relate to each other, so it
is MAR, with law and diplomacy touched heavily.

**The sea begins as a commons that cannot be enclosed.** Grotius argued the sea
could not be property because it could not be occupied, and because its use by
one did not diminish its use by another. Both claims were true of shipping and
roughly true of fishing at sixteenth-century effort. Freedom of the seas held for
three centuries not because states were restrained but because no state could hold
water. Coastal states claimed only a narrow belt they could defend from the shore,
first measured by cannon range, then fixed at three miles.

**Technology makes the seabed valuable and enclosure follows.** Offshore drilling
made the continental shelf worth claiming, and industrial trawlers and factory
ships made fish stocks worth defending. The Truman Proclamation of 1945 claimed the
shelf for the United States. Latin American states answered with claims to 200
miles of water. Within four decades the 1982 convention set territorial seas at 12
miles, exclusive economic zones at 200 miles, and continental shelves in places
to 350. Over a third of the world's ocean passed from open access to national
jurisdiction.

**It is the same mechanism as land enclosure.** AGR-200 describes consolidation
of common land into exclusive holdings when rising returns made exclusive control
worth its cost. Enclosure extinguished the customary use rights of people who had
never held title. The ocean case has every element of that:

- **A rise in value.** Wool and rotation on land; oil and industrial fishing at
  sea.
- **A legal instrument declaring exclusive rights.** Enclosure acts; a
  proclamation and then a convention.
- **Losers who had used the commons without title.** Commoners on land;
  distant-water fishing fleets and traditional fishers who crossed what became
  national lines.
- **A conservation argument for the change.** The tragedy of the commons, which
  was applied to both.

The comparison is the entry's strongest content. It shows the law of the sea as a
property revolution, not a diplomatic settlement.

**Islands and baselines are the lever.** An exclusive economic zone is measured
from land, so a rock generates a zone of sea far larger than itself. A single
feature can command over 400,000 square kilometres, and the rules on what counts
as an island decide whether it does. That makes uninhabited outcrops enormously
valuable and turns them into contested territory. Japan reinforces Okinotorishima
against erosion; the South China Sea features are built up and fortified; the
Senkaku/Diaoyu islands are disputed; Britain gave up an EEZ claim from Rockall when
it ratified. The convention denies a zone to rocks that cannot sustain habitation
or economic life, so states have an incentive to make features look habitable.
Archipelagic baselines, pioneered by Indonesia, let a state enclose whole inland
seas.

**Enforcement produced the first conflicts between allies.** The Cod Wars pitted
Iceland against Britain inside NATO. They show that a small state with one vital
resource could enclose it against a great naval power, because the confrontation
cost the stronger side more in alliance terms than the fish were worth.

**The deep seabed was meant to stay a commons, and partly did not.** Arvid Pardo's
1967 proposal made the seabed beyond national jurisdiction the "common heritage of
mankind", managed internationally with shared revenues. Industrial states objected
to the mining regime. The 1994 agreement rewrote it on their terms, and the United
States still has not ratified the convention. The last unenclosed seabed is
therefore held under a regime its largest maritime power does not accept.

**The Arctic is the present frontier.** Ice retreat opens both routes and seabed.
The Arctic states' continental-shelf submissions overlap along the Lomonosov Ridge,
and a Russian flag planted on the North Pole seabed in 2007 staked a symbolic claim.
The enclosure continues wherever the ice once made occupation impossible.

## Social consequence

At household scale enclosure redraws where a fishing family may work. A Newfoundland
outport, a Breton trawler crew, a Senegalese pirogue fisher, a Taiwanese
distant-water boat and a Kiribati government's budget were all rearranged by lines
drawn on a chart. Fleets that had fished off other countries' coasts for generations
became trespassers. Coastal communities gained exclusive rights that did not always
protect them. The Newfoundland cod moratorium of 1992 closed a fishery that had
sustained the island for five centuries, fifteen years after Canada had enclosed
it, and put tens of thousands of people out of work. National jurisdiction had
replaced foreign overfishing with domestic overfishing and optimistic stock
assessments. The fishery stock (MAR-100) collapsed under the enclosing state's own
management.

Enclosure also moved wealth to states that had no way of taking it by force.
Pacific island states became ocean powers on paper and funded their governments on
fishing access fees. Newly independent states in Africa and Asia gained zones they
often could not patrol. Off Somalia, foreign fleets fished an unpatrolled zone
until local armed groups turned to piracy. A jurisdiction without enforcement
invites the same free access it abolished in law.

## Regional specifics

Europe supplies the origin of the doctrine, Grotius against Selden, and the first
enforcement conflict in the Cod Wars. The modern enclosure was driven from outside
Europe:

- **The United States** claimed the shelf for oil in 1945.
- **Chile, Peru and Ecuador** claimed 200 miles of water for fish, with the
  widest claims coming from states whose resource lay in the water rather than
  under it.
- **Indonesia** invented the archipelagic state and wrote it into the
  convention.
- **Pacific island states** became the largest beneficiaries per head.
- **Bahrain and Saudi Arabia** divided the Gulf seabed for oil before the
  convention existed.
- **East Asia** carries the island lever at its most contested, from
  Okinotorishima to the Spratlys.
- **Bangladesh, Myanmar and India** settled their maritime boundaries through
  international arbitration.

The Caspian is the absence: it sat outside the regime because no one could agree
whether it was a sea.

The doctrine's first form grew out of the port-and-passage struggle MAR-185
describes. Grotius wrote to defend Dutch trade against a Portuguese claim to
exclusive navigation in the Indian Ocean, and in 1615 a Makassar ruler told the
Dutch that God had given the sea to all.

## Contested

Whether enclosure conserves a commons is disputed. Open-access economics and Hardin
said exclusive rights prevent overuse. Ostrom showed user communities managing
commons without enclosure. The ocean supports both readings: some fisheries
stabilised under national control, and Newfoundland cod collapsed under it.

Model enclosure as a transfer of control, with conservation outcomes conditional on
the enclosing state's management capacity and its willingness to restrain its own
fleet. The deep-seabed common-heritage regime is contested too. It was rewritten in
1994 for industrial states and remains unratified by the United States.

## Hook

The sea starts as an unappropriable commons. Freedom of navigation and open access
to fisheries hold because water cannot be occupied, and coastal jurisdiction reaches
only a narrow defensible belt.

**Enclosure triggers on rising value.** When seabed extraction or industrial
fishing raises the value of marine resources, coastal states enclose the ocean,
claiming a shelf and then a 200-mile exclusive zone. Distant-water users lose access
without title, exactly as land enclosure dispossessed commoners.

**Area is generated from land.** Zones are measured from land features, so small
islands and rocks generate large sea areas, which makes uninhabited features
strategic territory worth fortifying, building up or contesting. Archipelagic
baselines let island states enclose inland seas.

**Enclosure transfers control, not conservation.** `fishery stock` under national
jurisdiction recovers or collapses depending on the enclosing state's management
capacity and fleet restraint. Enclosure without enforcement reverts to open access
in practice.

**Two domains stay open.** The deep seabed beyond national zones stays a contested
commons under an incompletely binding regime. Ice retreat extends enclosure into the
Arctic.
