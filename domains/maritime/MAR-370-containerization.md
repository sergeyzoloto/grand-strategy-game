Standardized, stackable steel shipping containers, moved by dedicated crane
infrastructure directly between ship, rail, and truck without unpacking —
the single infrastructure change most responsible for the collapse in
shipping cost that underlies the entire late-20th-century globalized trade
system.

## Timing

Malcolm McLean's first container ship voyage (1956) is the origin point;
containerization scales through major world ports over the following three
decades, substantially complete as the dominant general-cargo shipping
method by 1990.

## Mechanism

Standardizing cargo into uniform containers that transfer directly between
ship, rail car, and truck without being unpacked and repacked at each stage
collapses port turnaround time and labor cost by an order of magnitude
relative to break-bulk cargo handling, which had previously required
laboriously loading and unloading individually-shaped items by hand at
every transfer point — the cost reduction is so large that it makes
manufacturing-for-export viable from far more distant locations than
previous shipping economics would have supported.

## Social consequence

Containerization eliminates the large dockworker labor forces that
break-bulk cargo handling required (directly reversing the labor-
concentration effect MAR-240 describes for the wet-dock era), provoking
major labor disputes and permanent workforce reduction at ports worldwide,
while simultaneously making it economically viable to relocate manufacturing
to wherever labor is cheapest rather than wherever is closest to the end
market — the technology that shrinks one workforce (dockworkers) directly
enables the offshoring that reshapes a much larger one (manufacturing labor)
globally.

## Regional specifics

East Asian economies' early and aggressive adoption of container port
infrastructure develops in direct tandem with their own export-oriented
manufacturing growth strategy, the technology and the development model
mutually reinforcing rather than one simply following the other. Africa's
comparative lag in deep-water container terminal investment functions as a
modern-infrastructure-scale version of the tidal-dock investment gap in
MAR-240, constraining which economies can practically compete for
export-manufacturing relocation regardless of their labor cost advantage
otherwise.

## Hook

Containerization collapses port turnaround cost and time by an order of
magnitude, converting manufacturing location choice from a proximity-to-
market decision into a labor-cost-driven one — a single infrastructure
standard whose absence (as in much of Africa's port investment gap) can
lock an otherwise labor-cost-competitive economy out of the global
manufacturing-relocation trend entirely.
