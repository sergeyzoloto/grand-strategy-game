A financial instrument, not a maritime technique — this entry exists because
the risk it prices (shipwreck, piracy, cargo loss) is maritime-specific
enough to warrant owning here rather than folding into a generic FIN
insurance entry, per this domain's carve-out from FIN in boundary-rules.

## Timing

Genoese and Mediterranean marine insurance contracts are attested from
roughly 1300; the instrument standardizes and scales significantly with the
rise of dedicated underwriting institutions (Lloyd's of London coffee-house
underwriting from the 1680s) through the 1700s.

## Mechanism

Marine insurance converts a large, low-probability, catastrophic loss
(total loss of ship and cargo) into a small, certain, budgetable cost (the
premium), which is what makes maritime trade financially viable for
investors who couldn't otherwise absorb the risk of any single voyage's
total loss — the underwriter's business model in turn depends on
aggregating enough independent voyages that the law of large numbers makes
the aggregate risk predictable even though any single voyage's outcome
isn't.

## Social consequence

Once a workable underwriting market exists, capital that would otherwise
stay concentrated in only the wealthiest risk-tolerant merchants can spread
across many smaller investors each taking a fraction of a voyage's risk via
insurance and partial ownership, widening who can participate in financing
long-distance maritime trade well beyond the small circle able to
self-insure by absorbing occasional total losses personally.

## Regional specifics

Islamic commercial law's risk-sharing partnership instruments achieve a
comparable underlying function (distributing catastrophic voyage risk across
multiple parties) through a structurally different mechanism shaped by riba
prohibitions on fixed-interest lending, rather than adopting or independently
inventing a premium-based contract — the same economic problem, two
legally distinct solutions. Chinese maritime trade's reliance on cargo-
splitting and diversified partnership investment shows portfolio
diversification can substitute for a dedicated insurance instrument
entirely, achieving comparable risk mitigation without transferable risk
contracts at all.

## Hook

Marine insurance converts catastrophic per-voyage risk into a budgetable
premium cost backed by `contract enforcement`, widening the pool of capital
willing to finance long-distance maritime trade — a function other
traditions achieve instead through profit-sharing partnership structures or
cargo diversification, not a universally-necessary instrument.
