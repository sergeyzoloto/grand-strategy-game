A second, mechanically distinct reversal case alongside the Ming
treasure fleet's institutional abandonment (MAR-005, MAR-008): an
existing, valuable trade network doesn't lose its capability at all — it
simply gets out-competed by a shorter route somebody else controls.

## Timing

Vasco da Gama's 1498 arrival at Calicut opens a direct all-water route
between Europe and Indian Ocean spice markets; the Portuguese Crown's
systematic exploitation of the route through the following decades
progressively diverts spice volume away from the Mediterranean-Red
Sea-overland relay chain, with Venice's spice-trade revenues in
sustained relative decline by 1600.

## Mechanism

The Mediterranean's pre-1498 spice supply depends on a multi-stage relay
— Indian Ocean shipping to Red Sea ports, overland or short-sea transfer
to the Mediterranean, then Venetian or Genoese distribution to the rest
of Europe — with each stage adding cost, time, and a toll-collecting
intermediary's cut. The Cape route replaces that entire relay with a
single all-water passage under one flag's control, and once the volume
and reliability of that direct route matures, the old relay chain's
accumulated route knowledge, institutional relationships, and
toll-collecting position at each stage convert from a durable commercial
asset into a stranded cost almost overnight — not because any capability
was lost, but because a competing route made that capability's output no
longer worth paying a premium for.

## Social consequence

Venice's political and commercial elite, whose wealth and civic identity
had been built substantially on controlling one stage of the old relay
chain, face a structural loss of relative commercial position they
cannot recover through better administration or renewed investment in
the same route — the problem isn't a decline in demand for spices or a
failure of Venetian commercial skill, but a competing geography that
makes the old route's entire value proposition obsolete regardless of
how well it's still run.

## Regional specifics

The old relay route's obsolescence without direct military contest or
blockade — the Mamluk and Ottoman-administered legs of the chain are
simply bypassed, not conquered or closed — shows a trade network can lose
its commercial function entirely while remaining physically and
institutionally intact, a sharper and more purely market-driven version
of capability loss than the Ming treasure fleet's deliberate institutional
dismantling elsewhere in this domain.

## Hook

A new, shorter, single-control route can convert an existing multi-stage
trade network's accumulated route knowledge and toll position from an
asset into a stranded cost almost overnight — a route-obsolescence event
distinct from institutional abandonment (MAR-005, MAR-008), since here
the old capability isn't given up, it's simply out-competed.
