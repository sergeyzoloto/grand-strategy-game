Two mirror-image naval strategies aimed at the same target — an enemy's
seaborne trade — from opposite directions: blockade (denying an enemy's
ports) and commerce raiding (hunting an enemy's ships at large), both
converting MAR-001's trade-dependency into a wartime vulnerability.

## Timing

Formalized blockade and commerce-raiding doctrine develop alongside the
regular navy (MAR-005) and privateering (MAR-012) institutions from the mid-
1600s, reaching their most systematically decisive application in the
unrestricted submarine warfare campaigns (MAR-024) of both World Wars
through 1945.

## Mechanism

Sea-lane strategy exploits the same trade-dependency that makes coastal and
oceanic trade (MAR-001, MAR-008) economically valuable in peacetime: a
polity dependent on seaborne imports for food, raw materials, or war
materiel can be strategically strangled by interdicting that trade, at a
cost to the interdicting power (ships, crews, diplomatic friction with
neutral shipping) that's typically far lower than the cost of defeating that
polity's land forces directly.

## Social consequence

Sea-lane interdiction imposes its cost on a targeted state's civilian
population and war-production capacity simultaneously and often before its
military forces are directly engaged at all, making blockade and commerce-
raiding strategies frequently more ethically and diplomatically contested
than direct military engagement — neutral-shipping disputes recur as a major
diplomatic friction point (feeding directly into DIP's territory) every time
a sea-lane strategy is employed against a trade-dependent target.

## Regional specifics

The Union blockade's deliberate targeting of Confederate cotton exports and
import dependency, rather than seeking decisive fleet engagement against a
Confederate navy that barely existed, shows sea-lane strategy can be a
primary war-winning approach rather than merely a supporting one when the
target's economic vulnerability is severe enough. Imperial Japan's opposite
choice — prioritizing decisive fleet battle over its own merchant-marine
protection — left its import-dependent economy critically exposed once
Allied submarines turned the same sea-lane-interdiction logic against it, a
doctrinal choice rather than a capability gap, since Japan had the
shipbuilding tradition to prioritize escort construction had its naval
doctrine emphasized that mission.

## Hook

Sea-lane strategy converts an enemy's own trade-dependency `route knowledge`
value into a wartime vulnerability from either direction — blockading its
ports or raiding its ships at large — at a cost far below direct land
engagement, but exposes the interdicting power to major neutral-shipping
diplomatic friction in proportion to how aggressively it's pursued.
