Sustained, deliberate voyaging out of sight of land across open ocean for
extended periods — the capability that finally breaks the coastal-cabotage
ceiling (MAR-001) and makes genuinely global maritime contact possible for
the first time.

## Timing

Zheng He's Ming treasure fleet voyages (1405-1433) are the earliest
large-scale, sustained instance; Iberian Atlantic and then global voyages
(from the 1420s Portuguese Atlantic exploration through Magellan's
expedition completing circumnavigation by 1522) follow on a separate,
overlapping timeline.

## Mechanism

Ocean navigation combines celestial/instrumented positioning (MAR-003),
flexible rigging able to handle a much wider range of wind conditions over
a long passage (MAR-007), and large enough vessels to carry the food and
water reserves a multi-week or multi-month passage requires — no single
component is sufficient alone, which is why the capability appears only once
all three mature roughly together, in more than one place independently.

## Social consequence

Once achieved, ocean navigation converts previously separate regional trade
and political systems into nodes of one connected global network for the
first time, with enormous and asymmetric consequences for whichever
societies end up on the receiving end of first contact (see the health and
migration domains' entries on the Columbian Exchange and virgin-soil
epidemics) rather than initiating it.

## Regional specifics

The Ming treasure fleet's scale — larger ships, far larger crews, earlier
timing — genuinely outclasses the roughly contemporary and following
Iberian voyages on every raw capability measure, which makes the fleet's
deliberate dismantling after 1433 (a domestic political decision, not a
technical failure, see MAR-005) one of the starkest "capability existed,
institutional commitment didn't" cases in this entire record: China had
ocean navigation before Europe and chose not to keep it, while Iberian
powers, starting from a smaller base, sustained and expanded theirs for
centuries. African absence of a comparable deliberate ocean-crossing
project, despite deep prior maritime trade experience, plausibly reflects
an already-adequate existing network rather than any missing capability.

## Hook

Ocean navigation permanently unlocks global-scale `route knowledge` once
celestial positioning, flexible rigging, and sufficient vessel range all
exist together — but, per the Ming case, the unlock is reversible at the
institutional level even after being technically achieved, unlike most other
technology unlocks in this record.
