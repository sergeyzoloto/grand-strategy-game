Maritime safety and environmental regulation is almost entirely reactive. A
catastrophe with visible casualties or visible damage produces an international
convention within a few years, and nothing produces one in between. Regulation is a
ratchet turned by disasters, not a gradual improvement, and flags of convenience
make the ratchet weaker than it looks.

## Timing

From the Titanic in 1912 and the first safety convention in 1914, through the
creation of the International Maritime Organization's predecessor in 1948, the
Torrey Canyon in 1967 and the pollution conventions that followed, the Amoco Cadiz
in 1978 and port state control from 1982, the Exxon Valdez in 1989 and the
double-hull mandates, to the Erika in 1999 and Prestige in 2002 and the European
response, and the Deepwater Horizon in 2010.

## Mechanism

**The ratchet.** Shipping is international, so its safety and pollution rules have
to be agreed among many states with different interests. Owners in flag states,
cargo interests and coastal states disagree about who pays for safety. In ordinary
years that disagreement blocks new rules. A disaster breaks the deadlock: visible
deaths or blackened coastlines create public pressure in the states that write
conventions, and a text is agreed within a few years:

- **Titanic, 1912.** Lifeboats for all and continuous radio watch followed, which
  is where MED-120's finding lives: the watch, not the apparatus, is what saves
  people.
- **Torrey Canyon (1967) and Amoco Cadiz (1978).** They produced liability
  conventions, a compensation fund, MARPOL, and port state control.
- **Exxon Valdez, 1989.** Double hulls followed.
- **Erika (1999) and Prestige (2002).** Europe accelerated the phase-out of
  single-hull tankers and tightened inspection.

Each step raises the `maritime safety floor`, and none reverses. Between disasters
the floor does not move.

**Visibility, not magnitude, turns the ratchet.** The response scales with how
visible the disaster is to the publics of wealthy port and flag states, not with the
number of deaths. The Doña Paz killed several times the Titanic's toll and produced
no convention. The Torrey Canyon killed nobody and produced several. A disaster
outside the international voyage regime, or outside the audience that drives it,
does not turn the ratchet at all.

**Flags of convenience weaken it.** A ship registers under a flag, and the flag
state holds jurisdiction over it: its safety standards, crew rules and inspection.
The owner chooses the flag. An owner can therefore register where regulation and
tax are lightest, in Panama from the interwar years, Liberia from 1948 and later
the Marshall Islands. That decouples regulatory authority from both the owner's
nationality and the waters the ship sails. It is a clean case of `regulatory
arbitrage`, the same logic FIN-240 describes for capital registered in tax havens.
Every convention the ratchet produces is enforced first by flag states, and the
flags that attract the most tonnage are those with the least capacity or will to
enforce. The ratchet raises the floor on paper faster than on the water.

**Port state control is the counter-move.** If the flag state will not enforce,
the state a ship arrives at can. The Paris memorandum of 1982 coordinated European
port inspection. The Tokyo memorandum of 1993 extended it across the Asia-Pacific,
and the United States enforced its own oil pollution law by closing its ports to
non-compliant tankers. A port state cannot choose which ships are registered where,
but it can detain any ship that wants to use its harbour. Enforcement moves from the
state you are registered in to the state you arrive in, and a large importing
market can impose a global standard by making it a condition of entry, as the
double-hull mandate showed. Boundary rules place this jurisdiction over vessels in
MAR, and MAR-205 carries the wider law of the sea it sits inside.

**Insurance and liability are the real enforcement.** Conventions set rules, but
the institutions that most reliably make owners follow them are private:

- **Classification societies** certify a ship as seaworthy.
- **Protection and indemnity clubs**, mutual insurers covering most ocean-going
  tonnage, refuse cover to substandard ships.
- **The liability conventions** make owners pay for pollution.

A ship that cannot get class or cover cannot trade with major cargo interests or
enter many ports, whatever its flag. This is often more effective than the
convention itself, because the insurer has money at stake on every voyage and
inspects accordingly. FIN-090 carries the actuarial pooling this rests on, and
MAR-120 its maritime origin.

**The ecological event is distinct from the economic one.** An oil spill's damage to
fisheries, tourism and cleanup budgets is measurable and compensable, and the
liability regime is built around it. Its damage to a marine ecosystem is slower,
less certain and harder to price. The Torrey Canyon's detergents killed more shore
life than its oil, and herring in Prince William Sound collapsed years after the
Exxon Valdez. A regime that compensates the economic event can leave the ecological
one largely uncounted.

**The boundary with ENG-265.** ENG-265 owns petroleum extraction: the substitution
of oil for earlier fuels and the stranding it causes. This entry owns the transport
of oil and other cargo by sea, and the regulation of ships. Deepwater Horizon was
an extraction platform, and the blowout belongs to extraction's risks. Only the
regulatory response, and the gap it exposed in international rules for offshore
drilling, belongs here.

## Social consequence

At human scale the ratchet protects some people and not others. Passengers on
international voyages from wealthy states gained lifeboats, radio watches and fire
rules. Passengers on domestic ferries in the Philippines, Senegal and Bangladesh
travelled on ships no convention reached. Seafarers from the Philippines, India,
Indonesia and elsewhere crewed flag-of-convenience ships whose standards depended
on registries that competed for business by asking little. Crews were sometimes
abandoned in foreign ports when owners defaulted. At the end of a ship's life,
workers at Alang and Chittagong cut it apart by hand.

Coastal communities meet the ratchet as a disaster first and a rule afterward.
Brittany and Galicia were oiled repeatedly before European rules caught up, and
fishing towns in Prince William Sound lost their herring fishery. Each disaster
eventually produced compensation and regulation, and always after the damage.

## Regional specifics

Europe supplies the ratchet's classic sequence. The Titanic, the Torrey Canyon and
Amoco Cadiz off Britain and France, and the Erika and Prestige off France and Spain
produced conventions and the first port state control regime. The United States
shows a single large market imposing a standard globally by statute, with double
hulls required before the international rule existed. Deepwater Horizon exposed the
limits of the regime offshore.

Outside Europe and North America the regime looks different:

- **Southeast Asia** supplies the absence. The Doña Paz, the deadliest peacetime
  disaster, turned no ratchet. The region also supplies the crews who bear the risk.
- **South Asia** carries shipbreaking, regulatory arbitrage at the end of a ship's
  life on the beaches at Alang and Chittagong.
- **Africa** hosts the largest flag of convenience in Liberia, and suffered another
  unregulated mass-casualty ferry disaster in Le Joola.
- **East Asia's** Tokyo memorandum put port state control on the busiest shipping
  lanes in the world.

## Contested

Whether disasters cause regulation or only open windows for proposals already
prepared is disputed. The focusing-event and policy-window literature holds that
conventions after disasters draw on drafts that existed before them, and the major
maritime cases fit that reading. The resulting rules' effectiveness is also
contested: double hulls reduce some spills and not others, and weak flag states
dilute every convention.

Model a disaster as opening a short window in which pending proposals are adopted.
The resulting rule's strength should be conditional on how much was already
prepared, and its real effect discounted by lax-flag registration and extended by
port state control.

## Hook

**The ratchet.** The `maritime safety floor` rises in discrete steps, each triggered
by a disaster visible to the publics of convention-writing states, and it does not
fall. Between visible disasters it does not move. Magnitude alone does not trigger
it: a large disaster among poor domestic passengers outside the international regime
produces nothing.

**Arbitrage.** Each rise is diluted by `regulatory arbitrage`. Owners register ships
under flags with the lightest enforcement, so the share of tonnage under lax flags
discounts the floor's real effect.

**The counter-moves:**

- **Port state control.** The state a ship arrives at detains substandard ships
  regardless of flag. A large market can impose a global standard by making it a
  condition of entry.
- **Private enforcement.** Insurers and classification societies deny cover and
  class to substandard ships, often more effectively than the conventions.

**Two costs stay outside the regime.** Oil spills produce a compensable economic loss
and a less-counted ecological one. Arbitrage extends to the end of a ship's life,
moving shipbreaking to the least regulated beaches.
