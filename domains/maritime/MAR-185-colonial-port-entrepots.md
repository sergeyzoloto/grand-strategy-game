A port is not a place where ships stop. It is a position that taxes, transships
and excludes, and holding one lets a power set the terms of a region's trade
without controlling the region. So the competition is for a small number of
specific points, and holding one is worth more than holding a great deal of land.

## Timing

From the Portuguese entry into the Indian Ocean after 1498, made possible by ocean
navigation (MAR-160), through the Estado da
Índia's fortified chain from Hormuz to Malacca to Macau, the VOC's Batavia from
1619, the British presidencies from 1639, and Omani Zanzibar, to the free ports
of Singapore in 1819 and Hong Kong in 1842, the treaty-port system in China and
Japan, and the coaling entrepôts of the steam age such as Aden. It closes around
1900, by which time most of these positions had become colonial port cities
administering territory rather than points on a contested chain.

## Mechanism

**The boundary with COM-130 and MIG-060.** COM-130 owns the strait or canal as a
transit point: control of a narrow passage lets its holder levy tolls and base
fleets on traffic moving through. This entry owns the port as an institution. It
has a hinterland it draws from and supplies, a legal regime that decides who may
trade and on what terms, a customs house, and a resident merchant community whose
credit and knowledge make it a market. Boundary rules already place ports in MAR.
MIG-060 carries the trading post as a form of colonisation with a small founding
population. This entry is about what the post did once it was a port, and why
powers fought over which port the trade used.

**A port taxes, transships and excludes.** Its holder levies customs on everything
that passes its quay. It is also the place where cargoes change hulls. Goods from
one monsoon system or one shipping network wait for another, so it accumulates
warehouses, brokers, credit and information. And it can refuse entry, or seize
ships that trade elsewhere. The first two make a port rich; the third makes it a
weapon.

**Protection selling enters an ocean that traded without it.** The western Indian
Ocean before 1500 had pirates and powerful port states. It had no general practice
of any state claiming to control the sea itself and charging for safe passage
through it. The Portuguese cartaz introduced exactly that: a pass, bought from the
Estado da Índia, without which a ship was liable to seizure. It obliged the holder
to call at Portuguese ports and pay duty there. This is WAR-020's protection rent
installed at sea by a power whose own warships were the threat being insured
against, and Steensgaard reads the whole Estado as a business in selling
protection rather than in trading goods. The Dutch refined it by fusing protection
with monopoly. The VOC's fortress ports and the destruction of rival production,
at Banda in 1621, let it capture the spice trade itself, not only a toll on it.

**A new entrepôt redirects trade; it does not add it.** Trade concentrates where
other traders already are, because that is where cargoes, credit and buyers are
waiting. So a region's transshipment tends toward one dominant port, and a rival
port wins not by producing more but by drawing that concentration away from the
incumbent. Its `entrepôt share` rises as the old port's falls, and a port that
loses its share can collapse within a generation. Malacca to Batavia to Singapore
is one route's worth of prosperity moving three times in three centuries. Hormuz
emptied after 1622 as trade went to Bandar Abbas. MAR-180 shows the same
displacement across a whole trading system when the Cape route stranded the
Venetian and Egyptian relay.

**Free ports win against taxing ports.** The counter-strategy to the cartaz and
the monopoly port is to charge nothing. Singapore's founding as a free port drew
trade away from Dutch ports that levied duties, and Hong Kong and Aden followed the
same model. A free port gives up customs revenue to maximise its `entrepôt share`,
and it earns through what concentration brings: warehousing, finance, shipping
services and, for Britain, strategic position.

**Treaty ports impose the same mechanism on a state never colonised.** When China
would not open its ports, the Opium War settlement opened them by force (COM-210).
The mechanisms were treaty ports with foreign concessions, fixed tariffs and
consular jurisdiction, all set in unequal treaties (DIP-140). Japan's 1858
treaties did the same to Yokohama and Nagasaki. A state kept its sovereignty over
its territory and lost it over its ports, and through the ports over the terms of
its trade.

## Social consequence

At household scale an entrepôt is a place of arrival and mixture. Singapore within
two generations of 1819 was a city of Chinese, Malay, Indian, Arab, Armenian and
European merchants and labourers living in quarters assigned by the port's
administration. Batavia's Chinese community was the city's commercial core, and
it was massacred by the VOC in 1740 when its position came to be seen as a threat.
Zanzibar's customs house, plantations and caravan houses drew Gujarati financiers,
Omani landholders, Swahili traders and enslaved workers from the mainland.

The port also decides fates beyond its walls. A hinterland whose outlet shifts from
one port to another reorganises around the new route. Pepper growers, caravan
leaders and weavers find buyers at a different coast. When a port loses its share,
its brokers, porters, boatmen and warehouse workers lose their livelihoods with it.
The people who lived by the displaced port bear the cost of the redirection.

## Regional specifics

The strongest cases are Asian and African, and the contest was never only between
Europeans.

- **Calicut and Hormuz** were thriving port states before the Portuguese
  arrived, and Hormuz shows how quickly trade abandons a port once its
  position is lost.
- **Aceh, Banten and Makassar** ran open entrepôts that competed with the
  European ports for a century. Makassar's ruler answered Dutch monopoly with
  a claim that the sea belonged to all, the same argument Grotius had made
  for the Dutch against the Portuguese, from which the law of the sea grew
  (MAR-205).
- **Oman** expelled the Portuguese from Muscat and Mombasa and built Zanzibar
  into East Africa's dominant port, winning the contest on the Swahili coast
  for two centuries.
- **Macau and Manila** existed because China and Japan restricted direct
  trade, and they profited from carrying what the states would not allow. The
  restriction was ritual as well as legal: Chinese markets opened only through
  tribute, the circuit DIP-075 carries.
- **Tokugawa Japan and Qing China** are the absence. Strong land states
  confined Europeans to Dejima and Guangzhou and kept the contest off their
  coasts until force broke it in 1842.

On the European side:

- **The Estado da Índia** introduced armed protection selling.
- **The VOC** fused protection with monopoly through Batavia.
- **Britain** built presidency ports that grew into cities, and then won the
  nineteenth century with free ports at Singapore, Hong Kong and Aden.

Where a port's position rested on the monsoon's timing, as Malacca's did, MAR-038
carries the physical reason. COM-150 carries the chartered companies that held most
of these ports. COM-130 carries control of the straits between them.

## Contested

How much the Portuguese and Dutch systems actually controlled Asian trade is
disputed. One reading, Steensgaard's, treats the Estado as a redistributive
protection enterprise and the Dutch as the first to profit by internalising
protection costs. Others show Asian shipping evading or buying into the system on
such a scale that the Red Sea spice trade revived within half a century.

Model port control as raising the cost of trading outside the system, not as
preventing it. The share captured should depend on the number of open alternative
ports within reach and on the holder's capacity to patrol between them.

## Hook

A port holds an `entrepôt share`, its fraction of a region's transshipment.
Trade concentrates toward the port where other traders already are, so share is
close to zero-sum across rival ports. A new entrepôt gains share by drawing it
from the incumbent, and a port that loses most of its share collapses. Holding a
port lets a power do three things:

- **Tax** everything crossing its quay.
- **Transship**, accumulating credit, warehousing and information.
- **Exclude or seize**, which lets a naval power levy `protection rent` on
  trade conducted elsewhere, as the Portuguese cartaz did.

Monopoly ports raise the cost of trading outside the system without preventing
it. Their captured share falls as open alternative ports within reach multiply and
as patrol capacity thins. Free ports trade customs revenue for share, and they beat
taxing rivals. Treaty ports impose the same mechanism by force on a state that
keeps its territory, transferring control of its trade terms without annexation. A
strong land state can deny the contest altogether by confining foreigners to one
port on its own terms, until military defeat removes that ability.
