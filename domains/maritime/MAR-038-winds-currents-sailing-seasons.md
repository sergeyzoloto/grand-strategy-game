The ocean is not uniform space. Before steam, a route is not a line between two
points. It is a seasonal window plus a fixed direction, and a ship that misses the
window waits months.

## Timing

From sail on the Nile in the later fourth millennium BCE to the steam transition
around 1870. Greek and Roman writers record the Mediterranean sailing season from
Hesiod onward. The monsoon crossings are documented by the first century CE. The
Portuguese Volta do Mar is worked out from the 1440s, the VOC's roaring-forties
route is mandated in 1616, and the Pacific return route is found in 1565. The
entry closes when compound-engined steamships and the Suez Canal make shipping a
timetabled service rather than a seasonal expedition. Weather still sinks ships
and closes seasons today, and modern ships are routed around it, but it no longer
decides which routes exist.

## Mechanism

**Winds and currents make routes directional and seasonal.** Prevailing winds,
ocean currents and storm seasons mean some passages can be sailed in only one
direction, some only at certain times of year, and some not at all under sail. A
route's capacity therefore depends on when and which way, not only on distance.
Its `sailing window` is the span of the year and the direction in which the
passage is possible at all, and it is a property of the sea rather than of the
ship. A better hull widens the window somewhat. It does not remove it.

**Consequence one: the monsoon clock turns trade into residence.** In the Indian
Ocean the wind reverses twice a year, so a merchant who sails to Calicut, Malacca
or Kilwa on one monsoon cannot leave until the next. Missing the return window
means staying a full year. That produces:

- **Long-stay merchant quarters.** Foreign traders lived for months or years
  in Guangzhou's and Quanzhou's foreign districts, Calicut's Arab community and
  Malacca's quarters.
- **Kinship ties that make stays manageable.** Mixed and temporary marriages
  gave visiting merchants households and local partners.
- **Creole port communities**, such as the Swahili coast and the Gujarati and
  Hadrami diasporas.
- **Settlement that becomes permanent** when the intended return never comes.

The social structure of the Indian Ocean trading world is a consequence of the
wind, and coastal trade (MAR-020) ran to this calendar long before any ship left
sight of land. MIG-070 carries trade diasporas as a network mechanism, and this is the
physical reason the maritime ones settled rather than commuted. It also places
markets. Malacca prospered because it sat where the two monsoons' shipping had to
wait for each other, which made it the prize in the later contest for entrepôts
(MAR-185).

**Consequence two: the counterintuitive route makes route knowledge an asset.** The
Volta do Mar requires sailing away from your destination to find the wind that
brings you back. No reasoning from a map finds it, only trial, loss and memory.
The Pacific return route defeated Spanish navigators for forty years before
Urdaneta found it. That makes `route knowledge` a real, hoardable, stealable asset
distinct from ship capability. A rival with equal ships and no knowledge of the
route cannot use it. Pilots, rutters and charts were guarded, bought and captured,
and a single experienced pilot could be worth more than a squadron. Ocean
navigation (MAR-160) depends on this knowledge as much as on hulls and
instruments, and position-finding (MAR-050) tells a navigator where the ship is,
not where the wind will let it go.

**Consequence three: war and trade have a weather calendar.** Caribbean hurricanes
from June to November, Indian Ocean cyclones around the turn of each monsoon, and
Western Pacific typhoons set seasons in which fleets are lost to weather more often
than to enemies. Spanish treasure fleets were timed around the hurricane season. A
West Indies campaign that ran late risked hurricane and the fever season together.
The Mongol invasion fleet of 1281 was destroyed by a typhoon. A naval power's
effective strength in a theatre is its fleet discounted by the weather calendar it
must operate inside.

**The traps.** Some zones stop sailing ships altogether. In the equatorial doldrums
ships could drift becalmed for weeks. The horse latitudes, the belt of calm around
the subtropical highs where the Sargasso Sea lies, held ships long enough to exhaust
water and fodder. Routes were laid out to cross these belts quickly or avoid them.

**Ports have tides and bars.** A river-mouth bar or a shallow tidal approach limits
which hulls can reach a port and when. As ships grew larger, ports that could not
admit them declined. Seville's Guadalquivir bar is the example: the Spanish
colonial trade office moved to Cádiz in 1717. Wet docks (MAR-240) are the
engineering answer to the tidal half of this constraint.

**Steam breaks the constraint.** A steamship sails against the wind and through the
calm on a schedule. Transatlantic steam from 1838, Cunard's scheduled mail service
from 1840, and Alfred Holt's compound-engined ships to China from 1866 turned
shipping from a seasonal expedition into timetabled service. Holt's engines burned
little enough coal to make the long run economic. The Suez Canal of 1869 sealed it,
because the Red Sea's contrary winds made the canal close to useless for sailing
ships and ideal for steam. The clipper (MAR-260) was sail's last answer. It ran
the charted wind belts faster than any sailing ship before it, in the same decades
steam power (ENG-170) was reaching the ocean, and it could not survive the
change. Regular passenger lines (MIG-180) are what the change made possible.

## Social consequence

At the scale of a working life the sailing season set the year. A Gujarati
merchant's household ran on the monsoon: departure, a season abroad, and return
with the reversal. Many merchants kept a second household at the far end. A port
town's population swelled and emptied with the wind, lodging and credit ran on
seasonal terms, and a harbour that was a crowded market in one month was quiet in
another.

The residence the wind forced produced the Indian Ocean's plural port societies:
Swahili towns with Arab, Persian, Indian and African ancestry; Chinese and Arab
quarters in Southeast Asian ports; the Portuguese casados who married locally and
stayed. Monsoon-bound trade made the stranger into a neighbour for months at a time,
and often for good, which is why the Indian Ocean's commercial world was so densely
cosmopolitan compared with trading systems that allowed quick round trips.

For sailors, knowledge of the wind was a career. Pilots who knew a route were hired,
lured away and sometimes captured by rival powers. Vasco da Gama took on a local
pilot at Malindi for the crossing to India. Crews carried the lore of when to sail
and when to wait, and a captain who ignored it could lose a ship to the season.

## Regional specifics

The Indian Ocean is the entry's centre. The monsoon's twice-yearly reversal
produced a trading system of long residence, mixed households and permanent
diasporas. It placed Malacca at the meeting point of two monsoons and closed the
Malabar coast in the height of the southwest winds.

The Pacific carries two cases:

- **The Spanish return route,** found only in 1565 after forty years of
  failure, which made the Manila galleon possible.
- **The typhoon calendar,** which destroyed the Mongol fleet off Japan.

Polynesian voyagers read Pacific winds, swells and stars without instruments, and
MAR-050 carries their wayfinding. Some reconstructions argue eastward settlement of
Remote Oceania exploited periodic spells of westerly wind that interrupted the
trades.

The Atlantic runs on the trades and the westerlies. The Volta do Mar and
Columbus's outward route on the northeast trades both depend on them, and the Gulf
Stream sped eastbound passages. The Caribbean adds the hurricane calendar that timed
Spain's fleets. The Mediterranean's closed winter season, recorded from Hesiod to
the Roman rule that closed the sea from November to March, is the same constraint
in a milder form. The roaring forties carried VOC ships to Java and wrecked them on
Australia. The Nile is the mechanism in miniature, with the wind upstream and the
current down.

The Americas show the absence. Without sail on the Caribbean and Atlantic side, the
wind-season structure did not organise routes until European ships brought it.

## Hook

Every sea route under sail has a `sailing window`, a seasonal span and a
direction in which passage is possible, set by winds, currents and storm seasons.
Distance alone does not define it, and ship quality does not remove it.

- **Missed windows** cost months, not days, so trade across reversing monsoon
  systems forces long residence abroad and produces merchant quarters, mixed
  households and permanent trade diasporas.
- **Counterintuitive routes** have to be found by trial and loss, so `route
  knowledge` becomes a separate, hoardable asset. Ships without the knowledge
  cannot use the route, and pilots can be hired, captured or lost.
- **Storm seasons** discount naval and merchant capacity in each theatre by
  calendar, so fleets are lost to weather in proportion to how far operations
  run into the season.
- **Calm belts** trap ships, and river bars and tides restrict which hulls reach
  which ports.

**Steam removes the constraint.** Once propulsion and fuel economy cross a
threshold, windows vanish and routes become timetables. That is the hinge from
seasonal expedition to scheduled service.
