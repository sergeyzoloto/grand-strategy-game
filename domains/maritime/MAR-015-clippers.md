A fast, narrow-hulled sailing ship trading cargo capacity for speed, built
specifically for trades where being first to market carries a large price
premium over simply carrying the most cargo.

## Timing

Clipper design and construction peaks from roughly 1840 through the 1860s,
driven substantially by the China tea trade and, in the earlier part of this
window, the California and Australian gold-rush passenger and cargo demand
(MIG-014), before rapid commercial obsolescence by the 1870s.

## Mechanism

A clipper's narrow hull and large sail area sacrifice cargo volume for
speed, a trade that only makes commercial sense where a specific market
pays a real premium for early arrival — the China tea trade's "first ship
home" premium is the paradigm case, rewarding speed directly and
disproportionately rather than paying a flat per-ton rate that would favor
a slower, higher-capacity vessel instead.

## Social consequence

The clipper era generates intense, closely-tracked ocean races (the "tea
race" home from China being the most famous) that become genuine public
spectacle and a point of national and commercial pride, converting a purely
commercial logistics question into celebrated competitive sport for the
brief window before steam makes the whole format commercially irrelevant.

## Regional specifics

The China tea trade's freshness-sensitive value structure is what makes the
clipper commercially viable in the first place — a bulk, non-perishable
commodity would never generate a comparable speed premium. The clipper's
subsequent obsolescence, driven specifically by the Suez Canal's opening
rather than by steamships simply becoming faster in open water, shows a
sailing ship's advantage can be erased suddenly by an infrastructure change
that alters the geometry of the best route entirely, rather than only by a
slow, incremental technology race.

## Hook

A clipper converts hull design (cargo capacity traded for speed) directly
into commercial value only where a market pays a real first-to-arrive
premium — a narrow, commodity-specific niche vulnerable to sudden
elimination by route-geometry-changing infrastructure (a canal) rather than
just by a faster competing ship type.
