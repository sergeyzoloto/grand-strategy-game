Where a river network with short portages connects two otherwise-separate
maritime trade zones, that network can substitute for an ocean route between
markets a purely coastal or open-water path would never directly connect.

## Timing

The Volga-Dnieper river-and-portage system operates as a continuous long-
distance trade corridor from roughly 800 through its gradual displacement by
more direct Mediterranean and later Atlantic routes by the 1500s-1600s.

## Mechanism

A river-and-portage route trades the higher speed and capacity of open-water
sailing for access to a corridor that reaches markets no coastal route
touches at all — the portage segments (dragging or rolling boats overland
between navigable stretches) are slow and labor-intensive, but they're what
lets the whole route exist, converting a chain of rivers into the functional
equivalent of a sea lane for goods (furs, slaves, silver) moving between the
Baltic and the Islamic Caliphate's silver-rich markets via the Caspian and
Black Seas.

## Social consequence

Control of portage points becomes as commercially and politically valuable
as control of a sea strait would be elsewhere, since the entire route's
throughput bottlenecks at exactly those short overland segments — the towns
and polities (early Rus principalities prominent among them) that control
key portages capture toll and protection revenue disproportionate to the
short physical distance involved.

## Regional specifics

The Volga-Dnieper system's role linking the Baltic fur and slave trade to
Abbasid silver markets makes it one of the clearest cases of a purely
riverine network substituting for a maritime one in this domain — the silver
dirham hoards found across the Baltic region are a direct material trace of
this route's function. The Ganges-Brahmaputra system's comparatively
regional rather than inter-zone-connecting role, despite comparable physical
scale, shows river size and navigability alone don't determine whether a
river network becomes this kind of long-distance connector — it also
requires two otherwise-separated markets with enough of a value differential
to justify the portage cost.

## Hook

A river-and-portage corridor extends `route knowledge`-based trade
connectivity to landlocked market pairs a coastal or open-ocean network
never reaches, at the cost of a slow, labor-intensive portage bottleneck that
concentrates toll and control value at those specific overland segments.
