The construction technique that turns timber, or in South Asian
traditions sewn and lashed planks, into a hull capable of carrying bulk
cargo or a fighting crew at sea — the substrate technology every other
entry in this domain silently assumes already exists.

## Timing

Egyptian and Mesopotamian plank-built river and coastal vessels are
attested from roughly -3000; Mediterranean shell-first construction
(planks fastened edge-to-edge first, frames added after) dominates
through antiquity; Norse clinker-building matures from around 800; South
Asian sewn-plank dhow construction and Chinese bulkhead-and-junk
construction develop on independent tracks, the latter reaching mature
form by around 1200.

## Mechanism

A hull's construction sequence and fastening method directly set its
practical size ceiling, durability against rot and worm damage, and how
easily it can be repaired far from its home port — shell-first
construction produces a strong, watertight hull well suited to
Mediterranean conditions but is labor-intensive to scale up; clinker
building trades some strength for lighter weight and shallower draft;
bulkhead compartmentalization trades some interior cargo flexibility for
survivability against a single hull breach. No technique is strictly
superior; each is a different bundle of tradeoffs that shapes what kind
of vessel, and therefore what kind of maritime activity, a given
tradition can build at all.

## Social consequence

Shipbuilding concentrates specialized, difficult-to-relocate skilled
labor — shipwrights, caulkers, timber selection and seasoning expertise —
in specific coastal or riverside locations with reliable timber supply,
making a shipyard's productive capacity a slow-to-build and slow-to-lose
regional asset in the same way a standing navy's trained crew is
(MAR-005): a state or city that loses its shipwright community cannot
simply rebuild hull-building capacity on demand.

## Regional specifics

Norse clinker construction's lighter, more flexible hull directly enables
the longship's shallow draft, which lets Viking raiders and traders
penetrate river systems (MAR-006) as readily as open coastline — a hull
design choice with a direct downstream effect on where a tradition's
vessels can actually go. Chinese bulkhead compartmentalization solves a
survivability problem — a single hull breach no longer means the whole
vessel floods — that Western shipbuilding doesn't adopt at comparable
scale until many centuries later, an independently superior solution to a
shared problem that doesn't transmit between traditions. Pre-contact
American watercraft achieve real sophistication within a different
technological envelope without reaching plank-built cargo volumes or
open-ocean durability, consistent with the coastal-trade and navigation
absence cases already noted elsewhere in this domain.

## Hook

Hull construction technique sets the ceiling on vessel size, durability,
and repairability that everything else in this domain is built on top of
— the substrate gate every coastal trade route (MAR-001) and every
warship tradition (MAR-002) silently assumes is already solved before
either can open.
