Cargo-carrying by sea along a coastline, staying within sight of or close to
land — cheaper and lower-risk per ton than any land transport alternative,
and the base layer nearly every other maritime mechanic in this domain
eventually builds on.

## Timing

Attested from the earliest Mediterranean and Mesopotamian riverine-to-coastal
trade (~-3000), with the Indian Ocean and East African coastal systems
developing on their own timelines from roughly -1000 and 800 respectively,
through to the ocean-crossing technology (MAR-008) that starts superseding
pure coastal cabotage as the dominant long-range trade mode from the 1500s.

## Mechanism

A loaded vessel moves bulk cargo at a fraction of the cost-per-ton-mile of an
overland pack animal or cart train, with the tradeoff of depending entirely
on coastline geography, seasonal weather, and (until MAR-003/MAR-007 mature)
staying within reach of familiar landmarks — coastal trade is the version of
this mechanic available to any society with basic seaworthy vessels and does
not require the navigation technology that later unlocks open-ocean crossing.

## Social consequence

Port towns positioned at natural coastal chokepoints or resource-transition
points (river mouths, strait narrows, monsoon turnaround points) accumulate
disproportionate wealth and cosmopolitan population relative to their
immediate hinterland, becoming commercially important out of proportion to
their agricultural or political weight — a pattern that recurs independently
in the Mediterranean, the Swahili coast, and the Indian Ocean rim alike.

## Regional specifics

The Indian Ocean system's dependence on a reversing monsoon wind calendar
(sail one direction for months, then wait for the wind to reverse) shapes an
entirely different trade rhythm from the Mediterranean's more evenly
distributed conditions, without either system being more "advanced" than the
other. The Swahili coast's specific role as an intermediary layer between the
African interior and the wider Indian Ocean network shows coastal trade
doesn't require a society to be either the ultimate source or destination of
what it carries. Pre-contact American coastal networks staying regionally
bounded rather than scaling into a comparable long-range system most plausibly
reflects a vessel and sail-technology gap (MAR-007's absence case) rather than
any lack of coastline or commercial motive.

## Hook

Coastal trade grants any settlement with a viable harbor access to `route
knowledge`-driven bulk-cargo trade at land-transport-beating cost, gated only
by basic seaworthy vessel technology — the entry point every longer-range
maritime mechanic in this domain (ocean navigation, regular shipping lines)
later builds on top of.
