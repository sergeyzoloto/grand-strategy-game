The specific limiting factor MAR-160's own account of ocean navigation
names but never explains: a shipboard diet lacking vitamin C becomes
lethal to a large fraction of a crew well before a vessel's food and
water stores would otherwise run out on a long ocean passage.

## Timing

Vasco da Gama's 1497-1499 voyage loses over half its crew, disproportionately
to scurvy, establishing the disease as the defining hazard of long-distance
ocean voyaging from the opening of the age of exploration; James Lind's
1747 controlled trial identifies citrus as an effective treatment; the
Royal Navy's 1795 mandate of a daily lime or lemon juice ration is the
first systematic institutional fix.

## Mechanism

Scurvy results from prolonged vitamin C deprivation, which onboard diets
of salted meat, hardtack, and dried goods — the standard provisions a
vessel can store for months without spoiling, per MAR-160's own account —
supply essentially none of. The disease becomes lethal past roughly ten
to twelve weeks without fresh provisions, capping effective ocean-voyage
duration independent of how much food and water a hull can physically
carry. A cheap, storable dietary fix (concentrated citrus juice) removes
this cap entirely once identified and systematically issued, without
requiring any change to the ship, its navigation, or its cargo capacity
at all.

## Social consequence

Scurvy's toll falls on ordinary crew rather than officers in any
structurally distinct way, but its cumulative effect — a long voyage
returning with half or fewer of its original crew able to work the ship —
directly threatens a voyage's ability to complete at all, making crew
health an operational constraint navies and merchant companies had to
plan around long before germ theory or any formal nutritional science
existed to explain why the fix worked.

## Regional specifics

The Indian Ocean's monsoon-timed trade calendar (MAR-020), with its
regular seasonal port calls and shorter coastal-to-coastal legs, rarely
exposes crews to the uninterrupted multi-month duration that makes
scurvy a major operational risk on Atlantic and Pacific crossings — a
structural difference in voyage length and provisioning opportunity, not
a difference in dietary knowledge between traditions.

## Hook

A vitamin-deficiency disease caps effective ocean-voyage duration
independent of a vessel's actual food and water storage capacity, until
a cheap and simple dietary fix removes the cap — a gate distinct from
MAR-160's own three components, since here voyage length is limited by
crew survival rather than by cargo, navigation, or vessel range.
