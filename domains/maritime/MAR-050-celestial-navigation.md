Determining position and heading by the fixed and predictable motion of
stars, sun, and moon rather than by landmarks — the technique that first
makes leaving sight of the coast a calculated risk rather than a fatal one.

## Timing

Instrumented Old World celestial navigation (using the astrolabe and later
the kamal) develops from roughly -600 through continuous refinement to 1500;
Polynesian non-instrumented wayfinding is independently attested from at
least -1500 and arguably considerably earlier.

## Mechanism

Any celestial navigation technique substitutes a predictable, universally
available reference (star position, sun angle) for the landmark-dependence
that otherwise caps a vessel's safe range at coastal cabotage (MAR-020) —
the specific instrument or method varies enormously by tradition, but the
underlying trade is the same: replace local, learned landmark knowledge with
a portable, teachable, sky-based method that works anywhere on Earth.

## Social consequence

Celestial navigation knowledge concentrates in a specialized, often
hereditary or guild-transmitted navigator class whose expertise is
difficult to verify from outside and correspondingly highly valued — a
society's oceanic reach becomes directly bottlenecked on how many trained
navigators it has and how well that knowledge transmits across generations,
independent of how many ships it can otherwise build.

## Regional specifics

Polynesian wayfinding achieves reliable open-ocean voyaging across the
Pacific's vastly greater distances centuries ahead of comparable Old World
instrumented methods, using an entirely non-instrumented, orally-transmitted
system built on star paths, wave and swell patterns, and bird behavior — a
technique that leaves no instrument in the archaeological record and was
consequently underrated by outside observers for a long time relative to its
actual sophistication. Arab Indian Ocean navigators' kamal and
star-altitude techniques feed directly into the instrument tradition later
Iberian navigators adopt and extend for Atlantic and eventually global
oceanic navigation (MAR-160) — a direct technical lineage often
underattributed to its Middle Eastern origin point in popular accounts of
the "Age of Exploration."

## Hook

Celestial navigation converts open-ocean travel from landmark-dependent
cabotage into calculated long-range voyaging, gated on a trained-navigator
population rather than on fleet size — a `route knowledge` unlock that,
per the Polynesian case, doesn't require any instrument at all to reach real
sophistication.
