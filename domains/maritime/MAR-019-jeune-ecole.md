A French naval doctrine arguing that cheap, fast torpedo boats and
commerce-raiding cruisers could neutralize a wealthier rival's expensive
battle fleet at a fraction of the cost — a direct strategic challenge to the
capital-ship logic MAR-010 and its successors assume.

## Timing

Develops as official French naval doctrine through the 1870s-1880s, peaking
in institutional influence in the mid-1880s before falling out of favor by
the 1890s as its underlying technology proved not yet decisive enough to
validate the doctrine at scale.

## Mechanism

The doctrine bets that a large number of cheap torpedo boats can inflict
disabling or sinking damage on a small number of expensive battleships at a
much lower cost ratio than the battleships cost to build, converting naval
competition from expensive capital-ship parity into an asymmetric
cost-imposition strategy — a bet that depends entirely on torpedo range,
reliability, and boat seaworthiness being good enough to actually land
effective attacks against a well-defended battle fleet, which 1880s
technology (MAR-023) wasn't quite there yet.

## Social consequence

The doctrine's rise and fall directly shapes French naval procurement and
international naval treaty anxiety for a generation, and its underlying
logic — cheap asymmetric capability threatening expensive concentrated
capital ships — recurs as a live strategic question every time a new cheap
weapon system threatens to devalue an expensive existing one, well beyond
this specific French episode.

## Regional specifics

Meiji Japan's explicit study and rejection of the Jeune École in favor of a
conventional battleship-centered fleet, vindicated at Tsushima's decisive
fleet action in 1905, shows a rising naval power correctly judging that its
own strategic situation (needing to win a decisive battle against a
comparable rival fleet, not merely to threaten a wealthier power's commerce)
called for the opposite doctrine — the same available strategic menu
producing opposite adoption decisions depending on the adopting power's
actual strategic goal.

## Contested

Whether the Jeune École reflected sound strategic insight simply ahead of
its enabling technology, or a confused overreaction to a real but overstated
vulnerability, is debated. Model it as a genuine, partially-correct
strategic bet whose payoff is conditional on torpedo and submarine
technology maturity (MAR-023, MAR-024) at time of adoption — failing as an
immediate battle-fleet substitute in the 1880s, but vindicated in essence
once WWI submarine commerce-raiding proves the same cheap-asymmetric-threat
logic decisively effective a generation later.

## Hook

A cheap-flotilla doctrine challenging expensive capital-ship dominance is a
real strategic bet whose success is gated on underlying weapon-technology
maturity (MAR-023) rather than doctrine alone — the same bet can fail
early and succeed later purely as the enabling technology catches up,
independent of the doctrine's own internal logic changing at all.
