The shift from assembling warships and crews for a specific campaign to
maintaining a standing fleet, trained crews, and a permanent administrative
apparatus between wars — a state-capacity threshold, not just a shipbuilding
one.

## Timing

Athenian and later Roman standing naval establishments are early
Mediterranean cases from roughly -500; the Chola navy's standing
establishment (from ~1000) predates the specific European institutional
model (England's Navy Royal administrative reforms, from 1546) that becomes
the most thoroughly documented case of the type.

## Mechanism

A standing navy requires continuous fiscal commitment — paying and training
crews, maintaining hulls against rot and worm damage, storing and replacing
rigging and ordnance — during peacetime years when there's no immediate
campaign to justify the expense, which is a fundamentally different and
harder sustained-cost problem than assembling an ad hoc fleet for a single
war and disbanding it afterward. This converts naval capability from
something that can be improvised quickly given enough money into something
that takes years of continuous institutional investment to build and, once
lost, takes years to rebuild.

## Social consequence

A standing navy creates a distinct professional class (career officers, a
permanent skilled labor force in dockyards and rope-walks, seniority and
patronage systems for advancement) with interests and internal culture of
its own, tied to the institution's continuity in a way an ad hoc levied
fleet's temporary crews never developed.

## Regional specifics

The Chola case shows the standing-navy model isn't a Mediterranean or later
European invention but recurs wherever a state values sustained rather than
episodic naval reach. England's Navy Royal reforms are notable specifically
for institutionalizing the administrative apparatus (a dedicated managing
board) rather than just the fleet itself, a distinction that matters because
it's the administrative continuity, not the ships, that lets naval capacity
survive the death of any one monarch or minister. Ming China's dismantled
treasure fleet is the clearest absence case in this record of technical and
fiscal capacity existing without the institutional commitment to sustain it
— a demonstration that a regular navy is a political choice to keep paying,
not merely a question of whether a state can afford one.

## Hook

A regular navy converts naval capability from a fast-buildable, fast-losable
campaign asset into a slow-building, slow-losing state-capacity stock — the
sustained fiscal and administrative commitment matters more than any single
shipbuilding program, and its absence (Ming China) can coexist with more than
enough wealth and technique to build one.
