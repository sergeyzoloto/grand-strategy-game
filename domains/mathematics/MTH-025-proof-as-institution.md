A claim established by deduction requires no observation and does not decay.
That makes mathematics the only body of knowledge whose results are
permanent, and the institution this entry owns is the shared standard that
decides what counts as a demonstration, who can check it, and how it is
carried forward.

## Timing

From Greek deductive geometry around -500 and Euclid's Elements around -300,
through Liu Hui's commentary in 263, the Indian upapatti tradition, the Arabic
Elements from around 800, and its return to Latin Europe around 1120, to the
nineteenth-century rigour movement that made proof the professional standard
of a research discipline.

## Mechanism

**Proof makes a result permanent, and permanence makes knowledge
accumulate.** An observational result is provisional — NAT-015's astronomical
tables drift, must be recalibrated against the sky, and can be refuted by the
next eclipse. A proved result cannot be refuted by anything that happens, so
it can be built on indefinitely. That is why a body of mathematics grows
rather than turning over: a theorem from Euclid is still a theorem, and the
work of every later mathematician rests on it without re-checking it against
the world. The contrast with NAT-015 is exactly the boundary the MTH carve-out
draws.

**Proof moves authority from the person to the demonstration.** A proved
result does not depend on who proved it, because anyone who has learned the
standard can check it. That makes mathematical knowledge portable across
languages, traditions and centuries in a way almost no other knowledge is —
a proof written in Greek is checkable by a reader in Baghdad or Kerala who has
never met a Greek. `intellectual authority` attaches to the standard rather
than to the author, which is a different social structure from a school
following its founder.

**The institution is the standard, and there is more than one.** Greek
deduction from axioms, Indian upapatti attached to rules, and Chinese
commentary justifying procedures all produce correct, permanent results.
They differ in what they rest on and therefore in what they make portable:
an axiomatic system transmits whole and can be extended by pure deduction,
but a single flaw at its base reaches everything (MTH-070); a standard that
justifies each rule separately transmits piecemeal and is immune to that
failure. Neither is the more advanced. They distribute rigour differently.

**Permanence accumulates latent capability.** Because proved results never
expire, they can sit unused for any length of time and still be correct when a
use arrives. Apollonius's conic sections waited eighteen centuries to become
Kepler's planetary orbits. `latent capability` is the stat this generates, and
design principle 9 is its consequence.

## Social consequence

Losing the manuscripts loses access to proved knowledge, not its truth, which
is a different vulnerability from NAT-015's destructible archive. Latin Europe
for six centuries had the statements of Euclid's propositions without the
proofs, and the result was not false geometry but geometry held on authority
rather than by demonstration. The standard itself can be lost while the
results survive, and that is the loss that matters.

The institution also creates a specific kind of person and a specific kind of
teaching. Learning a proof means re-deriving it, so mathematical education is
practice in checking rather than memorising, and a student who has done it
has acquired the standard as well as the result. That makes mathematics
unusually resistant to appeals to authority inside its own domain, and
unusually prestigious outside it — the demonstrative form is borrowed by
theology, law and philosophy precisely because it seems to compel assent.

## Regional specifics

The Islamic world is the institution's main carrier, not a relay: the Arabic
Elements was translated around 800, taught, commented on and criticised for
four centuries, and returned to Latin Europe with its proofs from Arabic around
1120. Indian upapatti and the Kerala Yuktibhasa show a rigorous standard
organised around rules rather than axioms. Liu Hui's commentary tradition shows
demonstration organised around the justification of procedures. The Greek
axiomatic form is the one that was fixed in a single transmitted text, and the
nineteenth-century rigour movement is its professionalisation. Latin Europe's
early medieval centuries are the genuine absence — the results without the
proofs, which is to say the standard missing and the content present.

## Contested

Whether proof is one category is disputed, and the entry follows the view that
Indian and Chinese traditions had demonstrations with their own criteria rather
than lacking proof. The permanence claim holds for results relative to a
standard; standards change slowly, as Cauchy's generation rejecting their
predecessors' arguments and the disputed status of computer-assisted proof
both show.

## Hook

Makes results permanent, so knowledge accumulates instead of turning over —
the defining difference from NAT-015's drifting tables. Authority should
attach to the standard of demonstration rather than to the author, making
proved knowledge portable across languages and traditions. Several standards
should be available and none ranked above another: axiomatic deduction
transmits whole and fails catastrophically at its base (MTH-070), rule-by-rule
justification transmits piecemeal and cannot fail that way. Every proved
result should add to `latent capability`, correct indefinitely and usable
whenever a later domain supplies an application. And losing texts should lose
access, not truth, while losing the standard itself — results kept, proofs
gone — should degrade knowledge into authority.
