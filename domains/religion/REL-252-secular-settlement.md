The state stops enforcing religious truth and starts enforcing civil order
among religions. This is not the decline of religion but a renegotiation of
what the state guarantees — and it comes in several institutionally distinct
forms, not one.

## Timing

From the Westphalian protection of private minority worship in 1648, through
toleration acts, American disestablishment in 1791, English emancipation in
1828-29, the Ottoman Tanzimat of 1839-56, the Mexican Reform of 1855-63, and
the Meiji constitution of 1889, to the French separation law of 1905. The
varieties run concurrently rather than in sequence — the partition model and
the separation model develop in the same decades, and neither is a stage on the
way to the other.

## Mechanism

**Uniformity is affordable only while the population is uniform.** The
confessional state (REL-220) makes confession a condition of political loyalty,
which works as a legibility and loyalty-sorting device exactly as long as the
wrong-confession minority is small, expellable, or convertible. Once it is
large, entrenched, and economically necessary, the device produces permanent
internal friction instead of loyalty, and toleration (REL-040) begins as a
pragmatic concession to that cost. The settlement is the point at which the
concession becomes the constitution: the state faces a choice between perpetual
internal conflict and detaching citizenship from confession, and takes the
second.

**What the state gives up is a legitimation source.** A confessional state is
legitimate because it defends the true faith; a secular settlement's state
cannot claim that. It gains lower confessional conflict and a larger population
that can be loyal to it, and it pays with the loss of `religious authority` as
something the state can draw on. The deficit must be filled from somewhere, and
the historical substitutes are the nation (DIP-160), whose Romantic vocabulary
of the people as a real collective (PHI-310) arrives in exactly this period;
civil religion, Rousseau's term, whose most literal attempt is the Cult of the
Supreme Being in 1794 and whose most durable one is the American flag and
founding texts; and, where neither takes, a monarchy or party that must find
its legitimacy in performance. **The settlement and nationalism are two halves
of one transaction** — one takes religion out of the state's claim to rule, the
other puts something back.

**The varieties are different institutional objects, not degrees of one.**

- **Disestablishment** — no state church and no public funding. Religion
  becomes voluntary association, and religious organisations must recruit.
  The American outcome is a religious market with rising participation.
- **Laïcité** — the state actively excludes religion from its own
  institutions, above all the school. It is a settlement reached through
  confrontation with a church identified with the old regime, in France and
  Mexico alike, and it tends to be preceded by a fight over who educates
  children.
- **Established but tolerant** — the state church survives as ceremony and
  loses its power over civil status. It keeps symbolic legitimation while
  surrendering coercion, which is the cheapest settlement where the
  established church is not a political enemy of the state.
- **Partition of jurisdiction** — the millet and plural-personal-law model.
  Communities keep authority over their members' marriage, divorce and
  inheritance while the state governs everything else. This is not a partial
  or failed separation. It solves the same problem — how one state holds
  several confessions without war — by dividing the legal person rather than
  privatising belief, and LAW-040 carries its personal-status machinery. It
  preserves communal `religious authority` over the household at full
  strength, it is compatible with formal equality in public law, and it can
  be turned into a citizenship barrier, as French Algeria shows.

Which variety a state gets is set by prior institutional state rather than
chosen (design principle 1). A plural society with no dominant church to
disestablish gets disestablishment by default. A state whose established church
fought on the losing side of a revolution gets laïcité. An established church
with small, incorporable minorities gets toleration within establishment. A
polity that already governed through communal jurisdictions, or a colonial
state unwilling to legislate for its subjects' households, gets partition.

**Legibility moves with it.** The parish register and the church court were how
the confessional state knew who was born, married, and dead (REL-210). Every
secular settlement except the partition model builds a civil registry and civil
marriage to replace them, so the settlement also transfers a record-keeping
capability from church to state — France in 1792, England in 1836-37, Mexico in
1859 — and that transfer is the first of several dateable functional losses
religious institutions sustain after the settlement.

## Social consequence

At household scale the settlement is concrete and legal rather than doctrinal.
Before it, a dissenter could not hold office, a mixed marriage might be void, a
child's legal existence ran through a baptism, and a nonconformist could be
refused burial by the parish. After it, a family can be of the wrong faith and
still marry, register, inherit, vote, and bury its dead through the state. In
the partition model the household experience runs the other way: the family's
most intimate law remains its community's, so a woman's divorce rights or a
daughter's inheritance share depend on which community the state has assigned
her to.

Religious institutions change character. Once the state stops guaranteeing
them, their authority rests on what members give voluntarily — attendance,
donation, obedience to discipline — and they must win it. That is the condition
later competition for schools and media depends on. Where the settlement
came through confrontation, the church also becomes a political party in all but
name, and religious identity becomes a line in electoral politics rather than a
condition of belonging to the polity.

The settlement also makes the confiscation of church wealth (REL-250) routine
policy rather than an extraordinary act. Mexico's nationalisation of church
property and the 1905 French transfer of church buildings to public ownership
are the same seizure, carried out by statute inside a settled constitutional
order rather than by a dissolution.

## Regional specifics

The American case supplies the entry's most consequential finding: separation
did not reduce religious participation but increased it. Adherence is estimated
to have risen from roughly a sixth of the population at independence to over
half by the late twentieth century (Finke and Stark, _The Churching of America
1776-1990_, 1992, ch. 2 — estimates built from denominational records and
disputed in detail, not in direction). A state that stops funding a church
creates a market, and markets recruit.

France and Mexico are the confrontational laïcité pair. In both the church was
identified with a defeated order, the fight ran through property, registration
and schools, and the settlement was legislated as a victory rather than
negotiated. England is the establishment-with-toleration case, in which a series
of repeals over half a century removed coercion without removing the church.

The partition model is what keeps this from being a Western entry, and it should
carry equal weight. The Ottoman Tanzimat reorganised communal jurisdiction
rather than abolishing it, pairing civic equality in public law with communal
authority in household law — a design that outlives the empire in Lebanon's
confessional personal-status courts and Israel's rabbinical jurisdiction over
marriage. British India codified the same division and independent India kept
it, reforming one community's law by statute while leaving another's in place.
French Algeria shows the model's darkest use, with personal status deciding who
could hold citizenship.

Meiji Japan shows the settlement's forms being imported and then turned against
their purpose: formal freedom of religion made room for a compulsory state rite
by classifying it as not religious at all (REL-260). PHI-280 carries the
Lockean and Weberian argument for separating religious and political authority
as a philosophical claim; this entry owns the legal settlements themselves, and
DIP-100 owns the interstate half of the 1648 package, in which religious choice
became a matter of internal sovereignty.

The absences mark where the mechanism's cost never fell due. Saudi Arabia kept a
confessional basis over a population without a large recognised minority, and
Iran reversed a partial settlement in 1979. Qing China never attached loyalty to
confession, so there was nothing to detach.

## Contested

The settlement's own founding narrative — that confessional war proved religion
too dangerous for politics and the neutral state ended it — is disputed.
Cavanaugh argues the wars of religion were substantially state-building wars and
that the story of religious violence tamed by secular authority is the secular
state's legitimating myth. If that is right, the settlement does not reduce
political violence so much as relocate it into national and ideological
conflict, carried by the substitute legitimation the settlement itself requires.

Model both. The settlement reliably lowers confessional conflict between groups
inside the polity — that is well supported. Whether total political violence
falls, or moves into national conflict, should be conditional on whether a
substitute legitimation forms and how exclusive it is: an inclusive civic
nationalism or civil religion lowers it, and an ethnic or confessionally-coded
nationalism reproduces the confessional state's loyalty-sorting under a new
name.

## Hook

A constitutional transition triggered when enforcing confessional uniformity
costs more than it returns — a large, entrenched, economically necessary
minority, the durability condition REL-040 sets for toleration, crossed from
policy into constitution. The variety is set by prior institutional state, not
chosen: a plural society with no dominant church gets disestablishment and a
competitive religious market; a church allied to a defeated regime gets
laïcité and a fight over schools; an established church with incorporable
minorities gets toleration inside establishment; existing communal
jurisdictions get partition, which keeps communal `religious authority` over
household law at full strength and can gate citizenship. Every variety lowers
confessional conflict and cuts the state's own draw on `religious authority` —
fully under disestablishment and laïcité, partly under establishment, not at all
inside partitioned communities — which opens a legitimation deficit that
nationalism or civil religion must fill, and an unfilled deficit is instability.
Separation variants also move `legibility` from church to state through civil
registration and civil marriage. The settlement is revocable, not a threshold:
it can be reversed where a state re-attaches office to confession.
