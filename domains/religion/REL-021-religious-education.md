A dedicated institution for training clergy, jurists, and scholars converts religious knowledge transmission from informal apprenticeship into a systematic, credentialing pipeline — the trained personnel this domain's canon-fixing councils, courts, and missionary corps all silently assume are already available.

## Timing

Jewish yeshiva academies for Talmudic study develop from around 220 with the founding of the Sura and Nehardea academies in Babylonia, maturing institutionally through the medieval period; European cathedral schools formalize from the Carolingian period (from around 800), eventually feeding into the university system; the Islamic madrasa network scales sharply from the Nizamiyya foundations of 1065.

## Mechanism

Before a dedicated religious-education institution exists, training a jurist, judge, or scholar depends on informal, personally-arranged apprenticeship to an individual established teacher — a madrasa, yeshiva, or cathedral school instead offers a standardized curriculum and, in the Islamic case, a formal credential (the ijaza) that lets a student's qualification be recognized and verified beyond the specific teacher who trained them, converting religious learning from a purely personal transmission relationship into a portable, institutionally-verifiable credential.

## Social consequence

A standardized religious-education pipeline produces a recognizable professional class — jurists, judges, scholar-officials — whose social standing rests on institutional credential and demonstrated learning rather than birth or wealth, a status-formation mechanism structurally similar to LAW-004's later secular examination bureaucracy but organized around religious rather than administrative learning specifically, and considerably predating it in several of the traditions covered here.

## Regional specifics

The Nizamiyya madrasa network's standardized curriculum and formal ijaza credentialing system, scaling across the Seljuk empire from 1065, directly supplies the trained scholarly personnel NAT-005's translation movements and REL-012's qadi courts both depend on to function at all. Chinese Buddhist and Daoist education's reliance on individual temple-lineage apprenticeship rather than a comparable cross-institutional credentialing network shows systematic religious training doesn't require a madrasa-or-cathedral-school-style shared institutional system to produce well-trained clergy — a real alternative organizational logic, not a less-developed version of the same one.

## Hook

A gate: the credentialing pipeline that produces the judges (REL-012), canon-fixing councils (REL-002), and missionary corps (REL-014) this domain's other entries assume already exist, trained and available — converting religious learning from personal apprenticeship into a portable, institutionally-verifiable credential for the first time.
