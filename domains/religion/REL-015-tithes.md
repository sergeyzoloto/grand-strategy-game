This entry owns the tithe specifically as a religious institution's own
revenue-collection mechanism — funding clergy, buildings, and church
operations; LAW-015 already covers zakat's parallel but functionally
distinct redistributive-welfare purpose.

## Timing

Church tithing is attested in Frankish practice from the 6th century
(formalized further under Charlemagne from 779), becoming a widely
enforced, legally-backed obligation across Catholic Europe by around 1200.

## Mechanism

A tithe fixes clergy and church-building funding as a standard proportion
(traditionally one-tenth) of a parishioner's agricultural or other income,
converting church revenue from dependent on voluntary generosity or
ad hoc patronage into a predictable, assessable, and — once backed by
secular legal enforcement — compulsory obligation, giving the church
institution a revenue base with a stability more typical of state
taxation than of charitable giving.

## Social consequence

A legally-enforced tithe makes the church a direct, unavoidable claimant
on ordinary household income alongside secular taxation and feudal
obligation, which is part of why tithe collection recurringly becomes a
source of real social friction and, at times, open resistance — the
church's revenue security comes directly at the cost of parishioner
disposable income in a way voluntary giving never would.

## Regional specifics

Chinese Buddhist monastic funding through occasion-linked voluntary dana
achieves comparable institutional revenue stability through an entirely
different mechanism — variable voluntary giving tied to specific
merit-generating moments rather than a fixed, universally compulsory
periodic rate — showing institutional religious funding doesn't require
the tithe's specific compulsory-and-fixed structure to succeed at scale.
Islamic zakat, covered in this record's law domain for its welfare-
redistribution function, operates on a comparable fixed-percentage
calculation but channels the resulting revenue toward specified categories
of need rather than toward general institutional operating funds — the
same basic mandatory-levy mechanism, purposed toward two genuinely
different ends.

## Hook

Fixing clergy and institutional funding as a compulsory, legally-
enforceable percentage of income converts church revenue from dependent
on voluntary generosity into something closer to state taxation in its
reliability — a stability that comes directly at parishioner expense, and
one that other traditions (Buddhist dana, Islamic zakat, see LAW-015)
achieve through structurally different giving mechanisms purposed toward
different ends.
