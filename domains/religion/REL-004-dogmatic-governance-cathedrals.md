The vault and dome construction technique that makes a cathedral physically
possible is ENG-004's; this entry owns the cathedral as an administrative
seat — the physical anchor of a bishop's doctrinal and jurisdictional
authority over a diocese, not merely an impressive building.

## Timing

Following Constantine's 313 recognition of Christianity, cathedral-centered
diocesan administration develops and formalizes across the following
centuries, with the network reaching its fullest medieval institutional
elaboration by around 1350.

## Mechanism

A cathedral is the literal seat (cathedra, the bishop's chair) from which
binding doctrinal and administrative authority radiates outward across a
defined territory — the building's location and scale directly signal and
enable the bishop's jurisdictional reach, converting doctrinal governance
from something requiring the bishop's constant physical presence
everywhere into something administered from one fixed, legible center with
subordinate parish structure (REL-011) extending its reach.

## Social consequence

Building a cathedral requires enormous, often generations-long capital and
labor investment from the surrounding community and diocese, which is why
cathedral construction functions simultaneously as a governance statement
and a durable civic project drawing in resources and labor well beyond
what the strictly administrative function requires — the building itself
becomes a source of local identity and pride independent of its doctrinal-
authority role.

## Regional specifics

The Islamic kulliye complex achieves a comparable function — a fixed,
endowed institutional center anchoring a city's religious governance,
education, and charity — through a bundled multi-purpose complex rather
than centering authority on a single bishop's specific administrative
seat, showing the underlying need (a fixed physical anchor for religious
authority and services) can be organizationally structured quite
differently while serving the same governance function. Chinese state
oversight of Buddhist and Daoist temple administration, with religious
governance delegated by and answerable to the state rather than operating
as an independent parallel hierarchy, shows the cathedral-diocese model's
autonomous-authority structure is a specific institutional choice, not
the only way religious governance can be organized.

## Hook

A cathedral anchors doctrinal and administrative authority to one fixed,
legible physical center, letting a bishop govern a whole diocese without
needing constant personal presence — `religious authority` radiating
outward from architecture (ENG-004's territory) rather than requiring the
authority-holder's continuous physical movement to remain effective.
