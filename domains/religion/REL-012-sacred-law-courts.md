This entry owns the religious court as an institution — who staffs it,
what trains and legitimates its judges, and how it relates to secular
legal authority; LAW-001 already covers the Manusmriti's substantive
content as social-hierarchy codification, a distinct angle from the
court-institution question this entry addresses.

## Timing

Islamic qadi courts administering Sharia develop from shortly after 622;
Christian canon law courts formalize substantially through the medieval
period, reaching sophisticated jurisdictional structure by 1200; Jewish
beth din courts and Hindu dharmashastra-trained judiciary operate on their
own long, overlapping timelines across the same broad era.

## Mechanism

A sacred-law court derives its legitimacy and its judges' authority from
religious learning and institutional standing rather than from a secular
ruler's direct appointment, which is precisely what lets it operate with
real independence from, and sometimes in active jurisdictional competition
with, secular courts covering overlapping subject matter — the same
underlying dispute (a marriage contract, an inheritance claim) can
potentially be heard in either forum depending on the parties' status and
strategic choice, with real consequences for which substantive law and
which sentencing norms actually apply.

## Social consequence

Where religious and secular courts compete for the same jurisdiction,
litigants gain a strategic choice of forum that can significantly affect
their outcome — a cleric facing serious secular charges claiming trial in
the more lenient church court, or a merchant preferring a qadi court's
commercial-law expertise over a secular alternative — converting court
selection itself into part of the legal strategy rather than a fixed,
predetermined matter.

## Regional specifics

Dharmashastra-trained judges make the South Asian case distinctive: not
just the substantive law but the court and judicial-training institution
itself is thoroughly religious, with no comparably developed separate
secular judicial track running alongside it the way medieval Europe
maintains both canon and secular court systems in active parallel and
competition. Europe's genuine jurisdictional overlap and forum-shopping
dynamic between church and secular courts has no clean equivalent in
traditions where religious legal authority is either the sole judicial
apparatus (much of South Asia's case) or fully fused with secular rule
(Islamic qadi courts operating as the state's own judicial system rather
than a rival to it).

## Hook

A sacred-law court's independent religious legitimacy is exactly what lets
it operate alongside, and sometimes in active competition with, secular
courts over the same subject matter — converting `religious authority`
into a genuine litigation strategy variable (which forum, which
substantive law) wherever both systems coexist, rather than a fixed
background fact about which court has jurisdiction.
