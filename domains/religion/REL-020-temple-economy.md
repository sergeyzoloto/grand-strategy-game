Before formal banking (FIN-100) or even reliable coinage (COM's money-
adjacent territory), the temple is often the most secure, most trusted, and
most administratively capable institution available for storing surplus,
extending credit, and organizing large-scale labor.

## Timing

Sumerian temple estates managing grain reserves and organized labor are
attested from roughly -3000; South Indian temple economies reach comparable
or greater relative scale and persistence, accumulating endowments that
remain economically significant into the 1800s.

## Mechanism

A temple's ritual authority gives it a trust advantage no purely secular
storehouse could match in an era without reliable secular contract
enforcement — depositing grain, precious metal, or land with the temple
carries the implicit sanction of divine consequence for misappropriation,
which is what lets temples function as de facto banks, landlords, and
employers well before secular institutions develop comparable trust
mechanisms of their own.

## Social consequence

Temple wealth and administrative capacity give the priesthood real
economic leverage over the surrounding population independent of, and
sometimes in tension with, secular political authority — a temple's grain
reserves in a bad harvest year, or its willingness to extend credit, can
matter as much to ordinary survival as anything the state itself provides,
concentrating a form of soft power in religious institutions that persists
long after more sophisticated secular financial institutions eventually
develop.

## Regional specifics

South Indian Hindu temples reach a scale and duration of economic
significance comparable to or exceeding the earliest Mesopotamian cases,
functioning as major regional lenders, landholders, and employers for over
a millennium rather than being a feature only of the earliest literate
civilizations. Delphi's treasury shows a genuine alternative scale: real
wealth and pan-Hellenic financial trust without ever becoming a major
direct agricultural landholder and employer the way the Near Eastern and
South Indian temple economies do — the underlying trust mechanism is the
same, but how far it extends into the broader economy varies enormously by
tradition.

## Hook

Temple ritual authority substitutes for absent secular contract
enforcement, letting the priesthood function as banker, landlord, and
employer well before secular financial institutions mature — a real
economic-power stat riding entirely on accumulated `religious authority`
rather than on any independent secular institutional capacity of its own.
