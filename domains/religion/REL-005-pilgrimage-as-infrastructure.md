A religious obligation or strong devotional pull to travel to a specific
place generates real, lasting physical infrastructure — roads, hospices,
wells, and markets — as an economic and engineering side effect of purely
religious motivation.

## Timing

The Hajj becomes an institutionalized pillar of Islamic practice from 632;
the Camino de Santiago pilgrimage network develops from the 800s; South
Asian pilgrimage circuits and periodic mass gatherings sustain comparable
infrastructure investment across the same broad era, continuing through
1500 and well beyond.

## Mechanism

Predictable, recurring mass pilgrim traffic creates commercial demand
(lodging, food, guides, souvenirs) dense and reliable enough to justify
permanent infrastructure investment along the route and at the
destination itself — the same route-knowledge and road-engineering
investment (ENG-003) ordinary trade would take much longer to justify on
its own gets built faster and denser because religious obligation
guarantees the traffic volume independent of ordinary commercial risk.

## Social consequence

Pilgrimage infrastructure creates a distinctive, temporary but recurring
cosmopolitan mixing zone along the route and at the destination —
pilgrims from wildly different regions, languages, and social classes
travel and lodge together under a shared religious purpose that overrides
ordinary social separation, making pilgrimage routes unusually effective
channels for the spread of goods, news, and ideas alongside the religious
purpose that generates the traffic in the first place.

## Regional specifics

The Hajj's status as a universal individual obligation for every capable
Muslim, on a fixed annual calendar, generates uniquely concentrated and
predictable mass traffic that sustains extensive, centrally-organized
supporting infrastructure across the pilgrimage routes converging on
Mecca. South Asian pilgrimage circuits achieve comparable infrastructure
investment through a more geographically distributed network of sacred
sites and periodic astrologically-timed mass gatherings rather than one
single mandated destination and calendar. Buddhist pilgrimage's continued
status as a valued but non-obligatory devotional practice, rather than a
mandated pillar, shows a religion doesn't need to make pilgrimage
compulsory to generate real supporting infrastructure — it generates less
of it, but the underlying mechanism (traffic density justifying
investment) still operates.

## Hook

Guaranteed, obligation-driven pilgrim traffic justifies infrastructure
investment (extending ENG-003's road-engineering territory) faster and
denser than ordinary commercial traffic would on its own — and the
resulting route becomes an unusually effective channel for spreading
`route knowledge`, goods, and ideas as a side effect of the religious
purpose that actually generates the underlying traffic volume.
