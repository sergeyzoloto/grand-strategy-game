The reverse of REL-220's confessional-state logic — a state deliberately
permits multiple religious communities to coexist under its rule rather
than promoting or enforcing a single confession, whether from principled
conviction, practical governance calculation, or some mix of both.

## Timing

The Cyrus Cylinder's policy of restoring displaced peoples and their
temples (from 539 BCE) is an early documented case; Ashoka's edicts on
respecting all sects date to around -260; the Ottoman millet system
develops progressively across the empire's history; the European
Westphalian settlement (1648) formalizes a comparable toleration principle
at the end of confessional warfare.

## Mechanism

Toleration substitutes managed coexistence for the confessional state's
consolidation-through-uniformity strategy (REL-220), trading some of the
legibility and loyalty-sorting clarity a single enforced confession
provides for the reduced friction, administrative cost, and stability of
not actively suppressing minority religious communities — this trade
pays off best where the tolerated communities are economically or
administratively valuable enough (as with Ottoman millet communities'
distinct commercial and craft roles) that suppression would cost the
state more than toleration does.

## Social consequence

Tolerated religious minorities under a system like the millet often gain
real communal self-governance — their own courts, schools, and internal
legal authority over community members — in exchange for accepting
formal political subordination and often specific additional taxation, a
managed-autonomy arrangement distinct from either full citizenship
equality or active persecution.

## Regional specifics

Ashoka's edicts frame toleration explicitly as a positive governance
virtue predating comparable European statements of the principle by over
a millennium, complicating any account crediting toleration's articulation
primarily to later Western Enlightenment thought. The Ottoman millet
system's communal-autonomy model manages religious difference through
delegated self-governance rather than either the territorial cuius-regio
logic Westphalia formalizes or a full individual-equality model, showing
toleration itself takes genuinely different institutional forms rather
than one universal template gradually adopted everywhere.

## Contested

Whether historical toleration reflects genuine principled pluralism or
primarily pragmatic governance calculation is disputed case by case, with
real pragmatic motivation identifiable even in cases sometimes cited as
principled exemplars. Model toleration's durability as conditional on
whether the practical governance interest it originally served remains
intact, rather than assuming a tolerant policy, once adopted, persists
independent of the conditions that motivated it.

## Hook

Toleration trades the confessional state's (REL-220) loyalty-sorting
legibility for reduced friction and administrative cost, often through a
managed-communal-autonomy arrangement rather than full equality — and its
durability tracks whether the original practical governance interest
behind adopting it remains intact, not any independent commitment to
`religious authority` pluralism as a fixed value.
