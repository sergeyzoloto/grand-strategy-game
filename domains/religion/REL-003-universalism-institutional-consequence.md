The ethical argument for universal moral community is PHI's fuller
territory; this entry owns what happens to a religious institution once it
makes that claim — membership converts from something inherited by birth
into a defined ethnic or kin group into something available to anyone
through conversion or acceptance of the teaching.

## Timing

Buddhism's universalist framing dates from its founding (traditionally
around -500) and receives major institutional reinforcement under Ashokan
patronage (REL-002, REL-014); Islam's ummah concept is embedded from 610;
Christianity's own universalist expansion runs on a parallel and
overlapping timeline through late antiquity into the 700s.

## Mechanism

A particularist religion (membership defined by birth into a specific
ethnic or kin group) has a natural, fixed ceiling on how large its
community can grow — a universalist religion removes that ceiling by
defining membership through belief, practice, or conversion rather than
ancestry, which converts the religion's growth potential from bounded by
population of one lineage or people into bounded only by how far
missionary contact (REL-014) and conversion can actually reach.

## Social consequence

Universalist religious institutions face a genuinely new organizational
problem particularist ones never had to solve: maintaining doctrinal and
practical coherence across converts from wildly different existing
cultures, languages, and social structures, which is a large part of why
canon-fixation (REL-002), standardized law and courts (REL-012), and
missionary institutional structure (REL-014) all become especially
elaborated in universalist traditions specifically.

## Regional specifics

Islam's ummah concept is institutionally load-bearing from the religion's
founding, with obligations (zakat, pilgrimage) applying uniformly
regardless of a believer's ethnic or tribal origin by explicit design.
Confucianism's sustained ethical seriousness without an equivalent
explicit universalist claim to transcend civilizational boundary shows
ethical sophistication and the specific institution-restructuring
universalist move are separable achievements — a tradition can be
comparably rigorous ethically while keeping membership implicitly bounded
to a defined civilizational sphere rather than making growth-unbounded
conversion its organizing principle.

## Hook

A universalist membership claim removes a religious institution's natural
population ceiling by substituting conversion for birth as the entry
criterion, at the cost of a genuinely new coherence-maintenance problem
across culturally disparate converts — directly driving canon-fixation
(REL-002) and religious court (REL-012) elaboration specifically in
traditions that make this move, rather than in ethically comparable ones
that don't.
