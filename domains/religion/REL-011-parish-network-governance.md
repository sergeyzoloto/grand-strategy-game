The parish's core religious function (a local congregation with its own
priest) makes it, almost incidentally, the most complete and granular
population-registration network available to most premodern states — a
governance capability riding on religious infrastructure built for an
entirely different purpose.

## Timing

Mandatory parish registration of baptisms, marriages, and burials becomes
increasingly standardized following the Fourth Lateran Council (1215),
maturing into comprehensive vital-record systems across Catholic and
later Protestant Europe through the 1600s; Japan's structurally similar
danka temple-registration system dates from 1638.

## Mechanism

A parish priest already has to record baptisms, marriages, and burials for
purely religious and sacramental reasons — extending that existing
record-keeping practice to serve state administrative purposes (census,
tax assessment, legal proof of age or marital status, movement tracking)
costs the state almost nothing extra, since the underlying data collection
was already happening for religious reasons and simply needs to be shared
with or reported to secular authority.

## Social consequence

Once parish records become the de facto legal proof of birth, marriage,
and death, the local priest or temple functionary becomes an
unavoidable node in an individual's legal existence — a person's civil
status can become dependent on their standing with, or registration
through, a religious institution regardless of their own actual religious
conviction, a quiet but pervasive form of religious institutional leverage
over purely civil matters.

## Regional specifics

Japan's danka system achieves a functionally near-identical outcome
(comprehensive, temple-anchored population registration doing double duty
for state administrative purposes) through an explicitly different
original motivation — identifying and excluding a suppressed religious
minority rather than routine sacramental record-keeping — showing the
same governance-through-religious-infrastructure mechanism can emerge from
very different starting motivations. Coverage and record quality within
Europe itself varied substantially by state wealth and administrative
centralization, showing "the parish network" wasn't one uniform, complete
system so much as a shared model implemented with very different rigor
across different territories.

## Hook

Parish or temple registration extends a state's `legibility` over its own
population almost for free, since the underlying record-keeping was
already happening for religious reasons — a governance capability that
can emerge from routine sacramental practice (Europe) or from deliberate
religious-minority suppression (Japan's danka system) and still converge
on the same comprehensive population-registry function either way.
