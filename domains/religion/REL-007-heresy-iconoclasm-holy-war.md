Once canon (REL-002) fixes what counts as correct doctrine and hierarchy
(REL-006) exists to enforce it, deviation becomes something the
institution has both the authority and the incentive to actively suppress
— a mechanism only possible once those two prior conditions already exist.

## Timing

Byzantine iconoclasm runs roughly 726-843; the Sunni-Shia division dates
to the succession dispute immediately following 632; the medieval Christian
crusading movement and inquisitorial heresy prosecution intensify through
the 1200s-1300s.

## Mechanism

A hierarchical institution with fixed canon has both the standing (its own
declared authority to define orthodoxy) and the incentive (deviation
threatens its own doctrinal monopoly and often its property and political
position) to actively suppress deviation rather than tolerate it as
harmless variation — the suppression mechanism can range from doctrinal
argument and excommunication through outright military campaign,
depending on how much material and political stake is bound up with the
specific doctrinal question at issue.

## Social consequence

Heresy prosecution and holy war both convert internal doctrinal
disagreement into an existential, often violent conflict rather than a
manageable difference of opinion, which is part of why these episodes
recur with such intensity specifically in traditions with strong
canon-fixation and hierarchy (REL-002, REL-006) — a tradition without
either has much less institutional machinery available to escalate
doctrinal disagreement into organized suppression in the first place.

## Regional specifics

The Sunni-Shia division shows the underlying mechanism can be triggered by
a succession and authority dispute rather than a strictly theological
disagreement about images or divine nature, and still generate comparably
durable, institutionally-organized sectarian conflict. Tang China's
Buddhist suppressions, driven substantially by state interest in
reclaiming tax-exempt monastic property rather than doctrinal orthodoxy
enforcement, show the same suppression mechanism (a state or institution
moving forcefully against a religious body) can be motivated primarily by
material state interest with religious framing doing comparatively little
independent work.

## Contested

Whether heresy campaigns, iconoclasm, and holy war are driven primarily by
genuine theological conviction or substantially by material and political
interest using religious framing as justification is disputed case by
case, with real evidence for both motivations often present
simultaneously in the same episode. Model each case as combining a
genuine-conviction component and a material-interest component in a
case-specific ratio rather than assuming either pure motivation applies
uniformly across all instances.

## Hook

Canon-fixation (REL-002) and hierarchy (REL-006) together supply both the
authority and the institutional machinery needed to escalate doctrinal
deviation into organized suppression — a mechanism whose actual trigger
ranges from genuine theological conviction to bare material interest in
land and revenue, with real cases combining both in a ratio worth modeling
explicitly rather than assuming.
