The mechanical printing technique is ENG-150's territory and the cognitive
reading-habit shift is HLT-190's; this entry owns what mass vernacular
scripture access does specifically to institutional religious authority —
breaking a hierarchy's practical monopoly on scriptural interpretation.

## Timing

Luther's German New Testament (1522) is the paradigm case of vernacular
scripture reaching a mass lay audience directly; the resulting
institutional fragmentation and the wars and settlements it drives
continue through the Peace of Westphalia (1648) and beyond.

## Mechanism

As long as scripture access requires either literacy in a restricted
liturgical language or dependence on clergy to read and interpret it for
you, institutional hierarchy retains substantial practical control over
what the text is understood to mean regardless of its official doctrinal
position — cheap vernacular print removes that practical dependency,
letting individual readers form their own scriptural interpretation
directly, which is precisely what makes sola scriptura ("scripture alone,"
without required clerical mediation) a practically viable religious
position for the first time at real popular scale.

## Social consequence

Once large numbers of ordinary believers can and do read scripture
directly, institutional hierarchy's authority to declare one interpretation
canonical (REL-070) and suppress rivals as heresy (REL-150) faces a
genuinely new practical challenge — dissenting interpretations can now
spread and sustain a lay following independent of clerical approval or
suppression, which is a structurally different threat than an earlier
clerical heretic working within an institution that still fully
controlled lay scripture access.

## Regional specifics

Korean and Chinese printed Buddhist and Confucian text circulation,
predating Luther's vernacular Bible by well over a century, shows mass
printed religious text access doesn't automatically produce comparable
schismatic institutional fragmentation — the outcome depends heavily on
how contested institutional authority already was going into the moment of
wider text access, not on print access alone. Reformation Europe's
fragmentation reflects print interacting with an already fragile and
contested single-hierarchy model (REL-120, REL-170's investiture tensions
among the accumulated pressures), a combination East Asian Buddhist and
Confucian institutional contexts didn't share in the same form.

## Contested

Whether the Reformation's institutional fragmentation should be
attributed primarily to vernacular print access itself or to pre-existing
political and theological pressures print merely accelerated is disputed.
Model print access as a necessary enabling condition for the specific form
and speed of the resulting fragmentation, without treating it as sufficient
on its own — the East Asian comparison case, where comparable print access
didn't produce comparable fragmentation, supports treating pre-existing
institutional contestation as the other necessary ingredient.

## Hook

Mass vernacular print removes lay dependency on clerical mediation for
scripture access, directly threatening a hierarchy's practical (not just
doctrinal) control over interpretation — but per the East Asian
comparison, this only produces schismatic fragmentation where existing
`religious authority` was already contested going in, not as an automatic
consequence of print access alone.
