Distinct from REL-005's traffic-volume logic, a relic functions as a scarce, non-fungible prestige good institutions compete to acquire, display, and defend the authenticity of — driving inter-institutional rivalry and outright theft or forgery in a way ordinary pilgrim traffic alone doesn't generate.

## Timing

Christian relic veneration scales from the 4th century onward, following the end of persecution and the growth of organized pilgrimage (REL-005); the practice reaches its most institutionally competitive and commercially significant form across medieval Europe through the 1500s, before Reformation-era (REL-016) critique sharply curtails it in Protestant territories specifically.

## Mechanism

A relic's value rests entirely on a claim of authenticity that's usually unverifiable by any means available at the time, which converts relic acquisition into a genuinely different economic logic than ordinary trade goods: a shrine's prestige and the pilgrim traffic (and REL-005's resulting infrastructure investment) it can draw depend directly on possessing a relic other institutions want and can't easily replicate, creating real incentive for outright theft (the medieval practice of furta sacra, "holy theft," where stealing a rival institution's relic was sometimes framed as the saint's own choice to relocate) and for producing convenient forgeries when genuine relics simply run short of demand.

## Social consequence

Because a relic's prestige value is entirely separable from any independently verifiable property, institutions holding relics of dubious or competing authenticity claims coexist without any reliable external mechanism to settle the dispute, converting relic authenticity into a durable, unresolvable source of inter-institutional rivalry — two shrines can each hold "the" head of the same saint, both drawing real pilgrim traffic and revenue, with no institutional process capable of definitively resolving which, if either, is genuine.

## Regional specifics

Medieval European Christendom's specific combination of institutional fragmentation (many competing dioceses, monasteries, and shrines) and organized pilgrimage infrastructure (REL-005) produces an unusually well-documented pattern of relic theft and forgery relative to the Islamic and Buddhist cases, where relic veneration develops real prestige-object dynamics but generally within less institutionally fragmented and competitive structures.

## Hook

A modifier: a scarce sacred-object economy distinct from REL-005's traffic-volume logic, driving inter-institutional rivalry, theft, and forgery precisely because a relic's prestige value rests on an authenticity claim no available institution can definitively verify or refute.
