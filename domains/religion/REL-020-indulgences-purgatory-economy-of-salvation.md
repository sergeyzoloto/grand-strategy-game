A developed doctrine of purgatory converts spiritual merit into a good the church can sell directly — the specific commercialized practice that provokes Luther's 1517 protest, which REL-016 then tracks the print-driven spread of without ever covering what actually lit the fuse.

## Timing

Purgatory doctrine develops and systematizes through the 1200s, formalized at the Second Council of Lyon (1274); indulgence sales scale sharply in the early 1500s, most consequentially the 1517 campaign funding St. Peter's Basilica's reconstruction, which directly provokes Luther's Ninety-Five Theses; the Council of Trent's 1567 reforms curtail the most commercially aggressive indulgence practices without abolishing the underlying doctrine.

## Mechanism

Purgatory doctrine holds that most souls undergo a temporary, purifying post-death punishment before reaching heaven, and that specific actions by the living or the dead person themselves — prayer, charitable donation, purchase of an indulgence — can shorten that duration; converting an abstract spiritual concern (the fate of one's own or a loved one's soul) into something with a concrete, purchasable remedy gives the church a revenue mechanism resting directly on lay anxiety about the afterlife, distinct from and more directly commercial than REL-015's standardized tithe.

## Social consequence

Once salvation itself becomes something with a price attached, the church's institutional authority becomes entangled with its own revenue interest in a way that's newly and visibly vulnerable to a charge of corruption — a critique that a fixed, land-based tithe (REL-015) doesn't provoke in the same way, since indulgence sales are an active, escalating commercial practice rather than a customary fixed obligation, making them a uniquely exposed target once reform sentiment (REL-016) has a print technology capable of spreading the critique widely and fast.

## Regional specifics

The 1517 St. Peter's Basilica indulgence campaign, run substantially through the Dominican preacher Johann Tetzel's aggressive sales practice in German territories, is the specific proximate trigger for Luther's protest — not an abstract complaint about church wealth in general, but a reaction to a concrete, escalating, and highly visible sales practice. Islamic theology's absence of a comparable purgeable intermediate afterlife state shows this specific commercialization mechanism depends on a particular doctrinal structure that not every tradition's afterlife theology provides the ingredients for.

## Hook

A gate/modifier: converts institutional authority directly into a saleable good, at the cost of a durable vulnerability to exactly the reform backlash REL-016 covers once that commercialization is perceived as corrupt — the specific practice that turns an abstract afterlife doctrine into the proximate trigger for the largest institutional fragmentation in this domain's own record.
