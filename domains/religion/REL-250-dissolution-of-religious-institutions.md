Every other entry in this domain spends centuries building religious institutional wealth and authority; this entry covers what happens when a state with sufficient administrative and military capacity seizes and redistributes it in a single deliberate act.

## Timing

Henry VIII's Dissolution of the Monasteries runs from 1536 to 1541, transferring roughly a quarter of England's landed wealth out of monastic hands within five years; the French Revolutionary government's confiscation of church property and subordination of the clergy to the state runs from 1789 through 1794.

## Mechanism

A monastery or religious institution's accumulated land, reserves, and buildings (REL-020, REL-060) represent centuries of continuous investment that no single generation could reproduce quickly — a state that successfully asserts legal and military authority to dissolve the institution and seize its assets converts that accumulated wealth into secular state or private hands essentially overnight, a conversion rate wildly favorable to the seizing state precisely because the wealth took so long to build and can be redistributed so much faster than it accumulated.

## Social consequence

Dissolution doesn't just transfer property, it eliminates the institution's ongoing social functions — charitable relief, hospitality, local employment, and the specific charitable-medical role REL-060 already notes monasteries carry — with no guarantee any replacement institution fills the resulting gap, meaning a dissolution's social cost frequently extends well beyond the displaced monks and nuns themselves into the surrounding community that depended on the institution's ongoing functions, not just its existence.

## Regional specifics

Henry VIII's dissolution is driven by a specific combination of doctrinal break with Rome, marital politics, and acute royal fiscal need, executed with enough administrative efficiency to complete in five years. The French Revolutionary case pursues comparable wholesale seizure through a different ideological engine — anti-clerical revolutionary conviction and desperate republican fiscal need rather than a monarch's personal doctrinal break — showing the underlying mechanism (a sufficiently powerful state seizing accumulated religious wealth) recurs across genuinely different political motivations. Ottoman waqf endowments' specific legal protection from comparable confiscation, by contrast, shows this mechanism isn't universally available even to powerful states — a legal structure can be deliberately designed to resist exactly this kind of seizure.

## Hook

An event: a depleting-pool mechanic for religious authority and accumulated institutional wealth (REL-020, REL-060), drawn down catastrophically by a single state action rather than eroding gradually, eliminating the institution's ongoing social functions along with its property and with no guarantee either is ever rebuilt.
