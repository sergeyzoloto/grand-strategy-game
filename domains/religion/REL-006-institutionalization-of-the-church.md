A single, hierarchically-organized "church" — bishops answering to
archbishops, a defined clergy, fixed diocesan territory — is one specific
institutional outcome among several, not the default form religious
authority takes once a tradition grows large.

## Timing

Christian church hierarchy formalizes substantially from the Council of
Nicaea (325) through the medieval period, reaching mature diocesan
structure by around 1500; the contrasting decentralized cases (Hindu
tradition, African traditional religions) have no comparable centralizing
moment because none occurs across their own much longer histories.

## Mechanism

Where a single doctrinal authority successfully claims jurisdiction over a
defined territory and clergy answerable to it, a church hierarchy forms
and can enforce canon-fixed doctrine (REL-002) consistently across its
whole territory; where no single authority makes or sustains that claim
successfully, religious practice instead organizes around a much larger
number of smaller, mutually independent centers — individual temples,
shrines, or guru lineages — each with real local authority and none
subordinate to any single apex, which trades doctrinal consistency for
resilience, since no single institutional failure can disrupt the whole
tradition at once.

## Social consequence

A hierarchical church concentrates religious authority (and its
accompanying temporal power, wealth, and political leverage) in a
relatively small number of office-holders whose authority comes from
institutional position; a decentralized tradition instead distributes
comparable authority across many more individually-legitimated priests,
gurus, or diviners, whose standing rests on personal reputation, lineage,
or community recognition rather than a hierarchical office — a
fundamentally different relationship between practitioner and religious
authority in each case.

## Regional specifics

Hindu tradition's persistence as a family of independently-authoritative
temple, sect, and lineage traditions, with no apex institution ever
forming despite an extremely long and continuous history, shows
decentralization isn't a stage a large tradition inevitably grows out of —
it can be the stable, durable long-term outcome. African traditional
religions organizing around shrine- and lineage-specific authority
similarly show decentralization as a durable normal state existing
alongside, not merely preceding, the centralized Christian and Islamic
institutions later introduced from outside — the two organizational
models coexist rather than one representing an earlier stage of the other.

## Hook

Whether religious authority consolidates into a single hierarchy or stays
distributed across many independently-legitimated local centers is a real
structural fork with lasting consequences — hierarchy trades resilience
for doctrinal consistency and concentrates `religious authority` in a few
office-holders, while decentralization trades consistency for resilience
and distributes it across many more locally-legitimated ones, with neither
outcome representing an immature version of the other.
