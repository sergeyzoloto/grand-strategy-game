Religious decline happened, but not everywhere and not because modernity
produces it. Western Europe declined sharply. The United States did not, and
much of the world intensified across the same two centuries. The question this
entry answers is why religion declined there, not why religion declines.

## Timing

Measurable drift begins in parts of France around 1750 and accelerates in
Protestant and Catholic north-west Europe through the nineteenth century. It
collapses suddenly across much of it in the 1960s. Coerced secularisation runs
from 1917 in the Soviet Union, 1924 in Turkey, 1926 in Mexico and 1949 in China.
The revivals that follow coercion, and the concurrent intensifications in Latin
America, Africa, South Asia and the Islamic world, run through 2000 and beyond.

## Mechanism

**Differentiation is not decline.** Casanova's decomposition is the structure
this entry is built on. "Secularisation" bundles three claims. First,
differentiation: religious institutions lose control of other spheres. Second,
decline: belief and practice fall. Third, privatisation: religion withdraws
from public life. Differentiation holds almost everywhere a secular settlement
(REL-252) took hold. Decline holds only in some places. Privatisation was
falsified by the public religious movements of the late twentieth century.
PHI-280 carries the thesis as a philosophical claim about disenchantment and
the autonomy of secular reason. This entry owns the institutional process and
its uneven distribution.

**Participation, authority and belief are separate quantities.** A population
can keep belonging while ceasing to attend, as in Scandinavia, where state-church
membership stays near universal with very low attendance. It can keep believing
while ceasing to belong, which is Davie's British pattern. An institution can
lose its public `religious authority` while participation holds. This is design
principle 3 applied to religion, and it is why `religious participation` is
tracked apart from `religious authority`.

Four explanations compete. They are presented as rivals because the record does
not settle among them.

- **Supply side.** A church funded by the state has no need to recruit, so it
  provides worship poorly and participation falls. Competing churches that
  must recruit or starve raise it. This explains the American-European
  contrast in one move, since REL-252's disestablishment created a market and
  establishment in Europe preserved monopolies. It fits Latin America's
  Pentecostal surge after the Catholic monopoly weakened. Its statistical
  support is weaker than its fit to cases.
- **Functional differentiation.** The state absorbs, one by one, the things
  the church did, and religious authority loses the practical ground it
  stood on. This explanation has the strongest mechanical content, because
  each transfer is a specific, dateable institutional change rather than a
  shift in belief:
  - Registration and marriage moved from the parish register (REL-210) to
    civil registries: France in 1792, England in 1836-37, Mexico in 1859,
    and obligatory civil marriage in Germany in 1875.
  - Schooling moved to the state (LAW-165): England's board schools from
    1870, the Ferry laws of 1881-82, Quebec's education ministry in 1964.
  - Poor relief moved from the parish to the poor-law union in England in
    1834, and then to social insurance (LAW-210) from 1883.
  - Hospitals, historically religious foundations (HLT-160), passed to
    state systems such as the NHS in 1948.
  - Jurisdiction over probate and divorce left church courts in England
    in 1857. LAW-040 carries the marriage-law side.

  Quebec is the demonstration. The transfers came first and compressed into
  a few years, and the collapse in participation followed them.
- **Existential security.** People turn to religion to face risks: early
  death, illness, destitution, and an old age with no support. Where the
  state and the economy remove those risks, demand falls (Norris and
  Inglehart). Welfare and `medical access` are the operative inputs. The
  explanation fits Europe against Africa. It struggles with the wealthy Gulf,
  and it can explain the United States only through that country's higher
  inequality.
- **Coerced secularisation** is a separate case and must not be merged into
  the others. Soviet and Chinese state atheism, Kemalism and Mexican
  anticlericalism were policies. They suppress the institution and its public
  practice while the state applies force. They produce a different result
  from drift, and a less durable one. Identification rebounds when the force
  lifts, as in Russia after 1991 and China after 1982. Practice lags
  identification. A population whose religion was taken from it treats the
  religion as part of a national identity it is recovering. A population that
  drifted away has nothing to recover.

The explanations are not exclusive. The regional pattern is best read as
conditional on three inputs that can each be observed before the outcome:

- **Establishment status.** Monopoly churches lose participation and
  competitive markets hold it.
- **State capacity to absorb church functions.** Where the state takes over
  schooling, welfare and health, religious authority loses its base. Where it
  cannot, churches and mosques keep running them.
- **Drift or imposition.** Drifted decline is durable, and imposed decline
  rebounds.

North-west Europe had all three conditions: established churches, high-capacity
states that absorbed church functions, and drift. The United States lacked the
first. Sub-Saharan Africa lacked the second. The Soviet Union and China supplied
the third by force.

## Social consequence

At household scale decline is uneven by life event. Weekly attendance goes first.
Church weddings go later. Baptism and religious funerals last longest, because
rites of passage mark belonging to a family and a place, not belief. A family can
stop going to church for two generations and still christen its children. The
institution meanwhile loses its role as the default provider of schooling,
charity and comfort in illness, which is why the functional transfers change
daily life more than any change in doctrine does.

Brown's gendered account deserves weight at this scale. In Britain religious
practice had been carried substantially by women and passed on by mothers. When
women's work, education and self-understanding changed in the 1960s, the
transmission line broke within a generation. That explains a sudden collapse
where the industrialisation account predicts a gradual one.

Where decline was imposed, the social consequence is different: a population
that kept rites in private, passed religion on through grandparents, and, when
coercion lifted, returned to religion as identity rather than as practice. Where
religion intensified, the same decades brought churches and mosques into the
functions states could not supply. Pentecostal congregations run mutual aid in
Brazilian urban peripheries. Mission and Islamic schools educate much of a
generation in West Africa.

## Regional specifics

Europe is the exceptional case, not the model (Davie, _Europe: The Exceptional
Case_, 2002), and the entry should be read that way. Its drifted decline starts
early in France, runs gradually through the nineteenth century, and collapses in
the 1960s. Quebec and Uruguay show the same outcome reproduced in the Americas
wherever the same conditions held: a state that absorbed church functions, and a
church that had been the monopoly provider.

The United States is the principal counter-case. It industrialised and urbanised
without comparable decline, under disestablishment and competitive
denominations. Its late rise in religious "nones" follows the politicisation of
religion rather than the drift the thesis predicts.

The intensification cases carry most of the world's population. Latin America's
religious market reorganised toward Pentecostalism rather than shrinking.
Christianity and Islam both grew across sub-Saharan Africa, where states lacked
the capacity to take over church functions. The Islamic world revived under
urbanisation and in the Gulf under wealth. India's religiosity held through
development.

The coerced cases are the cleanest natural experiment available. Soviet, Chinese,
Turkish and Mexican policy each suppressed religious institutions by state force,
and in each case religion outlasted the policy. Where the institution recovered,
it recovered as a marker of national or communal identity faster than as regular
practice.

## Contested

The contest is the entry. The strong thesis is falsified. Berger, once its
leading proponent, recanted, and Casanova's decomposition keeps only
differentiation. The live dispute is between the explanations of where decline
occurred. Supply-side theory explains the US-Europe contrast well, but its
central correlation between pluralism and participation turned out partly to be a
statistical artefact. Functional differentiation has the best mechanical content
and the clearest dated cases, and it struggles to explain why the United States
absorbed schooling without losing participation. Existential security fits the
broad global pattern and fails in the Gulf. Coerced secularisation is separable
because it is policy rather than drift, and its rebounds are the best-documented
outcome of the four.

Per design principle 6, do not choose among them. Make decline conditional on
establishment status, state capacity to absorb church functions, and drift versus
imposition. Draw the relative weights of the supply, differentiation and security
explanations per scenario, so that the same institutional history can produce a
European or an American outcome depending on which mechanism a given world
weights most heavily.

## Hook

`religious participation` and `religious authority` move on separate tracks, and
neither is a function of development. Participation should be driven by three
inputs, weighted per scenario:

- **Establishment:** a monopoly church loses participation and a competitive
  market holds it.
- **Existential security:** welfare coverage and `medical access` lower
  demand.
- **Functional transfer events:** civil registration, civil marriage, state
  schooling, poor relief, hospitals and court jurisdiction. Each strips a
  base from institutional `religious authority`, but only in a state with the
  capacity to take the function over. Where the state cannot, the religious
  institution keeps the function and gains authority from providing it.

Coerced secularisation is a separate state policy with its own dynamics:

- **While applied:** it suppresses public practice and institutional
  authority, at a standing enforcement cost.
- **When lifted:** identification rebounds fast and practice rebounds slowly,
  so the recovered religion functions as national or communal identity rather
  than regular devotion.

Drifted decline does not rebound. Because the inputs are institutional, the same
modernising state can end up European, American or African in outcome depending
on what it had built before modernising.
