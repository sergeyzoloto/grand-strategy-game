Translating the canon out of its frozen language is the only thing that
dissolves MED-040's gate. It does two things at once: it destroys the
intermediary's monopoly, and it creates the vernacular as a language
capable of carrying serious content, which it was not before.

## Timing

From the Chinese Buddhist translation bureaus of the third century onward,
through the Geez Bible, the bhakti vernacular movements from around 600,
the Wycliffite Bible of 1382 and the Lutheran and Tyndale translations of
the 1520s and 1530s, to roughly 1650.

## Mechanism

**The gate MED-040 builds is made of language, so translation is the one
tool that removes it.** An institutional barrier can be defended,
reformed, or captured; a linguistic one simply stops existing the moment
the text is available in a language the reader already has. That is why
translation is experienced as an attack by the class whose position rests
on the gap, and why it is so frequently prohibited rather than argued with.

**The second effect is less obvious and more durable: the vernacular is
created by the act.** A language that has carried folk song and daily
business but never law, theology or philosophy is not ready to carry them —
it lacks the vocabulary, the registers, and the settled conventions. A
translator must invent all three, and the language afterwards can do things
it could not do before. Luther's German and the King James English both
standardise their languages by supplying the version everyone owns; the
bhakti poets make Tamil and Marathi into vehicles for serious religious
argument by using them for it. **The translation is the last text written
in the old arrangement and the first written in the new one.**

**Fragmentation is conditional, and the corpus contains the
counterexample.** The Reformation reading treats vernacular scripture as
inherently schismatic. The Chinese Buddhist translation project is a
comparable effort in scale and duration that **strengthened** its
institution, because the sangha sponsored it, staffed it, and controlled
the terminology. Two variables decide which way it runs: **whether the
translating party sits inside or outside the institution holding the
canon**, and **whether a doctrinal quarrel with institutional backing
already exists**. Inside plus no quarrel extends reach. Outside plus an
existing quarrel produces a rupture.

**And print alone is not the trigger.** China had movable type for seven
centuries without a comparable rupture, a fact REL-230 already records.
The actual condition is translation *and* print *and* a doctrinal schism
with institutional backing — three variables, and any two of them produce
nothing at all. A model that fires on print alone is wrong on the largest
available case.

**The gate can also simply move.** Geez was a translation target and became
a frozen canon language itself within a few centuries, so the arrangement
regenerated one layer down. Any model should allow the vernacular of one
era to become the canon language of the next.

## Social consequence

The intermediary's position is what changes, and it changes asymmetrically.
A cleric's authority rested on two things — access to the text and training
in its interpretation — and translation removes the first while leaving the
second. The result is not the disappearance of the interpreter but a
sharp fall in how much can be claimed on the basis of access alone, and a
corresponding shift of the argument onto ground where laypeople can
participate, badly and in enormous numbers.

For the vernacular's speakers the effect runs deeper than religion. A
language that has been used for scripture acquires standing, and the
dialect chosen for the translation becomes the standard against which
others are measured as provincial — so translation elevates one vernacular
while demoting its neighbours, which is rarely the translator's intention
and reliably the outcome.

And where translation is refused, the refusal itself becomes a doctrine to
be defended. The Islamic position is not an absence of translation but a
developed theological account of why the Quran's language is inseparable
from its revelation, and the position is intellectually serious rather than
merely restrictive.

## Regional specifics

The Chinese Buddhist project carries the entry, and it should be given the
weight its scale deserves rather than appearing as a foil to Luther. It
ran for centuries with organised teams, revision passes and standardised
terminology under state patronage, rendering a foreign canon into Chinese
and strengthening the institution that sponsored it. The bhakti movements
supply the from-below variant, making vernaculars serious by using them.
The Islamic case supplies the branch: *ijaz* meant a translation was never
scripture, so the same technology produced no vernacularisation and the
gate held.

Europe is where all three conditions coincide, which is why the European
case looks like the mechanism's natural outcome and is in fact its special
case. The Americas and steppe absences make the complementary point from
opposite directions — in one there was no gate to attack, and in the other
the translations were commissioned by the gatekeepers.

## Contested

Whether translation fragments institutions is genuinely disputed, and the
model should carry the dispute rather than the Reformation's answer to it.
Translation removes the language barrier; fragmentation depends on who
translated and whether a backed quarrel already existed. Institution-
sponsored translation extends reach. Externally produced translation
entering a live doctrinal dispute ruptures the institution.

The print question is separate and equally important: print alone produces
nothing, on the evidence of seven centuries of Chinese printing. The
condition is the conjunction of three variables, and a model that fires on
any one or two of them is falsified by a case the corpus already contains.

## Hook

The only instrument that dissolves MED-040's gate, since the barrier is
made of language rather than membership — so `intermediary dependence`
falls sharply and `literacy training cost` returns to whatever MED-020 left
it at. It does a second thing simultaneously: the vernacular is *created*
as a serious medium by being used for serious content, so the translation
elevates one dialect into a standard and demotes its neighbours as a
side effect nobody intended. Fragmentation must be conditional on two
variables and never automatic — whether the translator is inside or outside
the institution holding the canon, and whether a backed doctrinal quarrel
already exists — with institution-sponsored translation extending reach
instead. Print is a third separate requirement, not a substitute for
either. And the arrangement can regenerate: a translation target can become
a frozen canon language a few centuries later, putting the gate back one
layer down.
