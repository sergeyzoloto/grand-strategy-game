The physical form of the administrative record. A bound volume fixes order at the
moment of binding. A card index breaks that fixity, and in doing so lets record-keeping
scale past what one clerk with one book can do. This is an entry about how
administrations hold records, not about how individuals take notes.

## Timing

From Gessner's slips of 1548 and the Revolutionary playing-card catalogue of 1791,
through van Swieten's standing slip catalogue in Vienna from 1780, Dewey and the Library
Bureau from 1876, and vertical filing from the 1890s, to police identification by
Bertillon from 1883 and fingerprint classification in Calcutta and La Plata in the
1890s, and the population registries of the twentieth century. The entry closes around
1970, as computer databases began to take over administrative records, although card
catalogues and filing cabinets remained in use for decades after.

## Mechanism

**Why this entry is MED and not MTH.** Boundary rules give MED the notation's surface,
channel and access gate, and MTH what a notation makes derivable and the procedures
written in it. MTH-075, the punched-card unit record, is placed in MTH because the
tabulator holds the sorting procedure outside any person's head. A card index holds no
procedure. Every sort, every lookup and every cross-reference is still performed by a
clerk. What changes is the physical form of the record, and the properties below follow
from that form, not from a procedure run on it. The concurrency and queryability claims
come close to MTH-075's territory. They stay here because in a card index both are
achieved by people working on a divisible substrate. The moment a machine reads the card
and performs the sort, the same record crosses into MTH, which is exactly what
Hollerith's card did.

**The precondition is cheap card stock.** A collection of hundreds of thousands of
slips, each discarded and replaced whenever it wears or errs, is affordable only once
paper is cheap enough to spend on single entries (MED-050). The medium is paper
treated as disposable and divisible rather than as a scarce surface to be filled.

**A bound volume fixes three things at once.** Every entry in a ledger or register is
permanent: it cannot be moved without being recopied. Every entry is sequential: it
follows whatever was written before it. And every entry is positioned relative to its
neighbours: its place is decided at the moment of writing. The card index breaks all
three.

- **Mobility.** A card can be moved without being recopied, so the same records can be
  reordered alphabetically today, by date tomorrow and by category after that, with no
  transcription cost. A bound register answers the one question its order was chosen
  for; a card file can be re-sorted to answer another.
- **Extensibility.** A new card is inserted in its place rather than appended at the
  end, so the collection has no fixed size and does not degrade as it grows. A ledger
  fills up and must be continued in a new volume; a card file simply gets another
  drawer.
- **Concurrency, the property usually missed.** A ledger is a single physical object
  and admits one writer at a time. A card index admits many at once, because each clerk
  is working on a different card. That is what lets an administrative body scale past
  the throughput of one clerk. It is also what makes bureaucracy possible as an
  organisational form, since a large office can put dozens of clerks on one collection
  simultaneously. The French revolutionary catalogue of 1791, with librarians across the
  country working on separate collections at once, is its first documented use. The
  standardised card also raises `execution delegability`: filing and retrieving by a
  fixed rule is work an untrained clerk can do.
- **Cross-reference.** One fact can be filed under several headings by duplicating the
  card. A person can be found by name, address and offence; a book by author, title and
  subject. The collection stops being a list and becomes a queryable structure.

Together these convert a register from something a state reads into something it can
search. That raises `legibility` far beyond what counting alone provides: FIN-020's
census and cadastre record a population, while a card index makes any individual in it
findable.

**The cost is integrity.** Cards can be lost, stolen, misfiled or reordered by
accident, and there is no binding to guarantee the file is complete. A ledger's rigidity
is an audit property. Pages are numbered and sewn, so a removed page shows, and an
inserted entry is visibly out of sequence. The card index trades that integrity for
flexibility, and so weakens the evidentiary assurance that `contract enforcement` rests
on. That is why financial records stayed bound long after everything else moved to
cards. Double-entry bookkeeping (FIN-060) is a self-auditing method built on the bound
form, and it had no reason to give up the one property that made its books evidence.

**The state makes a person findable by body.** The heaviest use of the card index was
identification:

- **Bertillon's anthropometric files** from 1883 let the Paris police find a repeat
  offender among thousands by his measurements, whatever name he gave.
- **Fingerprint classification** replaced it. It was developed in Calcutta in 1897
  under Henry by Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose, and independently at La Plata by
  Vucetich in 1891, and it did the same with a mark that could not be changed or faked.

In both, the classification is what makes the card index work. A body becomes a filing
code, and the code leads to the drawer. Police administration (LAW-110) took on a
searchable memory of individuals, not only a force on the street.

**Registers that states depend on become searchable.** The rolls behind conscription
(WAR-200) and compulsory schooling (LAW-165) had to find individuals, not merely count
them. The twentieth-century population register built on individual cards lets a state
follow a person from address to address and identify every member of a category. Under
occupation the Dutch registry was used to find and deport Jews. MTH-075 carries the
machine version of the same capability. The paper version did it without machines, by
concurrency and cross-reference alone.

**The index leaves the library and enters the office.** Dewey's classification and the
Library Bureau turned the card, the drawer and the cabinet into standard office
equipment, and vertical filing from the 1890s carried it into business. The card became
the unit of the customer, the employee and the stock item, and Taylorist management ran
on cards that routed work through a factory. The library was where the technology was
worked out, and the office and the state were where it mattered most.

**The machine is the direct descendant.** Hollerith's punched card (MTH-075) is a card
index a machine can read. It keeps the unit record, the mobility and the re-sorting,
removes the clerk from the sort, and so turns an administrative medium into a
computational one. The database (MED-150) completes the lineage: records with no
physical position at all, re-sorted and cross-referenced without cards.

## Social consequence

At human scale the card index made a new kind of work and a new kind of exposure. The
filing clerk, the card cataloguer and the records officer were large and substantially
female occupations, doing work classified as routine because the card had made it
rule-bound. Libraries, insurers, banks, police bureaux and government departments
filled rooms with cabinets and the staff to work them.

For the people recorded, the card index removed the protection of being hard to find.
A person who moved house, changed name or gave a false one could once slip out of a
bound register kept in one parish. A cross-referenced card file followed the person, and
a fingerprint card identified them regardless of name. That was the point for police
catching repeat offenders, and it was the same capability when an occupation authority
used a population register to find people for deportation. Resisters understood this,
which is why they set fire to the Amsterdam registry: destroying the cards was destroying
the state's ability to find people.

Scholarship used the same technology, and it is one application among many rather than
the frame. The Oxford English Dictionary was compiled from millions of quotation slips
sent in by volunteer readers, concurrency spread across a whole reading public. The
Grimms' German dictionary ran on slips for more than a century. Niklas Luhmann's
Zettelkasten of some ninety thousand cards was built around cross-reference. These show
the medium's properties clearly; they are not what made it consequential.

## Regional specifics

Checked deliberately, the material is concentrated in Europe and North America for the
library and office history, and genuinely wider for identification and registration.
The library catalogue's development runs from Gessner through revolutionary France and
Vienna to Dewey, and the office equipment industry is American. The regions add:

- **South Asia.** It supplied the fingerprint classification that the world's police
  adopted, worked out by two Indian officers in Calcutta, and colonial registration gave
  it a population to index.
- **Latin America.** Argentina reached an independent fingerprint file at La Plata at
  the same moment.
- **East Asia.** The card catalogue had to be re-engineered to file characters with no
  alphabetical order (MED-060), which produced the four-corner method and its rivals.

The card index was not universal, and the absences are chosen, not backward. Ottoman
administration kept court and survey records in bound registers because their authority
depended on completeness. Japan's koseki recorded households in bound registers for the
same reason. European ledgers and land registers stayed bound wherever tamper-evidence
mattered.

## Contested

Two things are disputed. How much a card register's efficiency caused outcomes
attributed to it is unsettled, most sharply for the Dutch deportation rate, which
comparative work attributes to several causes together. Model a thorough register as
lowering the cost of finding a category of people, with its share in any particular
outcome contested.

Krajewski's claim that slip-filing ranks among the period's most consequential
administrative technologies is an argument for neglected infrastructure, and others give
more weight to typewriters, carbon copies, telephones or organisational reform. Model the
card index as a necessary component of large-scale record administration, not its only
cause.

## Hook

A record medium with four properties a bound register lacks. It unlocks administration
at scale and gives up tamper-evidence.

- **Mobility.** Records re-sort with no transcription cost, so one collection answers
  many queries.
- **Extensibility.** Insertion in place lets a collection grow without limit.
- **Concurrency.** Many clerks work one collection at once, so administrative throughput
  scales past a single writer and `execution delegability` rises as filing becomes
  rule-bound.
- **Cross-reference.** Duplicate cards make a collection queryable.

**Legibility becomes search.** Together these raise `legibility` from counting a
population to finding any individual in it. Paired with a body-based classification, as
in anthropometry or fingerprints, a person becomes findable regardless of name.

**Integrity is the cost.** Cards can vanish or be inserted without trace, so the medium
lowers the evidentiary assurance behind `contract enforcement`. Records that must prove
completeness, such as ledgers, land registers, court records and household registers,
should stay bound, and that should be the correct choice rather than a lag.

**Purpose-indifference.** A card register can be destroyed to blind the state that relies
on it, and it is indifferent to purpose in the same way as its machine descendant.

**The next step.** Adding a machine that reads the card moves the medium into computation
(MTH-075).
