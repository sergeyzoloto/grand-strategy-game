The exact inverse of MED-020. A script that encodes meaning rather than
sound can be read by people who cannot speak to one another — so writing
becomes capable of unifying a polity that speech cannot, and the price is
paid in the cost of becoming a reader.

## Timing

From Qin and Han standardisation around -200, through the script's spread
into Korea, Japan and Vietnam from roughly 400, to the displacement of
Literary Chinese as an administrative medium around 1900. Akkadian
cuneiform performs a related function from around -2500.

## Mechanism

**A phonetic script encodes sound, so it is readable only by someone who
knows the language. A logographic script encodes meaning, so it is not.**
That single difference produces everything else. A graph standing for a
concept can be pronounced however the reader's own language pronounces
that concept, or not pronounced at all, and the meaning survives intact.
Writing therefore reaches across a language boundary that speech cannot
cross.

The political consequence is direct. A polity containing mutually
unintelligible speech communities can still administer, examine, legislate
and transmit a canon in one written medium, without imposing a spoken
language on anyone. China did this across Cantonese, Hokkien, Wu and
Mandarin for two millennia; the medium then continued to work beyond the
border, in Korea, Japan and Vietnam, whose elites read the same texts while
speaking unrelated languages. **Brush-talk** is the demonstration in
miniature — two officials with no common speech holding a fluent
conversation on paper.

**The cost is the mirror image of the benefit, and the entry must present
it as a trade rather than a defect.** Thousands of graphs put `literacy
training cost` high and keep it there, which means literacy stays
expensive, the literate remain a minority, and `intermediary dependence`
stays correspondingly high — a population deals with the written world
through people who can read it. It also binds the literate class to a
canon, because the years required to learn the script are spent learning it
on particular texts, so script mastery and canonical formation arrive
together rather than separately.

**MED-020 and this entry are opposite trades on one variable, and neither
is the better technology.** The alphabet buys cheap readers and pays by
fragmenting the readership along language lines — every vernacular
eventually needs its own literature, and a text stops travelling when the
language stops. The logograph buys a readership spanning languages and pays
in expensive readers. A model that ranks them has misread the trade: one
optimises the number of readers, the other the reach across them, and which
is worth more depends entirely on whether the polity in question is
linguistically uniform.

## Social consequence

The literate class is smaller, more uniform, and more transnational than an
alphabetic society's. Smaller because entry is expensive; more uniform
because everyone paid the same tuition in the same texts; transnational
because the qualification is portable across borders in a way that
fluency in a vernacular is not. A Korean official and a Chinese official
had read the same books and could establish that within minutes, in
writing, without a word passing between them.

That produces a scholarly world with an unusual shape — dense, durable,
and detached from any one state. It also produces a very sharp boundary.
There is no partial membership, because a script requiring years of study
admits no casual reader, so the line between inside and outside is a wall
rather than a gradient. Alphabetic societies get a spectrum of partial
literacies; this one does not.

## Regional specifics

The East Asian cases carry the entry and the Vietnamese one carries the
cost explicitly, since Literary Chinese served office and canon while the
locally invented chu Nom developed alongside it to write Vietnamese —
two scripts maintained at once because they did different jobs. Akkadian
cuneiform in the Amarna correspondence shows the mechanism operating two
millennia earlier and outside East Asia entirely, which is what establishes
it as a property of semantically anchored writing rather than a feature of
one civilisation.

Europe is carried as an absence and it is a genuine alternative rather than
a lack. Latin unified learned writing across Europe's vernaculars for a
millennium, but by making every reader acquire a second language whole —
the canon-language mechanism, not this one. The two solutions place the cost in
different places, and Europe could not have chosen this one, because a
script that encodes sound cannot be read by someone who does not know the
sounds.

## Hook

Buys a readership that spans mutually unintelligible languages, which lets
writing hold together a polity that speech cannot — administration,
examination and canon running in one medium across many spoken languages,
and continuing to work past the border, so the scholarly community is wider
than the state. The price is a permanently high `literacy training cost`
and therefore a permanently high `intermediary dependence`, with a literate
class bound to the canon its script was learned on. It is the exact
opposite trade to MED-020 on the same variable and must not be rankable
against it: the alphabet optimises the number of readers and fragments them
by language, this optimises reach across languages and restricts their
number. Which pays depends on whether the polity is linguistically uniform,
so the same choice should be correct in one place and wrong in another.
Membership should be a wall rather than a gradient — no casual readers,
because a script needing years of study admits none.
