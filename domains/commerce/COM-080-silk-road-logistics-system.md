A relay network of overland caravan routes connecting East Asia to the
Mediterranean world through Central Asia — not a single road but a
constantly-renegotiated chain of regional trade relationships, oasis cities,
and toll-collecting intermediary polities.

## Timing

Organized long-distance overland trade along these routes is attested from
roughly -130 (traditionally linked to Han dynasty diplomatic and commercial
outreach westward), with the network's peak volume and best documentation
coming under the 13th-14th century Pax Mongolica, before maritime routes
(MAR-160) progressively displace it by the 1400s-1450s.

## Mechanism

Overland caravan trade depends on a chain of oasis cities and intermediary
polities each controlling a segment of the route, each extracting toll or
taking a trade margin for safe passage and provisioning through their
territory — the route's total cost and reliability is a function of how many
such tolls and security risks a shipment must clear, which is why political
fragmentation along the route (many small tolling polities) raises costs
sharply while a single unifying security umbrella (the Mongol case)
collapses them.

## Social consequence

Oasis cities along the route (Samarkand, Kashgar, Dunhuang among many
others) develop disproportionately cosmopolitan, multi-ethnic merchant
populations relative to their surrounding agricultural hinterland, their
prosperity and cultural character tied directly to route traffic rather
than to local agricultural production — a demonstration that a settlement's
economic importance can be almost entirely a function of its position in a
logistics network rather than of its own resource base.

## Regional specifics

Southern branches through South Asia connect the overland network directly
to Indian Ocean maritime trade, making the subcontinent a hinge between two
otherwise separate logistics systems rather than merely a waypoint on one.
The Mongol Pax Mongolica's dramatic lowering of transit risk and toll
friction across the entire route, enabling documented figures like Marco
Polo to complete the full overland journey, shows the route's throughput was
gated as much by political security conditions as by physical distance or
terrain. Europe's position as the network's peripheral receiving end for
nearly its entire operational history complicates the popular "East-West
bridge" framing — direct European participation in the actual logistics is
minimal until the maritime alternative bypasses the overland system
altogether.

## Contested

Whether the Silk Road functioned as a genuinely continuous route some
merchants and goods traveled end to end, or as a relay chain of shorter
regional trades with goods and price information passing through many
intermediary hands, is debated. Model it as a relay network by default —
goods and information propagating hop by hop through a chain of merchant
communities — with true long-distance individual through-travel treated as
a special condition unlocked specifically during well-documented
security-umbrella periods like the Pax Mongolica, not as the network's
normal operating mode.

## Hook

The Silk Road's throughput and cost are a direct function of political
fragmentation versus security-umbrella conditions along the route — the same
physical distance and terrain can support either a slow, toll-heavy trickle
of `route knowledge` propagating hop by hop, or a fast, high-volume flow,
purely depending on whether one power (as with the Mongols) or many
independent tolling polities control the intervening territory.
