American silver mining (1545–1650) floods Atlantic Europe with specie and, via
the Manila galleon, feeds a Chinese fiscal system that has just converted itself
onto a silver standard — one supply shock and one demand shock landing on each
other from opposite sides of the planet.

## Timing

Cerro Rico at Potosí discovered 1545; mercury amalgamation (the patio process)
scales extraction from the 1550s. The Manila galleon route opens 1565, linking
Acapulco to Manila to the Chinese mainland market. Chinese fiscal silver demand
intensifies through the 1570s as the Single Whip reform spreads. Flow and price
effects peak across the early-to-mid 1600s; the window closes with the Ming
collapse (1644) and a mid-century slump in American mining output.

## Mechanism

Two shocks land on the same commodity from opposite ends of a global market: a
monetary-base shock (Spain injects a huge, exogenous supply of specie into
Atlantic Europe) and a demand shock (Ming China needs silver to run its own
fiscal system, having just converted tax obligations to silver payment). The
Spanish crown taxes the flow at the point of extraction (the quinto real, one
fifth) but cannot convert the windfall into durable productive capacity — it
borrows against future shipments instead, which transmits a resource-abundance
shock into serial sovereign credit failure. The underlying cost that moves is
the price of long-distance credit denominated in a commodity whose supply is now
set by a mine on another continent, not by the borrower's own economy.

## Social consequence

In Castile, rentier and noble incomes fixed in silver terms erode in real value
as the price level roughly triples across the period (the Price Revolution),
while anyone positioned to reprice contracts captures a windfall — a quiet
redistribution running well below the level of policy. In Ming China, fiscal
centralization onto a silver standard makes state revenue hostage to a commodity
the state does not mine and cannot print, wiring central fiscal health to
Potosí's output on the other side of the world — a dependency nobody in Beijing
chose or could see coming.

## Regional specifics

China's Single Whip reform (from the 1570s) is not a downstream *effect* of the
Atlantic price revolution — it's an independent structural demand shock that
happens to be satisfiable only by the same Atlantic supply, which is what makes
the silver market genuinely global rather than two unconnected regional stories.
Japan, by contrast, is producing silver domestically at meaningful scale in the
same window (see `absence` above) and sits on the export side of the same
commodity that the rest of East Asia is absorbing — a reminder that "silver
shock" is not a uniform regional experience even within one macro-region.

## Contested

Hamilton's classic quantity-theory account treats the price rise as a direct,
sufficient consequence of bullion inflow expanding the money supply. Rival
accounts credit European population growth outrunning food and land supply
(pushing prices up independent of any silver), and repeated coinage debasement
by fiscal-strapped crowns (a monetary cause that isn't the American bullion at
all) as equally load-bearing.

Model as three independently-triggerable, weighted contributions to a single
`price level` stat rather than scripting one cause as *the* explanation: bullion
inflow rate, population/land-ratio pressure, and debasement events. A region or
timeframe with little debasement history and flat population growth should show
a cleaner bullion-driven signature than one where all three are firing at once —
letting the contested weighting surface as regional variation in outcome, not as
a single dice roll the player never sees.

## Hook

Silver inflow is a conditional convertor, not a flat bonus: a polity holding a
working tax-and-credit institution converts bullion inflow into durable state
capacity (`sovereign credit capacity` rises), while a polity without one takes
the same inflow and converts it into serial sovereign default and resource-curse
volatility instead — the historical Spanish crown defaulted repeatedly across
this exact window (1557, 1560, 1575, 1596/1607). Same input stat, opposite
output, gated entirely by prior institutional state (design principle 1).
