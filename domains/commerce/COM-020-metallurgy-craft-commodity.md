The smelting chemistry itself is ENG's territory, and the bronze-to-iron
shift's military and social consequence is WAR-020's; this entry owns
metalworking as an organized craft trade and its output as a tradeable
commodity, from the earliest Bronze Age guilds through the long era in
which iron and steel both remain comparatively scarce, skilled, craft-
scale goods — steel's much later conversion into a cheap bulk commodity
is a distinct, separately-owned entry (ENG-260).

## Timing

Bronze Age metalworking guilds organize as specialized craft trades from
roughly -3300; South Asian wootz steel export flourishes from around
-300; iron and steel both remain comparatively expensive, craft-scale
goods across every region covered here through this entry's close around
1500, well before any process innovation changes that basic economics.

## Mechanism

Metalworking requires enough specialized, non-transferable skill that it
organizes into guild-like craft structures wherever it's practiced at
scale, converting raw ore availability into a value-added tradeable
commodity whose price reflects skilled labor and fuel input as much as
raw material cost — a pattern that holds consistently across bronze and
early iron/steel working alike, since the underlying skill-scarcity
driving guild formation doesn't depend on which specific metal is being
worked.

## Social consequence

Where metalworking organizes as hereditary or guild-restricted craft
knowledge, it confers durable social standing and often ritual or
quasi-magical status on smiths across many independent traditions — a
recurring pattern for a craft whose product (weapons, tools) is both
economically valuable and physically transformative in a way that impresses
onlookers regardless of the smith's actual social class origin.

## Regional specifics

South Asian wootz steel's export as a high-value finished good, feeding
directly into the Middle Eastern Damascus steel tradition, shows
metallurgical craft skill itself functioning as an exportable commodity
independent of raw ore trade. West African ironworking guilds' comparable
social organization and ritual status, developed with no contact with
Old World guild traditions, suggests craft-guild structure is a close-to-
inevitable social response to concentrated specialized skill rather than
a culturally specific institution. Europe's steel staying craft-scale and
expensive throughout this entry's entire window, only breaking free of
that pattern with the much later Bessemer process (ENG-260), shows
craft-commodity status can persist for millennia without any process
innovation forcing a change.

## Hook

Metalworking commodifies as a guild-organized craft trade wherever it's
practiced at scale, with price reflecting skilled labor and fuel input as
much as raw ore — a stable craft-commodity economics that holds across
bronze and early iron alike for millennia, until a specific later process
innovation (ENG-260) breaks the pattern for one metal at a time rather
than all at once.
