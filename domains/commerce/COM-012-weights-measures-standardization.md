A buyer and seller can only trust a transaction's stated quantity if both
sides agree what the unit actually means — standardized weights and
measures are a quiet precondition for market trade at any distance beyond
face-to-face haggling over a specific physical lot.

## Timing

Indus Valley standardized weights are attested from roughly -2600; Qin
China's unification standards date to -221; European standardization lags
both by millennia in practice, only reaching continental consistency with
the metric system from 1795 and its gradual international adoption through
the following century.

## Mechanism

Without a trusted common standard, every transaction between parties from
different markets requires either face-to-face verification of the actual
goods or a trusted intermediary to vouch for measurement — standardization
removes that friction entirely, letting a merchant trade sight-unseen across
long distances on the strength of a stated weight or measure alone, directly
extending how far a market can practically integrate (feeding into route-
knowledge-dependent trade like MAR-001's coastal networks and beyond).

## Social consequence

Whoever controls the standard — a temple, a state mint, an imperial edict —
gains a quiet but pervasive form of authority, since every transaction using
that standard implicitly recognizes it; conversely, a merchant caught using
false weights faces a trust penalty that can be more damaging than the
immediate fraud's value, since market reputation depends on being a reliable
counterparty for future trades far more than on any single deal.

## Regional specifics

The Indus Valley's cross-site weight consistency, without any evidence of a
single unifying political authority comparable to Qin's, suggests
standardization can emerge from sustained merchant practice and convergent
convention rather than only from top-down imperial imposition. Qin China's
simultaneous standardization of weights, measures, and infrastructure in one
administrative act shows the alternative, faster top-down path. Europe's
persistent, costly fragmentation well into the early modern era —
standardization arriving millennia after the Indus case — is a genuine
absence with real trade-friction costs, not resolved until a deliberate,
much later political project (the metric system) forced convergence.

## Hook

A trusted common standard for weight and measure directly extends the
distance and trust-depth of `contract enforcement` in trade, letting
transactions happen sight-unseen — and whichever institution controls that
standard, market authority or state edict, gains a pervasive, hard-to-
displace form of quiet leverage over every transaction that uses it.
