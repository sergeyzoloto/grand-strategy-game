A tariff is simultaneously a revenue tool, a domestic-industry shield, and,
once other states retaliate, a weapon in its own right — and whichever
state doesn't control its own tariff rate has lost a lever every other
sovereign economic actor in this record takes for granted.

## Timing

Tariff policy becomes a major, self-consciously wielded tool of industrial
policy from the post-Napoleonic protectionist era (from 1815, particularly
in the United States and German states), through a cycle of retaliatory
tariff escalation recurring across the following century.

## Mechanism

A protective tariff raises the price of imported goods relative to domestic
production, shielding domestic industry from foreign competition at the
direct cost of consumers paying more and trading partners losing market
access — when a trading partner retaliates with its own tariffs in response,
the result is a tariff war whose costs compound on both sides, since each
round of retaliation further restricts trade volume without either side
necessarily gaining the protective benefit that motivated the first move.

## Social consequence

Domestic industries protected by tariffs gain a durable constituency with a
direct financial stake in maintaining and extending protection, making
tariff policy notoriously difficult to reverse even once its costs to
consumers and export-oriented sectors become clear — protection creates its
own defending interest group the moment it's imposed.

## Regional specifics

Qing China's loss of tariff autonomy through the unequal treaties following
the Opium Wars is one of the starkest cases in this record of tariff-setting
itself, not just market access, becoming the direct object of external
coercion — China could not raise import duties above treaty-fixed rates
regardless of its own domestic industrial policy needs. The Ottoman Empire's
comparable loss of tariff autonomy accumulates gradually through decades of
bilateral capitulation agreements rather than one coercive settlement,
showing the same sovereignty-over-tariffs erosion can arrive by either a
single sharp shock or a slow accumulation of smaller concessions. Colonial
India's salt tax shows a state-protected monopoly's evasion following the
same enforcement-capacity-versus-price-gap logic ordinary smuggling runs
on elsewhere (MAR-150), but escalating into organized civil disobedience
once a movement deliberately mobilizes around it — evasion of a
subsistence-good monopoly carries a politically mobilizable weight an
ordinary luxury-good tariff rarely does.

## Hook

Tariff-setting is a sovereignty lever as fundamental as currency or
military force — losing control over it, whether abruptly (unequal treaty)
or gradually (accumulated capitulations), removes a state's ability to use
its own market access as either a shield or a bargaining chip, with
retaliatory tariff wars compounding losses on all sides once the lever is
pulled, and evasion of a monopoly or tariff on a subsistence good carrying
political-mobilization potential a luxury-good tariff never does.
