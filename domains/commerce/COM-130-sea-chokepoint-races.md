This entry owns the peacetime commercial logic of controlling a narrow
strait or canal — tolls, basing rights, and diplomatic leverage; MAR-220
already owns the wartime sea-lane-denial doctrine that the same geography
enables once a conflict starts.

## Timing

Portuguese efforts to control the Strait of Malacca from 1511 are an early
case; European strategic focus on the Suez route intensifies from 1798,
sharpening after the canal opens in 1869; the Panama Canal (opened 1914,
following the engineered 1903 secession of Panama from Colombia) applies
the same logic to a chokepoint built rather than found; strategic contest
over the Strait of Hormuz continues into the modern era this entry's window
extends toward, sharply visible again in 1979-1980.

## Mechanism

A small number of narrow maritime passages carry a disproportionate share
of the world's long-distance shipping simply because the alternative routes
are dramatically longer or don't exist at all — whoever controls that
passage, whether by adjacent territorial sovereignty, canal ownership, or
naval basing rights, can extract tolls, restrict access, or simply hold
leverage over every state whose trade depends on the route, converting a
narrow strip of geography into commercial and diplomatic power far out of
proportion to its physical size.

## Social consequence

States without their own chokepoint access become structurally dependent on
whichever power does control the relevant passage, a dependency that
recurringly shapes alliance behavior and diplomatic posture toward the
controlling power — access to a contested strait becomes a recurring
bargaining chip in international relations entirely independent of any
direct military conflict actually occurring there.

## Regional specifics

The Suez case shows how a canal can be actively engineered to create an
entirely new, enormously valuable chokepoint where none existed before,
while the Panama case shows the same logic can extend to actively
engineering the surrounding political geography (Panama's manufactured
independence) to secure control of it. The Cape of Good Hope route's sharp
loss of strategic value once Suez opens demonstrates a chokepoint's worth is
inherently relative to available alternatives, not an intrinsic property of
the geography itself — the same coastline that was once essential becomes a
commercial backwater the moment a shorter route opens elsewhere.

## Hook

Control of a narrow, alternative-poor maritime passage converts a small
geographic footprint into disproportionate toll revenue and diplomatic
`protection rent`-style leverage — a chokepoint's value is entirely relative
to available alternative routes, so it can be created (a new canal),
destroyed (a shorter competing route), or fought over (MAR-220's wartime
mechanic) without anything physically changing at the chokepoint itself.
