Fixed steel rail massively lowers the cost and raises the speed and
reliability of overland bulk cargo movement relative to any pack-animal or
wagon-road alternative (MIG-040), converting land transport economics
almost as much as containerization (MAR-370) later converts sea transport.

## Timing

Commercial steam railways scale from the Stockton and Darlington line
(1825), with major national and colonial networks built out across Europe,
the Americas, South Asia, and elsewhere through the following nine decades,
substantially complete in their core continental configurations by the
outbreak of World War One (1914-1916).

## Mechanism

A railway converts overland bulk transport cost from a function of
pack-animal or draft-wagon capacity (MIG-040's ceiling) into a function of
locomotive power and track capacity, an order-of-magnitude cost and speed
improvement that makes interior regions with no navigable river or coastal
access commercially reachable for the first time — but a rail network's
actual economic effect depends entirely on its route design, since a line
built purely to move a single export commodity to a port serves a
completely different function than a network connecting many population
and production centers to each other.

## Social consequence

Railway construction and operation demand enormous, often coerced or
harshly exploited labor forces (Chinese labor on the American
transcontinental line, and various indentured and forced-labor
arrangements on colonial lines elsewhere), while the finished network's
benefits accrue very unevenly between the financing capital, the
colonial or national state, and the population living along or dependent
on the route — the same infrastructure investment can be simultaneously a
genuine development asset and an extraction instrument depending on who
actually captures the resulting value.

## Regional specifics

Indian railways' guaranteed-return financing structure — which paid British
investors a fixed return regardless of a line's actual profitability,
funded ultimately by Indian tax revenue — is a specific financial mechanism
(FIN-adjacent) that shifted commercial risk away from the financiers and
onto the colonized population, independent of whatever the network's
physical infrastructure value turned out to be. The American transcontinental
line's dependence on Chinese immigrant labor under markedly worse conditions
than white workers received directly connects this entry to the gold-rush
migration and subsequent legal-exclusion history already covered in this
record's migration domain. African colonial rail networks' near-universal
interior-to-port design, rather than interconnected domestic networks, shows
route design itself — not just financing terms — determines whether a
railway serves extraction or integration.

## Contested

Whether colonial railway construction net-benefited or net-extracted from
the colonized economy is disputed and plausibly varies by specific network
rather than having one universal answer. Model each network's domestic-
integration value and its extractive-logistics value as separately scored
effects determined by its actual route design (interior-to-port versus
interconnected) and financing structure (guaranteed-return versus market-
risk), rather than assuming a single verdict applies to every colonial-era
railway alike.

## Hook

Rail infrastructure converts overland cargo cost and `route knowledge` value
by an order of magnitude over pack-animal transport (MIG-040), but whether a
specific network serves extraction or integration is entirely a function of
its route design and financing structure — the same steel-rail technology
producing opposite economic outcomes depending on who designed the network
and who bears its financial risk.
