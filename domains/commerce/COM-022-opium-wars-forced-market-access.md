This entry owns the war's trade-forcing mechanism and its treaty-port and
tariff-sovereignty consequences (COM-018); the underlying commodity's own
addiction and social-epidemic dynamics inside China are HLT-019's territory,
not duplicated here.

## Timing

The First Opium War runs 1839-1842, ending with the Treaty of Nanking; a
Second Opium War (1856-1860) extends and deepens the same forced-market-
access settlement, opening additional treaty ports and further restricting
Qing tariff autonomy (COM-018).

## Mechanism

Qing China restricts foreign trade to a single controlled port (Canton) and
attempts to suppress opium imports specifically on public-health grounds;
Britain's military response converts a market-access dispute into a forced
settlement opening multiple additional treaty ports, fixing low tariff
rates Qing China cannot unilaterally raise (COM-018), and establishing
extraterritorial legal jurisdiction for foreign nationals — trade access
achieved through military coercion rather than negotiation, with the
military and commercial objectives inseparable from each other in the
settlement's actual terms.

## Social consequence

The resulting treaty-port system creates enclave zones of foreign legal and
commercial privilege embedded within Chinese territory, a durable
humiliation in subsequent Chinese national memory (the "Century of
Humiliation" framing) that shapes Chinese political and economic policy
for a century afterward — the settlement's terms remain a live reference
point in trade and sovereignty politics long after the treaty ports
themselves are gone.

## Regional specifics

British India's role as the opium production base under colonial monopoly
makes South Asia a supplier within this same coercive system rather than a
parallel case of its own market being forced open — the Bengal opium
monopoly directly funded and supplied the trade whose forced continuation
the wars secured, tying two different colonial and semi-colonial
relationships (India as producer, China as forced market) into one
integrated commercial system.

## Contested

Whether the Opium Wars are best framed primarily as trade-liberalization
conflicts or primarily as wars to preserve a drug-trafficking profit stream
against a target government's public-health suppression effort is disputed,
with both descriptions genuinely applying to the same events. Model the
war's trigger as carrying both mechanics simultaneously — market-access
coercion and addictive-substance-trade protection (linking directly to
HLT-019's social-epidemic mechanic) — rather than requiring the model to
adjudicate which framing is the single correct one.

## Hook

Military force converts a restricted, contested market into a treaty-
mandated open one, fixing tariff terms (COM-018) the losing side cannot
unilaterally revise and creating durable extraterritorial privilege zones —
a forced-market-access mechanic whose commercial and coercive elements
cannot be cleanly separated from each other in the settlement's own terms.
