An unbroken chain of refrigerated storage and transport from producer to
final consumer, extending perishable goods' viable trade range from purely
local (whatever spoils before it arrives) to genuinely global.

## Timing

The first successful long-range refrigerated shipping demonstration (1876,
Argentina to France) opens the technology's commercial era; cold-chain
infrastructure — refrigerated rail cars, ships, warehouses, and eventually
retail refrigeration — builds out progressively through the following
seven decades.

## Mechanism

Without refrigeration, a perishable good's trade range is set entirely by
how fast it spoils relative to how fast it can be transported — a hard
ceiling no amount of route knowledge (MAR-001) or transport speed
improvement alone can fully overcome for genuinely perishable goods. An
unbroken cold chain removes that ceiling by holding temperature low enough
to arrest spoilage for the entire journey, converting perishables into
goods that can be traded over the same long distances as any non-perishable
commodity — but the chain only works if every single link (storage,
transport, transfer points) maintains the cold temperature, since a single
broken link spoils the shipment regardless of how well every other stage
performed.

## Social consequence

Cold-chain access restructures entire agricultural economies around
export-oriented perishable production wherever it becomes available —
Argentina's beef industry and similar cases elsewhere reorient substantial
productive capacity toward a distant export market that simply didn't exist
as a viable outlet before refrigerated shipping, while regions without
comparable infrastructure investment remain confined to local or regional
perishable markets regardless of their production quality or volume.

## Regional specifics

Argentina's refrigerated beef trade is the paradigm case of a cold-chain
breakthrough restructuring a national agricultural economy almost
immediately once the technology proves viable. South Asia's comparative
lag in cold-chain infrastructure buildout, persisting well past the
technology's initial proof of concept elsewhere, produces an ongoing,
measurable post-harvest spoilage cost — a case where an infrastructure gap's
consequences compound continuously rather than being a one-time historical
disadvantage that later closes on its own.

## Hook

An unbroken cold chain converts perishable goods from locally-bound to
globally tradeable, but only functions end to end — any single broken link
spoils the shipment regardless of how well every other stage performed,
making cold-chain trade access an all-or-nothing infrastructure investment
rather than one that pays off partially at partial completion.
