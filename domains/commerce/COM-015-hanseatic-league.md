A confederation of merchant guilds and cities, not a state, that
collectively negotiates trade privileges, maintains its own courts and
trading posts (kontors) in foreign cities, and on occasion wages war —
extending Northern River Navigation's Baltic trade routes (MAR-006) into a
formal, self-governing commercial institution.

## Timing

Formally organized from 1358 (though cooperative practice among member
cities predates the formal league by roughly a century), reaching peak
influence across the Baltic and North Sea trade in the 14th-15th centuries,
declining gradually as centralizing territorial states absorb its member
cities' autonomy, with the last formal assembly meeting in 1669.

## Mechanism

Individual trading cities lack the leverage to negotiate favorable terms or
enforce contracts against powerful territorial rulers on their own; the
League pools that leverage across dozens of member cities, letting it
collectively threaten trade embargoes, maintain privileged trading posts
(kontors) with their own legal jurisdiction in foreign cities like Novgorod,
Bruges, and London, and occasionally back its economic demands with naval
force — converting scattered individual-city bargaining weakness into
collective bargaining strength without requiring the cities to merge into a
single state.

## Social consequence

Merchants operating within a kontor lived under Hanseatic law and jurisdiction
rather than the host city's own, a durable extraterritorial legal privilege
that let League merchants operate with a level of contract security and
predictable dispute resolution a foreign merchant working alone in the same
city could never obtain — a structural advantage entirely separate from
capital or trade goods themselves.

## Regional specifics

The League's specific institutional form — a confederation of nominally
independent cities pooling commercial leverage while individually retaining
local political autonomy — has no close parallel in East Asian commercial
history of the same era, where merchant organization stays embedded within
existing dynastic state authority (guild and lineage networks) rather than
forming a cross-city confederated bloc capable of independently negotiating
with or occasionally opposing sovereign rulers.

## Hook

A merchant league pools scattered individual-city bargaining weakness into
collective negotiating leverage and extraterritorial legal privilege
(`contract enforcement` extended into foreign jurisdictions via the kontor
system) without requiring political unification — a commercial-power
mechanic entirely distinct from, and not requiring, state sovereignty.
