This entry covers land-border and general internal-market smuggling and
gray-market activity — MAR-016 already owns the specifically maritime cases
(haijin-era China, colonial American mercantilism evasion); the underlying
enforcement-capacity-versus-border-length mechanic is shared, but the
land-border theatre and the specific tax-evasion cases here are distinct.

## Timing

Land-border and internal-tax smuggling recurs wherever a tariff, excise, or
state monopoly creates a price gap worth evading; the specific salt-tax
case in British India runs from the tax's colonial-era imposition through
its most famous political consequence, the 1930 Salt March.

## Mechanism

Any state monopoly or tax on an internally-produced or internally-traded
good creates the same evasion incentive MAR-016 describes for maritime
trade restrictions, but along a land border or within a domestic market
instead of a coastline — enforcement capacity again sets the ceiling on how
much of the price gap actually gets captured by the state versus diverted
to evaders, and where the taxed good is a basic subsistence necessity
(salt, in the Indian case) rather than a luxury, evasion becomes both more
widespread and more politically charged.

## Social consequence

Where a monopolized or heavily-taxed good is a basic necessity rather than
a luxury, evasion of that tax can escalate from ordinary economic behavior
into an act with direct political meaning — the Salt March's deliberate,
publicized violation of the salt monopoly converts individual gray-market
behavior that already existed at scale into an organized, symbolically
loaded act of collective resistance, a transformation ordinary luxury-good
smuggling rarely undergoes.

## Regional specifics

The Indian salt-tax case shows gray-market evasion of a subsistence-good
monopoly can escalate all the way to organized civil disobedience once a
political movement deliberately mobilizes around it, a trajectory
distinct from ordinary background smuggling. European internal land-border
smuggling, by contrast, stays a chronic low-intensity phenomenon without a
comparable escalation to organized political resistance in this period,
plausibly because the taxed goods involved (tobacco, textiles, and similar)
touched basic subsistence far less directly than salt did in the Indian
case.

## Hook

Smuggling scales with enforcement-capacity gaps just as MAR-016 describes
for maritime trade, but when the evaded tax or monopoly touches a basic
subsistence good rather than a luxury, gray-market evasion becomes newly
available as raw material for organized political resistance rather than
staying purely economic background behavior.
