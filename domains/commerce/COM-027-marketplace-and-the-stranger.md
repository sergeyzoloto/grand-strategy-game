Before any of this domain's later entries can matter, two people who share
no kinship, no community, and no reason to trust each other's word need a
way to exchange goods without either one risking total loss to the
other's bad faith — the precondition every later trade route, standard,
and institution in this domain silently assumes already exists.

## Timing

Documented neutral-ground and third-party-guaranteed exchange arrangements
appear independently across multiple regions from roughly -3500 through
-1000, with specific institutional forms (karum colonies, temple markets,
market-judge-supervised plazas) reaching mature, well-documented form at
different points within that window in different regions.

## Mechanism

A transaction between kin or community members is self-enforcing because
both parties expect to interact again and both answer to the same social
network if either cheats; a transaction with a genuine stranger has
neither guarantee. Solving this requires substituting some other
enforcement mechanism — neutral or sacred ground where violence and fraud
carry a separate, heavier penalty (a temple precinct, a market-day truce),
a third party (temple, palace, or eventually state) who guarantees the
weight or measure and takes a cut for the service, or an on-the-spot
adjudicator empowered to punish cheating immediately rather than leave it
to reputation. Whichever mechanism a society adopts, it converts trade's
maximum safe radius from "as far as my kin network reaches" into "as far
as this guarantee institution's reach and credibility extend."

## Social consequence

Whoever provides the neutral-ground guarantee — a temple, a palace, a
market judge, a treaty-protected merchant colony's own recognized
procedure — accumulates a foundational, largely invisible form of
authority: every stranger-to-stranger transaction using the guarantee
implicitly depends on and reinforces it, long before that authority shows
up in any more visible political form. A market or colony without a
credible guarantor simply doesn't attract stranger-trade at all, regardless
of what goods might otherwise be exchanged there.

## Regional specifics

The Assyrian karum system shows the guarantee mechanism can be treaty-
portable — the same recognized dispute procedure travels with the merchant
colony into a foreign city rather than depending on local institutions at
all. The Aztec tianguis reaches a comparably rigorous solution (a
permanent, empowered market judge) through entirely independent
development, contradicting any notion that reliable stranger-trade
institutions are a distinctively Old World achievement. Aboriginal
Australian long-distance exchange shows a society can move goods across
comparably vast distances without ever generating a market-as-place
institution at all — kinship-mediated ceremonial gathering solves the same
distance problem through a structurally different mechanism, not a lesser
one.

## Contested

Whether market exchange differentiated gradually out of embedded kin-
reciprocity economies, or existed alongside kin exchange as a separate
stranger-trade sphere from very early on (documented "silent trade"
practices, where parties never meet and simply adjust left goods until
both accept, suggest the latter), is a live, unresolved anthropological
debate. Model both as available, region-dependent starting
configurations for how a given society first solves the stranger-trust
problem, rather than one universal sequence.

## Hook

A market's `market trust radius` is set by whichever guarantee mechanism
(neutral ground, market judge, treaty-portable procedure) a society has
established, not by physical distance alone — trade with a genuine
stranger is impossible below that radius regardless of how valuable the
potential exchange would be, and every later expansion of that radius
(standardized measures, COM-012; portable reputation, MIG-009; brand
trust, COM-033) is a variation on the same underlying problem this entry
first solves.
