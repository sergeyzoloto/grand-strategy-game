A colonized or economically dependent region's productive capacity narrows
around one or two export commodities, leaving it structurally exposed to
that commodity's price volatility and to the buyer economies that set the
terms of trade for it.

## Timing

Commodity concentration under colonial administration intensifies from the
mid-1600s Caribbean and American plantation economies, through the Dutch
Java Cultivation System (from 1830) and African colonial cash-cropping (from
the 1880s partition), with the resulting dependency structures persisting
for many former colonies well past formal political independence, into the
1970s and beyond.

## Mechanism

A single-commodity export economy captures short-term gains from
specialization and from whatever infrastructure (ports, railways) the
colonizing or dominant trading power builds to move that commodity out
efficiently, but at the cost of near-total exposure to that one commodity's
price — a price the exporting economy typically has little power to
influence, since it's usually set in the buyer economy's markets — converting
what may begin as a rational specialization decision into a structural
dependency that's very costly to exit once other productive capacity has
atrophied.

## Social consequence

Populations in a commodity-trap economy experience boom periods tied
directly to that commodity's price cycles, with genuine local benefit during
upswings, followed by severe downturns when the same price cycles fall —
producing a volatility in local livelihood entirely dictated by demand
conditions and price-setting decisions made in a distant market the local
population has no influence over.

## Regional specifics

The Dutch Cultivation System in Java and various African colonial cash-crop
mandates show the mechanism operating through direct administrative
compulsion rather than emergent market response — colonial administrations
frequently didn't wait for comparative advantage to produce specialization
on its own, they mandated it. The Ottoman core's relative success at
avoiding comparable single-commodity lock-in, despite real trade
concessions under the capitulation system (COM-150), suggests retained
fiscal and administrative sovereignty — not merely unequal trade terms
generally — is what most reliably prevents the trap from closing.

## Contested

Whether commodity-trap specialization was primarily externally imposed
against the colonized economy's own interest, or in some cases reflected a
genuine comparative-advantage choice that only became a "trap" once
external terms of trade shifted unfavorably, is disputed between dependency-
theory and more market-oriented accounts of the same historical cases.
Model each instance as a mixture of imposed-coercion and genuine-advantage
components in a case-specific ratio, rather than requiring a single verdict
that applies uniformly across every commodity-dependent economy in this
record.

## Hook

Commodity concentration captures short-term specialization gains at the
cost of long-term price-volatility exposure and lost productive diversity —
a trap whose entry can be either coerced (direct administrative mandate) or
seemingly voluntary (comparative-advantage response), with `protection
rent`-style external terms-of-trade control determining how much of the
resulting value the dependent economy actually captures either way.
