A fixed-location shop selling directly to a walk-in local consumer, open
on an ordinary daily rather than periodic schedule — the retail layer
most premodern commercial activity actually ran through day to day,
distinct from both the periodic fair's (COM-090) temporary wholesale
gathering and the manufactory/factory's (COM-100, COM-170) production
side.

## Timing

Fixed-location shops line the agora and later Roman tabernae from
roughly -700; the Middle Eastern suq develops as a permanent, covered,
specialized-by-trade shopping district across the following centuries;
Song dynasty China's shift to continuously-open shopping streets dates
from around 1000, with the fixed urban shop remaining the dominant
day-to-day retail form across major world cities through the eve of
industrial-era mass retail around 1800.

## Mechanism

A shop substitutes ongoing local reputation, built through repeated
daily transactions with a stable neighborhood clientele, for the one-time
or periodic trust mechanisms COM-010 and COM-090 describe — a shopkeeper
who cheats a regular customer loses that customer's future business
directly and immediately, which disciplines everyday retail conduct
without requiring a market judge, a merchant court, or a fair's scheduled
gathering at all. This lets a fixed location sustain trade at far lower
individual-transaction value than a periodic fair requires to be worth
attending, since the shop's profitability comes from transaction
frequency over a long relationship rather than volume at a single event.

## Social consequence

A permanent shop anchors its owner's economic and social identity to one
fixed location within a neighborhood in a way itinerant peddling or
periodic-fair trading doesn't, converting retail from an activity into a
recognized social position (the shopkeeper) with a stake in the
neighborhood's ongoing stability and reputation.

## Regional specifics

Song dynasty Kaifeng's deliberate abandonment of the older walled-ward
market system shows fixed retail's spatial and temporal organization is
a real urban-policy choice, not a fixed technological stage — converting
retail from confined and scheduled to continuous and dispersed was a
decision with its own documented resistance and consequence, not a
passive drift. West Africa's comparative reliance on periodic markets and
caravan trade (COM-050) over fixed urban shops reflects lower sustained
urban population density outside major trade termini, showing permanent
retail infrastructure requires a population density threshold periodic
or itinerant trade doesn't.

## Hook

A fixed shop substitutes ongoing local reputation for the one-time or
periodic trust mechanisms this domain's other market institutions
require, letting retail sustain far lower per-transaction value than a
fair or caravan needs to be worth the trip — a modifier on local market
depth and reach rather than a gate, since a shop's viability scales
smoothly with neighborhood population and repeat-visit frequency instead
of requiring a threshold of pooled participation.
