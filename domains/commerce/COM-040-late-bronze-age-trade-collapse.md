A highly specialized, tightly interlocking long-distance exchange network
— bronze production depends on tin from a small number of distant
sources, funneled through a handful of palace-controlled redistribution
economies — collapses across the eastern Mediterranean within a few
decades, taking most of its constituent trade nodes down with it rather
than degrading gradually.

## Timing

The collapse concentrates within a narrow window around -1200 to -1150:
Ugarit's final, unanswered plea for help and subsequent destruction,
the last Linear B administrative records at the Mycenaean palaces, and
the broader disruption Egyptian sources attribute to the "Sea Peoples"
all cluster within a generation or two of each other.

## Mechanism

A network built around a small number of essential, geographically
concentrated inputs (tin above all, for bronze) and a small number of
palace-scale redistribution hubs achieves high peak throughput and value
precisely because it's so tightly optimized — but that same
specialization removes the redundancy a more diversified network would
have to absorb a shock. A small number of broken links (a tin source cut
off, a couple of palace centers destroyed by raiding or internal revolt)
doesn't degrade the network's throughput proportionally — it can trigger
cascading failure across the whole interlocked system within a single
generation, since so many nodes depended on the same few chokepoints
functioning at once.

## Social consequence

Where the network's collapse is total enough, the specialized
administrative and craft knowledge built up to sustain it — palace
scribal bureaucracies, bronze-specific metalworking expertise, the
long-distance shipping knowledge connecting Cyprus, the Levant, and the
Aegean — disappears along with the network itself rather than persisting
in a simpler form, producing the archaeologically visible "dark age"
that follows across multiple regions simultaneously.

## Regional specifics

Ugarit's final archive is an unusually direct, contemporaneous
documentary snapshot of a trade node's collapse in the act of happening,
rather than a reconstruction from later absence. The simultaneous end of
Mycenaean Linear B record-keeping shows the administrative technology
tracking the network and the network itself were tightly enough coupled
to fail together. Egypt's survival as a state while still losing most of
its long-distance trade connections shows political and commercial
collapse are separable — a polity's own institutional durability doesn't
guarantee the trade network it depended on survives with it.

## Contested

Whether the collapse is best explained by systems-collapse dynamics
inherent to over-specialization, climate-driven drought and migration
pressure, the Sea Peoples' raiding directly, internal revolt against
palace elites, or some combination remains genuinely unresolved among
Bronze Age archaeologists. Model the collapse as several independently-
triggerable stressors whose combination, not any single one, produces
cascade failure — echoing the multi-cause structure COM-140 already uses
for the Price Revolution.

## Hook

Network specialization is a double-edged modifier, not a pure bonus: it
raises peak throughput and `network fragility` together, so the same
tight interdependence that makes a trade network like this one
exceptionally valuable at peak also makes it exceptionally vulnerable to
a shock of a given size — a small number of broken links cascading into
total collapse rather than proportional degradation, with recovery
measured in generations, not seasons.
