A state grants a private, joint-stock company a legal monopoly over trade
with a specific region, along with delegated sovereign powers (making
treaties, waging war, administering territory) that go far beyond what
any ordinary merchant venture — or even a Hanseatic-style merchant league
(COM-015) — could exercise on its own.

## Timing

The Dutch and English East India Companies (chartered 1602 and 1600
respectively) are the paradigm cases; comparable chartered companies operate
across Africa and the Americas through the following two and a half
centuries, with most such companies' special sovereign privileges wound down
by the mid-to-late 1800s as territorial states absorb their functions
directly.

## Mechanism

Chartering solves two problems simultaneously for the sponsoring state: it
mobilizes large-scale private joint-stock capital (a FIN-adjacent innovation)
for expensive, risky long-distance ventures the state treasury doesn't want
to fund directly, while delegating the political and military risk of
operating in a distant, contested region to a company structure whose
losses don't directly fall on the state itself — the company in turn is
granted state-like powers (military force, treaty-making, taxation)
precisely because operating profitably at that distance without them isn't
viable.

## Social consequence

A chartered company blurs the line between commercial and political actor
so thoroughly that its shareholders back in the home country become
financially interested parties in what amounts to conquest, taxation, and
governance of a foreign population — profit and sovereignty become the same
balance sheet, with shareholder dividend expectations directly shaping how
aggressively a company's territorial administration extracts revenue from
the population it governs.

## Regional specifics

The East India Company's transformation from trading concern to territorial
sovereign after Plassey is the starkest instance of this entry's core
mechanic reaching its logical extreme — a company literally ruling as a
state while remaining accountable to shareholders. The Ottoman capitulation
system's alternative approach — negotiating foreign trade access directly
state-to-state rather than delegating it to a chartered intermediary — solves
the same underlying access problem without ever creating a quasi-sovereign
commercial actor in between, showing chartering was a choice available
sponsoring states could and did decline to make.

## Hook

Chartering converts a private joint-stock company into a quasi-sovereign
actor — capable of war, treaty, and taxation — precisely where operating
profitably at long range without those powers isn't viable, aligning
shareholder profit motive directly with what amounts to conquest and
governance, with no reliable internal check on how far that alignment gets
pushed.
