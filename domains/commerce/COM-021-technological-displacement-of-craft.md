When mechanized production (feeding into COM-023's factory model) can
match or exceed a craft good's quality at a fraction of the labor cost, the
skilled artisans whose livelihood depended on that craft face displacement
on a timeline far faster than they can retrain or than their guild
institutions can absorb.

## Timing

British hand-loom weavers' organized resistance (the Luddite movement)
peaks from 1811 to roughly 1816; the much larger-scale displacement of
Bengali hand-loom weavers by machine-made British textile imports unfolds
over the following decades through the mid-1800s.

## Mechanism

Mechanized production breaks the direct link between skilled labor time and
output volume that craft production depends on for its livelihood — once a
machine-tended process can produce comparable-quality goods at a small
fraction of the labor-hours, craft prices can't compete without either a
protective tariff (COM-018) or a quality/status premium the market is
willing to sustain, and where neither exists, displacement follows on
whatever timeline the mechanized competitor can scale production and
distribution.

## Social consequence

Displaced artisans lose not just income but the social status, guild
membership, and skilled-worker identity that craft production conferred,
producing organized resistance (Luddite machine-breaking) where displacement
is fast and geographically concentrated enough to mobilize collectively — a
resistance that, historically, slows but doesn't reverse the underlying
economic substitution, since the mechanized competitor's cost advantage
persists regardless of local resistance.

## Regional specifics

Bengali hand-loom weaver displacement operates at a much larger scale than
the British Luddite case and is driven as much by colonial tariff and trade
policy actively favoring British machine-made imports (COM-018's mechanism
turned against Indian producers rather than protecting them) as by the
technology gap itself — a case where policy and mechanization compound
rather than either alone explaining the outcome. Ottoman craft guilds facing
slower, later mechanized competition than Indian textiles shows the pace of
displacement depends heavily on the pace and directness of market
integration with industrializing production centers, not on the technology
gap being any less real elsewhere.

## Contested

The classic "deindustrialization" thesis reads Bengal's hand-loom decline as
a direct, primarily externally-imposed consequence of British machine
imports and discriminatory colonial tariff policy (COM-018 turned against
rather than for the colonized producer). Revisionist economic history
challenges this with population growth, shifting domestic demand patterns,
and acknowledged weaknesses in the surviving 19th-century output data as
alternative or contributing explanations, without denying that large-scale
livelihood loss among hand-loom weavers did occur. Model the livelihood-loss
outcome itself as solid, while treating the causal split between external
trade-policy shock and internal structural change as a variable, region- and
period-specific ratio rather than defaulting to the classic thesis as
settled fact.

## Hook

Mechanized production breaks craft labor's link between skill-hours and
output, displacing artisan livelihoods on a timeline set by how fast the
mechanized competitor can scale and distribute — a timeline that trade and
tariff policy (COM-018) can either slow (protecting domestic craft) or sharply
accelerate (favoring foreign machine-made imports), independent of the
underlying `time discipline` shift already underway wherever mechanized
production takes hold.
