Rationalist and empiricist argument (PHI-018, PHI-019) doesn't stay confined
to university disputation or private correspondence in this era — it
circulates through a new, semi-public social venue: the salon, the
coffeehouse, and the periodical, none of which argue anything new
themselves, but all of which change who gets to participate in the
conversation and how fast it moves.

## Timing

The London coffeehouse and the Parisian salon both become significant sites
of intellectual sociability from the mid-17th century, with the format
maturing and multiplying across European cities through the following
century and a half, closing roughly at 1800 as newer, more explicitly
political and revolutionary forms of public assembly begin to supersede it.

## Mechanism

A salon or coffeehouse lowers the entry cost for participating in
philosophical and political conversation relative to a university lecture
hall or a private scholarly correspondence — admission requires social
connection or (for the coffeehouse) simply the price of a drink, not
enrolled academic standing, letting merchants, women excluded from
university education (salons were frequently hosted and shaped by women),
and interested amateurs participate in circulating and testing arguments
that originate in more formal settings.

## Social consequence

Because these venues mix people who wouldn't otherwise share an intellectual
conversation — aristocrat and merchant, university-trained and
self-taught, native and visiting foreigner — they function as an informal
filter and amplifier: an argument that plays well across a socially mixed
salon or coffeehouse audience spreads faster and reaches further than one
that only circulates within a single university's walls, without the venue
itself contributing any new argument of its own.

## Regional specifics

The Parisian salon tradition, substantially hosted and curated by women
(Madame Geoffrin and Madame du Deffand prominent among many), gives women
structurally excluded from formal university philosophical training real
practical influence over which arguments and which thinkers gained wider
circulation and patronage — an influence exercised through curation and
conversation rather than through published philosophical authorship itself.

## Hook

A modifier, mostly connective tissue: speeds and widens circulation of
argument already produced elsewhere (PHI-018, PHI-019) without generating
new argument of its own — a venue-level effect on how fast and how broadly
an idea spreads, not a lever any other entry's Mechanism depends on.
