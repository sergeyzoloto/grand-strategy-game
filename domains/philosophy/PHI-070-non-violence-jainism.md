Jainism's central and most demanding claim is that ahimsa (non-violence)
is not merely a virtue among others but the supreme ethical principle,
grounded in a specific metaphysical argument: karma is literal, subtle
particulate matter that attaches to the soul (jiva) through harmful
action and weighs it down, making even unintentional or minor harm to any
living being a real metaphysical cost, not just a moral failing.

## Timing

Mahavira, Jainism's twenty-fourth tirthankara ("ford-maker") in the
tradition's own reckoning, is traditionally dated to roughly -599 to -527,
teaching within the same broader shramana ferment that produces Buddhism
(PHI-090) in the same region and era.

## Mechanism

Because karma is understood as literal matter accumulating on the soul
through harmful action rather than as a metaphorical debt or purely
symbolic stain, ahimsa becomes a matter of rigorous practical
consequence, not sentiment — extending systematically outward from human
beings to animals and even, in the most demanding interpretations,
single-sensed organisms (plants, microorganisms), which is what motivates
Jain ascetic practices (careful movement, filtered water, dietary
restriction) that read as extreme only if the underlying metaphysical
stakes are missed.

## Social consequence

Jain doctrine also develops anekantavada (many-sidedness) and syadvada
(conditional predication) — a systematic epistemological humility holding
that any single perspective on a complex reality is necessarily partial,
famously illustrated by the parable of blind men each correctly but
incompletely describing different parts of an elephant — which functions
as a distinctive method for handling philosophical disagreement itself,
treating rival schools' conflicting claims as partial truths to be
integrated rather than errors to be refuted outright.

## Regional specifics

Jainism emerges alongside Buddhism (PHI-090) as a shared reaction against
the Vedic sacrificial tradition's acceptance of ritual animal sacrifice as
religiously unproblematic — both shramana movements reframe causing harm
as a metaphysically consequential act binding the soul, a genuine
doctrinal break from, not a continuous elaboration of, the broader
tradition both emerge within alongside the Upanishads (PHI-040).

## Contested

Jain tradition places Mahavira within a lineage of twenty-four
tirthankaras reaching into the mythical past, with the immediately
preceding tirthankara, Parshvanatha, sometimes argued to have
independent historical grounding beyond scripture — but Parshvanatha's own
historicity and exact relationship to Mahavira's teaching remains
genuinely uncertain given the available evidence. Model Jain doctrine's
core claims as reliably datable to Mahavira's era while treating the
deeper lineage claims as tradition rather than established history.

## Hook

Karma-as-literal-particulate-matter converts non-violence from a
sentiment into a rigorous, extendable practical calculus — a mechanic
where harm to any living being carries a real, trackable metaphysical
cost proportional to how broadly "living being" is drawn, with
anekantavada's partial-perspectives epistemology offering a distinct model
for resolving rather than merely litigating doctrinal disagreement.
