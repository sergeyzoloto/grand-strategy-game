Existentialism centers individual lived existence, radical freedom, and
anxious choice as philosophy's proper starting point; structuralism
instead argues individual subjects are substantially effects of
impersonal underlying structures (language, kinship systems, discursive
formations) they don't consciously author or control — a direct,
publicly-conducted rivalry (Sartre and Lévi-Strauss disputed each other's
frameworks explicitly and by name) over whether the free individual or
impersonal structure is the proper primary unit of philosophical analysis.

## Timing

Heidegger's Being and Time (1927) and Sartre's Being and Nothingness
(1943) anchor existentialism's classic period; structuralism develops
from Saussure's earlier linguistics through Lévi-Strauss's anthropological
application in the 1950s-60s, reaching a peak influence around Foucault's
The Order of Things (1966).

## Mechanism

Sartre's existentialism argues "existence precedes essence" — a person
has no fixed pre-given nature determining who they are, only the
accumulating sum of their freely chosen actions, with "bad faith" naming
the still-fully-conscious self-deception of pretending otherwise (a
direct rejection of Freudian unconscious determinism, PHI-023, that
preserves radical freedom and responsibility by other means).
Structuralism instead models meaning and social order as arising from
differential relations within a closed system (Saussure's insight that a
linguistic sign means what it means only by its position relative to
other signs, not by direct reference to things) — Lévi-Strauss extends
this to kinship and myth, arguing individual actors are as much products
and carriers of an underlying structural system as they are its free
authors.

## Social consequence

Where existentialism places full responsibility for a person's identity
and choices on that person's own freely-exercised will, structuralism
relocates a great deal of what determines a person's thought and
behavior to impersonal systems operating prior to and independent of any
individual's conscious choice — a genuinely different account of how much
credit or blame an individual actually deserves for who they've become,
with real stakes for how each framework treats moral and political
responsibility.

## Regional specifics

Fanon's Black Skin, White Masks transforms existentialist categories
(bad faith, being-for-others) to analyze colonized subjects' experience
of being reduced to an object under the colonizer's racializing gaze — a
substantive philosophical contribution developed from within the African
and Caribbean colonial experience, not merely a European framework
applied secondhand to a new case, and one that anticipates
structuralism's and post-structuralism's own concern with how impersonal
systems (here, colonial racial discourse) constitute subjects who
experience themselves as autonomous.

## Contested

Whether structuralism's "death of the subject" is a genuine advance over
existentialism's individual-freedom starting point, or a totalizing
overreach erasing real agency and responsibility, is a foundational,
still-live dispute in philosophy and social theory. Model individual
agency and impersonal structure as competing, both-partially-valid
explanatory registers whose relative weight is the contested variable,
rather than assuming either fully displaces the other.

## Hook

Existentialism and structuralism supply philosophy's two rival post-
Freudian (PHI-023) answers to how much of a person is self-authored versus
structurally determined — bad faith preserving full conscious
responsibility on one side, impersonal system-membership diluting it on
the other — with Fanon's colonial application showing the same axis
extends directly into how racializing social structures constitute the
very subjects who experience themselves as freely choosing.
