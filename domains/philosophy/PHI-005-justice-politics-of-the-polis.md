Plato and Aristotle both treat the polis (city-state) as the necessary
setting for human flourishing and argue systematically about what justice
actually is — not merely what specific laws a city should have, but what
structural arrangement of a city (or a soul) counts as just in the first
place.

## Timing

Plato's Republic dates to roughly -380; Aristotle, Plato's student, writes
his Politics and Nicomachean Ethics in the following generation, through
his death in -322 — both working within and theorizing about the same
Greek polis system that Alexander's conquests would soon render politically
obsolete (PHI-010).

## Mechanism

Plato argues justice in a city mirrors justice in a soul — each functions
well when its parts (rulers/reason, guardians/spirit, producers/appetite)
perform their proper distinct role without usurping another's, with
philosopher-kings uniquely qualified to rule because they alone grasp the
Form of the Good directly. Aristotle instead argues the polis is the
natural culmination of human beings' inherently social/political nature
(zoon politikon) — humans can only fully flourish (eudaimonia) as
participating citizens of a polis, making political community not a
convenient arrangement but the setting flourishing itself requires.

## Social consequence

Both theories make full human flourishing conditional on political
citizenship specifically — which is exactly what makes the question of who
counts as a citizen (LAW-002) so consequential in this framework: someone
excluded from citizenship isn't merely denied a convenience but is held,
within the theory's own terms, incapable of the fullest form of human
flourishing altogether.

## Regional specifics

Kautilya's Arthashastra, composed in a comparable era, builds political
theory from the opposite direction — starting from the coercive
precondition (danda) that makes any cooperative order possible at all,
judging a ruler's legitimacy by effective statecraft rather than by
philosophical insight into justice or the good. The contrast shows Axial-
Age-era political philosophy converging on the state's central
importance while reaching very different conclusions about what actually
grounds its legitimacy — civic/psychic harmony in one tradition,
effective coercive capacity in the other.

## Contested

Whether Aristotle's naturalization of slavery and gendered hierarchy is
separable from his core theory or is actually required by it — since the
polis's citizen leisure for political life depends on an excluded laboring
substrate — is genuinely disputed among Aristotle scholars. Model the
polis's flourishing-through-citizenship mechanic as resting on a real,
sizeable excluded population whose extent and treatment is itself a design
parameter, not an incidental historical detail separable from the theory.

## Hook

Both theories make political citizenship (LAW-002) a precondition for the
fullest human flourishing, not merely a convenience — meaning a polity's
citizenship boundary directly gates who the state's own political theory
recognizes as capable of a fully realized life, with the excluded
population's size a real, trackable design parameter rather than
background noise.
