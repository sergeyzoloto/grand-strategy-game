Mahāyāna, the "great vehicle", is the largest development in Buddhist thought and the
form of Buddhism that spread across Central and East Asia. It made two moves that are
separable and should be kept so. One is philosophical: Nāgārjuna's argument that all
phenomena, including the elements earlier Buddhist analysis had treated as ultimate,
are empty of independent existence. The other is soteriological: the goal shifts from
one's own liberation to the vow to liberate all beings. The first changed what Buddhist
philosophy could claim; the second changed who Buddhism could be for.

## Timing

The Perfection of Wisdom sutras appear from about the first century BCE, and the Lotus
and Vimalakīrti sutras in the first two centuries CE. Nāgārjuna writes in the second or
third century, Asaṅga and Vasubandhu systematise Yogācāra in the fourth and fifth, and
Nālandā becomes the tradition's great teaching centre from the fifth. Transmission runs
alongside: through the Kushan empire from the second century, into China from
Lokakṣema's translations of 179 and Kumārajīva's bureau from 401, into Korea from 372
and Japan from the sixth century. The date end marks the point at which Mahāyāna was
established across East Asia; Xuanzang's return in 645 and the Samye debate of about
794 are carried as its continuations.

## Mechanism

**The first move: śūnyatā.** The Buddha taught that there is no fixed self (PHI-090).
The Abhidharma scholastics who systematised that teaching did so by analysing the
person and the world into momentary elements, dharmas, each with its own intrinsic
nature (svabhāva). The self was empty; the elements were real. Nāgārjuna turned the
analysis on itself. Everything arises in dependence on causes and conditions
(pratītyasamutpāda). Anything that arises in dependence cannot have an intrinsic nature
of its own, because an intrinsic nature would not depend on anything. So the dharmas
are empty too — and so is emptiness, which is not a hidden substance behind things but
the absence of any. His method is reductio: take any position an opponent holds about
causation, motion, time or the self, and show that it contradicts itself, without
putting forward a position of one's own. The conclusion is that no view about how
things ultimately are can be held without clinging. Nāgārjuna distinguished two truths —
conventional truth, in which people, tables and causes work as everyone says they do,
and ultimate truth, in which none of them has intrinsic existence — and argued that
even nirvāṇa and saṃsāra are not two different realms.

**A tradition turning its method on its own foundations.** This is a move of the same
order as the foundational crisis in mathematics (MTH-070): a tradition applies its own
analytical method to the elements that method had treated as bedrock and finds no
bottom. The difference lies in what follows. MTH-070's crisis was productive because it
forced mathematics to rebuild explicit foundations. Nāgārjuna's argument treats the
absence of foundations as the soteriological point: the craving for a final ground is the
clinging that binds. The same discovery can end in reconstruction or in the claim that
reconstruction is the mistake, and the entry should keep both outcomes available.

**The second school: Yogācāra.** Madhyamaka was not the only Mahāyāna philosophy.
Asaṅga and Vasubandhu argued that what is given in experience is consciousness
alone (vijñaptimātra). The apparent external world arises from karmic seeds stored in a
storehouse consciousness (ālayavijñāna), and liberation is a transformation of that
consciousness rather than an insight into the emptiness of everything. Yogācāra supplied
what Madhyamaka refused to: a positive account of how delusion works and how practice
changes it, which made it the framework for Buddhist psychology and meditation theory.

**The Madhyamaka–Yogācāra debate.** Each side accused the other of the error its own
position was built to avoid. Yogācāra thinkers charged Madhyamaka with nihilism — if
everything is empty, nothing can be practised or attained. Mādhyamikas charged Yogācāra
with reifying mind, giving consciousness the intrinsic existence Nāgārjuna had denied to
everything. At Nālandā in the sixth century Bhāviveka and Dharmapāla argued the question,
and Candrakīrti in the seventh restated Madhyamaka against both. Śāntarakṣita in the
eighth century synthesised the two — Yogācāra for conventional truth, Madhyamaka for
ultimate — and carried the synthesis to Tibet. The dispute kept Mahāyāna philosophy
scholastically alive for centuries, and its polemics later reached Advaita Vedānta,
whose opponents accused Śaṅkara of crypto-Buddhism (PHI-190).

**The second move: the bodhisattva.** In the older ideal the goal was to become an
arhat, one who has extinguished craving and will not be reborn — a path for renunciant
monks. Mahāyāna sutras declared that goal incomplete. The bodhisattva vows to attain
full buddhahood for the sake of all beings and defers final release until they are
liberated, practising the six perfections (generosity, ethics, patience, effort,
meditation and wisdom) over many lives. Three developments followed from the vow:

- **Skilful means (upāya).** The Lotus Sutra teaches that the Buddha adapted his teaching
  to his audience and that all paths lead to one vehicle, so simpler practices are valid
  stages, not lesser religions.
- **Celestial bodhisattvas and buddhas.** Avalokiteśvara, who hears the cries of the
  world, and Amitābha, whose Pure Land receives those who call on his name, could be
  prayed to. Salvation could come as help from outside, not only from one's own practice.
- **The lay bodhisattva.** In the Vimalakīrti Sutra a married householder out-argues the
  Buddha's greatest monastic disciples. A layperson could be further along the path than a
  monk.

**The institutional consequence: from a renunciant path to a universal one.** A
tradition whose highest goal is reached only by monks can command lay patronage but
cannot offer lay salvation. Once devotion to a bodhisattva, recitation of a buddha's
name, or merit transferred from a bodhisattva can bring a householder to liberation, the
tradition can become a mass religion. This entry names that as `salvific access`: how
much of the population can reach a tradition's highest goal without renouncing
household life. REL-050 owns the parallel mechanism for membership — universalism
removes the ceiling of birth into a people by making conversion the criterion. Mahāyāna
removes a different ceiling within a religion already universal in membership: the
ceiling of renunciation on who can reach the goal. The two are distinct and should not be
collapsed. A universalist religion can still reserve its goal for monks, as Theravāda
did; Mahāyāna shows the second ceiling coming down after the first. The monastic
institution (REL-060) did not disappear, and in East Asia it grew — but it now served a
laity that could itself be saved.

**Transmission: the Silk Road and the translators.** Mahāyāna spread with trade. Kushan
patronage from the second century put Buddhism across the routes between India, Iran and
China, the oasis kingdoms of Khotan and Kucha became Mahāyāna centres, and monks and
texts moved with the caravans along COM-080's logistics system. Missionary monks (REL-080)
were often Central Asians. The Chinese translation project — Lokakṣema, then Kumārajīva
and his bureau, then Xuanzang — is carried in MED-070 as the case where translation
strengthened the institution that sponsored it, and it is not re-derived here. The
philosophical consequence belongs here: early Chinese readers took emptiness for the
Daoist non-being of PHI-060, and Kumārajīva's terminology separated the two, after which
Chinese thinkers could build Madhyamaka and Yogācāra into schools of their own.

**Divergence into distinct traditions.** Once transplanted, Mahāyāna did not stay one
thing:

- **China** produced Tiantai's synthesis of all teachings by level, Huayan's
  interpenetration of all phenomena, Pure Land devotion, and Chan's rejection of
  scriptural study for direct insight (PHI-180), which drew on emptiness as its theory.
- **Tibet** chose the Indian analytical path at Samye and built a scholastic Madhyamaka
  that preserved Indian texts lost elsewhere.
- **Korea and Japan** received Chinese schools through courts and made their own — Seon
  and Hwaeom, Tendai, Shingon, Pure Land and Zen.

Chan's claim that awakening is sudden and non-conceptual is recognisably the practical
form of Nāgārjuna's argument that no concept reaches ultimate truth. It had six centuries
of Mahāyāna behind it.

## Social consequence

At human scale Mahāyāna changed what an ordinary person could hope for and do:

- **A Chinese farmer of the Tang.** They recite Amitābha's name, trusting to be reborn in
  the Pure Land, without learning to read the sutras or leaving the family.
- **A Japanese court lady of the Heian period.** They copy the Lotus Sutra as an act of
  merit.
- **A Kuchean or Sogdian merchant.** They fund a cave shrine at Dunhuang on the route home,
  with themselves painted as donors at the base of a bodhisattva.
- **A Nālandā student.** They spend years learning to argue Madhyamaka against Yogācāra
  under formal debate rules.
- **A Tibetan monk after Samye.** They train in analytical debate because the kingdom
  decided awakening comes gradually.

Mahāyāna made the laity religious agents with a path of their own, and it made patronage
of monasteries, images and copying a way of making merit that carried whole economies of
donation. It also changed gender and status in text if not always in practice: the lay
bodhisattva and the universal vow put householders and, in some sutras, women within
reach of the goal, while monastic institutions kept their hierarchy.

## Regional specifics

This is the corpus's largest body of non-European philosophy, and each region added its
own form:

- **South Asia** produced the philosophy — Nāgārjuna in the Deccan, Asaṅga and
  Vasubandhu in Gandhara, and Nālandā as the university where Madhyamaka and Yogācāra were
  argued out.
- **Central Asia** carried it. The Kushan empire, Khotan, Kucha and Dunhuang made the Silk
  Road a Buddhist route, and many of its translators were Central Asian.
- **China** translated it and then transformed it into schools of its own, Chan among
  them.
- **Korea and Japan** received it through courts as a religion of state and culture.
- **Tibet** received Indian Madhyamaka directly and, after Samye, built its scholasticism
  on it.

The absence is Sri Lanka, where the Mahāvihāra's Pali canon and arhat ideal prevailed over
Mahāyāna at the Abhayagiri. Theravāda Sri Lanka, Burma and Thailand kept the older
renunciant goal and a laity whose religious role was supporting monks.

## Contested

Two disputes bear on the entry:

- **Origin.** Hirakawa read Mahāyāna as a lay movement around stupa worship; Schopen,
  Harrison and Nattier read its earliest sutras as the work of ascetic monks for whom the
  bodhisattva path was harder, with mass lay religion arriving later.
- **Emptiness.** It has been read as a metaphysical thesis, as a therapy that asserts
  nothing, and by its critics as nihilism.

Model the expansion of `salvific access` as conditional on institutional carriers —
devotional practice, lay patronage, state sponsorship — and Madhyamaka and Yogācāra as
rival readings a tradition can hold in tension or synthesise.

## Hook

**Two separable moves.**

- **Emptiness.** Applying a tradition's analysis to its own foundations can end in
  reconstruction (MTH-070) or in the claim that the search for foundations is the error.
  Madhyamaka is the second branch, and it raises the tradition's `intellectual authority`
  in debate while removing any doctrinal bedrock to defend.
- **The bodhisattva.** Replacing individual liberation with a vow to save all beings
  raises `salvific access` — the share of a population that can reach the tradition's goal
  without renunciation — once devotional practice and lay patronage carry it.

**Distinct from universalism.** REL-050 lifts the ceiling of birth on membership; the
bodhisattva lifts the ceiling of renunciation on the goal. A tradition can do the first
without the second.

**Transmission and divergence.** The tradition travels with trade along the Silk Road and
through institution-sponsored translation, then splits into regional schools — Chan,
Pure Land, Tibetan scholasticism — whose differences follow what each receiving society
chose, as at Samye.

**Rival readings.** Madhyamaka and Yogācāra should be able to coexist, contend and
synthesise, with a tradition's choice among them shaping its practice.
