This entry owns Legalism as systematic political theory — Han Feizi's
synthesis of fa (impersonal codified law), shu (the ruler's technique for
controlling officials), and shi (positional power) into an explicitly
amoral theory of statecraft; the specific institutional codification this
theory produces under the Qin is LAW-001's territory.

## Timing

Legalist statecraft develops from Shang Yang's reforms in Qin from around
-400, reaching its fullest theoretical synthesis in Han Feizi's writings
shortly before the Qin unification of China in -221, and remains the
Qin state's operative ideology through its collapse in -206.

## Mechanism

Legalism argues rulership should rest entirely on impersonal, uniformly
and predictably enforced law plus calculated technique for controlling
officials, rather than on the ruler's or officials' personal virtue —
explicitly rejecting Confucian ritual cultivation (PHI-004) as an
unreliable, hard-to-verify substitute for mechanical, universally-applied
reward and punishment, which Legalist theorists argue produces
predictable compliance regardless of any individual official's actual
character.

## Social consequence

Because Legalism evaluates people entirely by observable compliance and
productive output rather than by hereditary status or claimed virtue, it
directly and deliberately dismantles aristocratic privilege — a peasant
farmer's or soldier's family could rise through Qin's rank system based on
agricultural output or military merit in a way birth-based systems
elsewhere in this record don't permit, at the direct cost of eliminating
any legal protection for traditional elite prerogative.

## Regional specifics

No Mediterranean political theory in this era argues as explicitly for
law's value stripped of any grounding in justice or virtue — Greek
political philosophy (PHI-005) treats law as properly answerable to a
substantive theory of the good, making Legalism's frank instrumentalism a
genuinely distinct position within this record's political philosophy,
not a variant available within the Greek tradition too.

## Contested

Whether Legalism is inherently self-destructive as a sole governing
philosophy — the Qin state collapsed within roughly fifteen years of
unification — or whether that specific collapse reflects a succession
crisis and administrative overreach rather than a flaw in the theory
itself is disputed among historians, especially since subsequent
dynasties retained substantial Legalist administrative technique beneath
officially Confucian rhetoric. Model Legalist technique and Legalism as
exclusive ruling ideology as separable variables: the technique persists
and gets absorbed elsewhere, while the specific total-Legalism collapse
case may not generalize.

## Hook

Legalism raises a state's `legibility` over its own population by
replacing hard-to-verify claimed virtue with mechanically-enforced,
uniform reward and punishment — a technique that survives and gets
absorbed into later, officially non-Legalist administrations even where
Legalism as an explicit governing ideology collapses.
