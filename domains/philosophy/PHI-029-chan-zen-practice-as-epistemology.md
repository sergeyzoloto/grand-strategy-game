Chan (and its Japanese form, Zen) makes a distinctive epistemological
claim: enlightenment is reached through direct, unmediated practice and
experience — meditation (zazen) and provocative pedagogical devices like
koans (paradoxical riddles designed to exhaust and break through ordinary
discursive reasoning) — rather than through doctrinal study or scriptural
argument, captured in the tradition's own self-description as "a special
transmission outside the scriptures, not founded on words and letters."

## Timing

Chan traditionally traces its founding to Bodhidharma's arrival in China
around the 5th-6th century; the tradition's classic period elaborates
through the following centuries in China before Dogen's transmission
establishes Soto Zen in Japan, formalized by his death in 1253.

## Mechanism

Where Indian Buddhist scholastic traditions (building on PHI-012's
doctrine) pursue enlightenment substantially through sustained
philosophical analysis and dialectical argument (Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka
method among others), Chan argues conceptual, discursive reasoning is
itself an obstacle past a certain point — a koan's deliberate
unsolvability by ordinary logical analysis is the method's actual
mechanism, designed to exhaust the discursive mind until direct,
non-conceptual insight (satori/kensho) breaks through where step-by-step
argument could not reach.

## Social consequence

Because Chan/Zen locates authority in a teacher's direct verification of
a student's experiential breakthrough rather than in mastery of textual
argument, its master-disciple relationship and lineage-transmission
structure (comparable to PHI-001's general schools-of-thought mechanism)
carries a different kind of institutional authority than a scholastic
teaching lineage's — one grounded in personally certified experiential
attainment rather than in demonstrated command of doctrine.

## Regional specifics

Dogen's transmission of the tradition to Japan is a genuine doctrinal
refinement, not merely relocation — his shikantaza ("just sitting")
teaches that meditation practice already is enlightenment's expression in
the moment of sitting, rather than a means toward some separate future
attainment, a distinctively Japanese Soto development within the shared
Chan/Zen practice-as-epistemology framework.

## Contested

Chan's traditional unbroken-lineage narrative back through Bodhidharma to
the Buddha is substantially a retrospective construction — Dunhuang
manuscript discoveries reveal early Chan history was considerably more
contested and multi-branched than the later standardized account admits,
with Bodhidharma's own historicity genuinely uncertain. Model Chan's
foundational lineage claim as a legitimating narrative constructed well
after the fact, comparable to this project's other invented-tradition
cases (PHI-025), rather than as reliable history of the tradition's
actual early transmission.

## Hook

Koan practice deliberately exhausts discursive reasoning to force
non-conceptual insight where step-by-step argument can't reach — a
distinct `habitus formation` regimen (alongside Confucian li, PHI-004,
and Stoic askesis, PHI-010) whose authority runs through a teacher's
direct certification of a student's experiential breakthrough rather than
through demonstrated mastery of doctrinal argument.
