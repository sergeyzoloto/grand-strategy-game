Kierkegaard argues the individual's subjective, passionately-committed
relation to existence — not any objective system, Hegelian or otherwise —
is where truth that actually matters to a life gets decided, making him
existentialism's actual 19th-century founder, a full generation before this
domain's own existentialism entry (PHI-028) otherwise begins.

## Timing

Kierkegaard's authorship runs from his dissertation (1841) through his major
pseudonymous works of the 1840s (Fear and Trembling, 1843, prominent among
them) to his death in 1855.

## Mechanism

Against Hegel's claim that a comprehensive rational system can capture truth
about existence from an impersonal, totalizing standpoint, Kierkegaard
argues the most important truths — how to live, what to commit to, whether
to believe — can't be reached by detached systematic reasoning at all, only
by a passionate, subjective "leap" the individual makes without complete
rational justification: Abraham's binding of Isaac, Kierkegaard's paradigm
case, is neither rationally justifiable nor refutable by any general ethical
system, and that's precisely the point — genuine faith requires exactly the
kind of individual commitment no impersonal system can make on a person's
behalf.

## Social consequence

Because Kierkegaard locates the individual's subjective commitment, not
membership in any institution or system, as where the decision that actually
matters gets made, his account sits uneasily with organized Christendom's own
routine, inherited, low-stakes churchgoing — a large part of his later
polemical writing directly attacks the Danish state church for converting
what should be an anxious, individually-decided leap into comfortable social
convention.

## Regional specifics

Kierkegaard writes from within, and largely against, the Danish Golden Age's
own Hegelian intellectual establishment, giving his individualist critique a
specifically local institutional target even as its influence spreads well
beyond Denmark in the following century.
