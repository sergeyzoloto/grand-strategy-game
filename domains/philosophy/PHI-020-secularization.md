The claim — and its serious empirical challengers — that modernization
drives an institutional differentiation of religious and political
authority, converting political legitimacy from something resting on
religious sanction (REL-013's confessional state, DIP-006's
pre-Westphalian religious-political fusion) into something resting on
autonomous secular grounds (PHI-019's consent-based legitimacy, PHI-013's
natural law).

## Timing

Locke's 1689 "A Letter Concerning Toleration" is an early and influential
argument for separating religious conscience from state coercion; the
thesis's clearest institutional endpoint in this era is France's 1905 law
formally separating church and state (laïcité), though the broader
differentiation process the thesis describes runs continuously and
unevenly across the intervening two centuries.

## Mechanism

Max Weber's influential formulation ("disenchantment") argues rationalized
modern institutions — bureaucratic administration, positive law, natural-
scientific explanation — progressively displace religious authority and
explanation from domains (law, governance, natural knowledge) it
previously occupied by default, not because religious belief itself
necessarily weakens, but because the specific institutions doing law,
governance, and knowledge-production stop requiring religious
justification to function or be considered legitimate.

## Social consequence

Where this differentiation proceeds furthest, an individual's religious
identity becomes separable, at least formally, from full legal and
political standing — converting religious affiliation from a
precondition of full citizenship (as under REL-013's confessional state)
into one private matter among others a citizen holds independent of their
civic status, a genuinely novel arrangement historically, whatever its
lived unevenness in practice.

## Regional specifics

Independent India's constitutional secularism explicitly rejects strict
separation in favor of "principled distance" — formal even-handedness
across religions combined with a retained state right to intervene in
religious practice for reform purposes — a conceptually distinct model
from the European differentiation thesis. Ottoman and Turkish reform
follows a substantially different logic again: state-driven, relatively
abrupt modernization restructuring existing religious-legal institutions
rather than the slow spontaneous differentiation Weber's account
describes, showing "secularization" names a family of genuinely different
institutional processes rather than one universal template gradually
adopted everywhere it occurs at all.

## Contested

The secularization thesis is one of the most heavily contested claims in
the sociology of religion — Peter Berger, an early prominent proponent,
later publicly reversed his position, arguing persistently high American
religiosity and substantial Global South religious growth contradict the
thesis at a global level. Model secularization as a real, specific
institutional process (differentiation of religious and political
authority in particular domains, per REL-017 and DIP-006) rather than as
a general law of modernization predicting religion's overall social
decline.

## Hook

Institutional differentiation of religious from political authority is
real and trackable domain by domain (law, governance, knowledge-
production) without implying any general, universal decline in religious
belief or social significance — letting a state secularize its legal
system (REL-017's toleration, DIP-006's sovereignty) while religious
`religious authority` in other domains stays stable or even grows,
against the contested stronger thesis that treats these as one single
inevitable process.
