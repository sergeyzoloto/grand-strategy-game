The prophetic tradition argues ethical obligation to the poor and the
stranger is covenant-binding on the ruler and the community alike, and
licenses the prophet to publicly indict a king's or a people's conduct
against that standard regardless of the indicted party's political power —
one of the Axial Age's own named breakthroughs (see the `_meta/threads/
axial-age.md` thread page), and, until now, the one without an entry to
point to.

## Timing

Amos and Hosea, among the earliest prophetic voices whose material survives
as a distinct textual corpus, are traditionally placed in the 8th century
BCE; Isaiah's material spans a longer and more layered compositional window;
Jeremiah's prophetic activity runs through the late 7th century into the
Babylonian exile's early 6th-century aftermath — a broad era in which the
core covenant-critique move recurs across multiple named prophetic voices
rather than originating with a single founder the way PHI-100's Socrates or
PHI-070's Mahavira more plausibly do.

## Mechanism

The prophetic argument holds that the covenant between the people and their
god is not merely a ritual-observance contract but a binding ethical
obligation — toward the poor, the widow, the orphan, the stranger —
violation of which the prophet is licensed to publicly and specifically
indict, up to and including indicting the king himself, regardless of the
king's political power to punish the messenger: moral authority here derives
from claimed direct divine commission, not from institutional office,
scholarly credential, or civic standing, which is what lets a prophet
address a ruler as a superior addressing a subordinate rather than as one
citizen persuading another.

## Social consequence

Because prophetic authority claims a source (direct divine commission)
entirely external to and unconstrained by existing political or priestly
hierarchy, it creates a standing channel for delegitimizing a ruler's
conduct that neither Greek civic-virtue argument (PHI-140, which works
through participation in a shared political community the ruler is also
part of) nor Confucian role-based ritual ethics (PHI-080, which works through
correctly performing an ongoing hierarchical relationship) provides in the
same form — the prophet doesn't argue the king is failing at his role, but
that the king stands under an obligation no political office can exempt him
from.

## Regional specifics

The prophetic tradition's ethical-covenant critique of power develops within
a specifically Levantine political context of small, frequently
threatened kingdoms whose rulers' conduct carries immediate, visible
consequences (foreign conquest read as covenant punishment) for the whole
community — a context giving the prophet's public indictment of the ruler
real, felt social stakes distinct from either the Athenian polis's internal
civic dialectic or the itinerant Chinese court-advisor's persuasion-for-
patronage relationship (PHI-030's Warring States region_variant).

## Contested

How much of the prophetic corpus reflects the stated pre-exilic dating versus
later post-exilic redaction and retrospective systematization is a live,
unresolved text-critical dispute. Model the ethical content — covenant-bound
obligation applying to ruler and community alike, and the prophet's licensed
public indictment of power that violates it — as reliably datable to the
broad era, while treating precise authorship and composition dates for any
specific passage as uncertain rather than settled.
