This entry owns Mozi's ethical-political doctrine — jian'ai (impartial
concern for all people regardless of kinship distance) defended through
explicitly consequentialist argument (a practice is justified by whether
it benefits the state and common people in aggregate); the Mohist
logicians' formal semantic and inferential work is NAT-003's territory.

## Timing

Mozi teaches in China from roughly -470; his followers organize into a
disciplined, hierarchically-led movement that remains influential through
the Warring States period until fading as an organized school by the
Qin unification in -221.

## Mechanism

Mohism argues directly against Confucian graded love (which scales care
by kinship and social closeness, PHI-004) on consequentialist grounds:
impartial concern for all people, Mozi argues, actually produces greater
aggregate benefit than a graded-care system that tolerates neglecting
distant others — the same consequentialist test (does this practice
increase aggregate benefit to the state and its people) is then applied to
reject elaborate ritual and lavish funeral expenditure as wasteful, and to
condemn aggressive war as a practice that only benefits a narrow ruling
interest while imposing enormous aggregate cost.

## Social consequence

The Mohist movement organizes itself into a disciplined, quasi-military
hierarchy under a chief (juzi) whose followers could be dispatched as
expert reinforcements to help a besieged state defend itself — converting
the anti-aggression doctrine from abstract ethical argument into an
organized applied practice, with Mohist fortification and defensive-siege
expertise becoming renowned in its own right independent of the
underlying ethical theory motivating it.

## Regional specifics

No Mediterranean school in the same era pairs a systematic impartial-
benevolence ethic with organized, technically sophisticated defensive
military engineering the way the Mohists did — the fusion of ethical
doctrine and applied fortification technique, disciples deployed to
directly defend besieged states as ethical practice, has no close Greek or
Roman parallel in this record.

## Contested

Whether Mohist jian'ai and its consequentialist justification are
genuinely comparable to modern utilitarianism, or whether that comparison
anachronistically imports a foreign framework of aggregatable "utility"
onto a differently-structured argument, is disputed in comparative
philosophy. Model Mohist policy evaluation as its own distinct
benefit-maximizing logic rather than treating it as an early, undeveloped
version of Benthamite utilitarianism specifically.

## Hook

Mohist consequentialism supplies a portable policy-evaluation test —
does this practice increase aggregate benefit to state and people — that
the doctrine applies uniformly against graded-kinship ethics, ritual
expenditure, and offensive war alike, backed by an organized movement
willing to deploy real defensive-engineering expertise as the doctrine's
practical enforcement arm.
