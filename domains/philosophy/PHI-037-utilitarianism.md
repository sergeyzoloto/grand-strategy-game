Bentham argues right action is whatever maximizes aggregate pleasure over
pain, quantified and compared directly across persons with no principled
ceiling on what can be traded off against what; Mill's revision reintroduces
qualitative distinction and individual-liberty side-constraints specifically
to block the aggregation logic from licensing a minority's suffering
whenever it maximizes majority welfare.

## Timing

Bentham's Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789)
states the felicific calculus as a systematic decision procedure; Mill's
Utilitarianism (1861) and On Liberty (1859) revise and defend the framework
against its harshest early objections through the following seven decades,
to Mill's continued advocacy until his 1873 death, with this entry's window
closing at Utilitarianism's publication.

## Mechanism

Bentham's calculus treats pleasure and pain as commensurable quantities
(intensity, duration, certainty, proximity, and several further dimensions)
that can in principle be summed across every affected person to determine
which available action produces the greatest aggregate balance of pleasure
over pain — a policy or an individual act is right exactly to the degree it
maximizes this sum, with no person's pleasure or pain counted as
categorically different in kind from any other's. Mill accepts the
aggregate-welfare goal but revises the calculus in two load-bearing ways: a
qualitative distinction between higher (intellectual, aesthetic) and lower
(bodily) pleasures resistant to simple quantity-trading, and the harm
principle — that individual liberty may be restricted only to prevent harm
to others, never merely to maximize aggregate welfare directly — introduced
specifically to block the aggregation logic from licensing a tyrannized
minority's suffering whenever a majority's pleasure outweighs it.

## Social consequence

Because utilitarian reasoning evaluates policy by aggregate outcome rather
than by any actor's rank, birth, or claimed natural right, it supplies a
genuinely reformist policy-evaluation tool usable directly against
inherited privilege — Bentham's own circle applied the calculus to argue for
prison reform, expanded suffrage, and (in Bentham's own unpublished writing)
decriminalizing homosexuality, each case reasoned from aggregate
welfare-effect rather than from custom or inherited moral intuition — while
the same aggregation logic, applied without Mill's liberty side-constraint,
is exactly what critics from Mill onward argue can just as readily justify
sacrificing a minority's basic interests whenever the sums favor it.

## Regional specifics

Utilitarianism develops as a specifically British reform-movement philosophy,
tightly linked in Bentham's own practice to concrete legislative and
institutional proposals (the Panopticon prison design prominent among them)
rather than remaining a purely academic ethical theory — a practical,
policy-facing character distinguishing it from the more purely argumentative
register of Kant's roughly contemporaneous categorical imperative (PHI-022).

## Contested

The aggregation-versus-rights-side-constraint tension between act- and
rule-utilitarianism is a live, unresolved internal dispute, not a problem
Mill's revisions cleanly settle — does maximizing aggregate welfare ever
license instrumentalizing an individual for the greater sum remains a
genuinely open question within the tradition itself. Model act-utilitarian
and rule-utilitarian resolutions as a branch with different practical
consequences in edge cases, rather than treating Mill's harm principle as a
completed patch on Bentham's original calculus.

## Hook

A branch: direct, symmetric rivalry with Kant's categorical imperative
(PHI-022) — outcome-aggregation versus universalizable duty regardless of
outcome — on the same load-bearing-fork pattern PHI-022's own Hook already
uses for its Mohist consequentialism (PHI-009) comparison, now supplying the
actual modern Western utilitarian tradition that comparison was reaching
toward without naming.
