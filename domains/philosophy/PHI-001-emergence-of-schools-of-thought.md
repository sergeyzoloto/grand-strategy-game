Before any specific doctrine can accumulate, spread, or compete, a society
needs an institutional form capable of carrying an argument across
generations — a school: a recognized master, a body of disciples, and a
transmissible teaching distinguishable enough from rivals to be worth
founding a separate lineage over.

## Timing

Greek pre-Socratic circles and the earliest identifiable philosophical
lineages date from around -800; the Warring States "Hundred Schools" era in
China runs from roughly -475 through the Qin unification; the Indian
darsana system's competing orthodox and heterodox schools mature across a
comparable multi-century window from around -600.

## Mechanism

A school converts an individual thinker's insight into a durable,
transmissible institution the way a monastic order converts individual
asceticism into a durable one (REL-009's structurally similar mechanism,
applied to argument rather than renunciation) — disciples preserve,
elaborate, and defend a teaching after its founder's death, competing for
patronage, students, and prestige against rival schools making
incompatible claims, which is what actually forces doctrines to sharpen
their distinguishing claims rather than blur together over time.

## Social consequence

Whichever schools successfully attract court patronage or a sustained
disciple base carry outsized influence over which questions and answers a
whole later tradition inherits as live options — a school that fails to
keep training capable disciples effectively disappears regardless of its
founder's original brilliance, converting a philosophy's long-term survival
into a question of institutional transmission capacity at least as much as
of the argument's original merit.

## Regional specifics

Warring States China's itinerant masters compete directly for employment
across rival courts, meaning a school's persuasiveness had immediate,
observable political stakes distinct from a purely academic dispute. The
Indian darsana system's formal public-debate culture gives doctrinal
survival a similarly high-stakes, adversarial test, spanning both
Vedic-affirming and explicitly heterodox schools within one shared
institutional format. The Yoruba Ifa corpus shows a society can sustain an
equally rigorous and consequential body of systematized knowledge through
interpretive authority over one stable corpus rather than through
competing schools disputing first principles from scratch — a genuinely
different, not lesser, institutional solution to the same underlying
problem of preserving and transmitting complex knowledge.

## Hook

A school's long-term influence tracks its `intellectual authority` — a
function of sustained patronage and disciple-training capacity, not just
the founder's original argument — meaning a philosophically weaker school
with better institutional transmission can outlast a stronger one that
fails to train successors capable of defending it in the next generation's
debates.
