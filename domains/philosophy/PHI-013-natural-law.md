The claim that moral and legal norms can be discovered through reason as
part of the natural order itself, existing prior to and independent of
any particular sovereign's decree — a foundation later secularized into
Enlightenment natural-rights theory (PHI-019) and eventually into codified
international human-rights law (LAW-009).

## Timing

Stoic philosophy (PHI-010) first systematizes a "law of nature" grounded
in universal reason from around -300; Aquinas's medieval Christian
synthesis, integrating natural law as reason's participation in a divine
eternal law, reaches its fullest statement by 1274; the tradition
continues developing and secularizing well beyond both dates.

## Mechanism

Natural law argues moral norms aren't arbitrary human inventions or
purely local custom but can be discovered the way a mathematical truth
is discovered — Cicero's formulation ("true law is right reason in
agreement with nature") makes the discoverability claim explicit: any
sufficiently rational person, regardless of which polity's positive law
they live under, can in principle access the same natural-law
conclusions, which is precisely what gives the framework its later
portability into a claim about rights independent of any specific state's
grant (PHI-019, LAW-009).

## Social consequence

Because natural law claims authority independent of and superior to any
particular ruler's positive law, it supplies a standing standard against
which existing law and rulers can be judged and found wanting — a
resource used across the tradition's long history both to justify
resistance to unjust rulers and, in less liberatory applications, to
declare certain existing social arrangements simply "natural" and
therefore beyond legitimate challenge.

## Regional specifics

The Mu'tazila school's rationalist Islamic theology argues independently
for objective, reason-discoverable moral qualities prior to divine
command, provoking sustained Ash'ari theological opposition insisting
morality is constituted by God's command alone — an internal Islamic
philosophical debate over substantially the same underlying question
natural law poses, developing on a largely separate track from the Latin
Christian tradition. Andean normative thought's grounding of right
conduct in reciprocal obligation (ayni) binding person, community, and
nature together, rather than in a universal rational human nature, shows
"norms grounded in something beyond arbitrary human decree" doesn't
require natural law's specific individual-reason-discovers-fixed-order
structure to reach a comparably serious normative framework.

## Contested

Natural law's derivation of normative conclusions from claims about
nature or reason runs into the unresolved is-ought problem: no purely
descriptive premise validly entails a normative one without an unstated
normative premise doing the real work. Model natural-law-derived claims
downstream of this entry as resting on a contested philosophical
foundation whose is-ought gap remains a live, modelable source of dispute
rather than settled ground the tradition has moved past.

## Hook

Natural law supplies a standing, reason-accessible standard that existing
positive law and rulers can be judged against — a portable justificatory
resource for both resistance to unjust authority and declarations that a
given arrangement is simply "natural," whose own foundational is-ought
gap stays open as a genuine, trackable philosophical weak point rather
than a solved problem.
