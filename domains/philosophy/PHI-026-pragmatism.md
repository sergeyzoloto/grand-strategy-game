American pragmatism rejects both rationalism's (PHI-018) confidence in
pure reason and traditional empiricism's (PHI-019) passive picture of
experience, arguing instead that the meaning and truth of an idea lie in
its practical, experiential consequences — what believing it would
actually let a person do or anticipate.

## Timing

Charles Peirce's 1878 essay "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" first states
the pragmatic maxim; William James popularizes and extends pragmatism as
a full philosophy from the 1890s-1900s; John Dewey's instrumentalism
carries the program into education and democratic theory through the
1920s-30s.

## Mechanism

Peirce's pragmatic maxim holds that a concept's entire meaning consists
in its conceivable practical effects — two apparently different beliefs
that would produce identical practical consequences in every possible
situation are, pragmatically, the same belief. James extends this into a
theory of truth itself: a belief is true to the extent it "works,"
usefully organizing experience and successfully guiding action, rather
than to the extent it corresponds to some independently-existing fact.
Dewey extends the same instrumentalist logic to ideas generally, treating
them as tools for solving concrete problems rather than as mirrors
aiming to accurately reflect a fixed, independent reality.

## Social consequence

Dewey's instrumentalism, applied to education and democracy, argues
schools should cultivate problem-solving capacity through active inquiry
rather than transmit fixed authoritative content, and that democratic
institutions themselves function best as ongoing, revisable experiments in
collective problem-solving rather than as the application of a fixed
political doctrine — a distinctly practice-oriented, anti-dogmatic civic
philosophy with real influence on American public education.

## Regional specifics

Pragmatism develops as a self-consciously American philosophical
tradition, explicitly rejecting both Continental rationalist metaphysics
(PHI-018) and traditional British empiricism (PHI-019) as impractical for
a rapidly industrializing, pluralistic democracy needing philosophy that
could resolve concrete disputes rather than stay confined to abstract
first-principles debate — a regional character its practitioners
themselves frequently emphasized as a deliberate departure from European
philosophical priorities.

## Contested

James's formulation that a belief is true insofar as it usefully guides
action was immediately and heavily criticized — Russell mocked it as
collapsing truth into mere usefulness — and the objection persists: does
"useful to believe" collapse into "true," risking circularity or
relativism. Model pragmatist truth as a genuinely distinct, still-
contested theory of truth, not merely a refinement of correspondence
theories, whose relationship to usefulness-versus-fact remains
unresolved.

## Hook

Pragmatism converts an idea's meaning and truth into a function of its
practical, experience-guiding consequences rather than its correspondence
to independent fact or its derivation from pure reason — a
distinctively American, practice-first philosophical stance whose central
truth-as-usefulness claim remains a live target for the correspondence-
theory objection it was built specifically to displace.
