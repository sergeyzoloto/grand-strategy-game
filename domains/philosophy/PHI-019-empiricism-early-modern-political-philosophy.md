This entry owns empiricism specifically as a philosophical epistemology —
Locke's argument that all knowledge derives from sensory experience
(tabula rasa, the mind as a blank slate written on by experience) — and
its direct extension into political philosophy through Hobbes's and
Locke's rival social-contract theories; NAT-007 owns empiricism as
scientific/experimental method (testing hypotheses against controlled
observation), a related but distinct claim about knowledge.

## Timing

Hobbes's Leviathan appears in 1651, in the immediate aftermath of the
English Civil War; Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding and Two
Treatises of Government both appear in 1689, following England's Glorious
Revolution.

## Mechanism

Locke's epistemological empiricism (knowledge built up from sensory
experience rather than innate rational ideas, PHI-018's rival claim)
extends directly into his political philosophy: since no one is born with
innate knowledge of a natural social hierarchy, political authority can't
rest on any claimed natural or divine right to rule, but only on the
consent of those governed — Hobbes reaches a different political
conclusion (an near-absolute sovereign, needed to escape a brutal state of
nature) from a similarly empirical starting premise, while Locke's more
optimistic state-of-nature assumption yields limited government,
natural rights to life, liberty, and property (grounded in PHI-013's
natural-law tradition), and a people's retained right of revolution
against a government that violates the trust it was granted.

## Social consequence

Grounding political legitimacy in consent rather than inherited or divine
right converts a subject into someone whose ongoing cooperation the state
must in principle continue to earn, not merely command — a genuinely
different relationship between ruler and ruled than the natural-hierarchy
assumptions it displaces, one that directly enables later citizenship
(LAW-002) and human-rights (LAW-009) frameworks built on the same
consent-and-inherent-rights logic.

## Regional specifics

Hobbes and Locke reach opposite political conclusions — near-absolute
sovereignty versus sharply limited government — from a shared empiricist
epistemological starting point and a shared social-contract method,
showing empiricism's political implications aren't determined by the
epistemology alone: how pessimistically or optimistically a theorist
assesses the state of nature does at least as much work in determining
the resulting political theory as the underlying empiricist premise does.

## Contested

Locke's natural-rights philosophy sits in documented tension with his own
direct financial and legal involvement in slavery and colonial
constitution-drafting. Whether this is separable personal hypocrisy or
exposes a real limitation in the theory's original intended scope is
disputed among historians of political thought. Model Lockean natural
rights as a framework whose universal textual claim and its author's own
applied scope diverged in specific, documented ways, rather than assuming
the stated universality straightforwardly describes the theory's original
reach.

## Hook

Social-contract theory relocates political legitimacy from inherited or
divine right to the consent of the governed, with Hobbes's and Locke's
opposite constitutional conclusions from a shared empiricist premise
showing the state-of-nature assumption, not the epistemology alone,
determines the resulting government design — a foundation LAW-002's
citizenship and LAW-009's human-rights frameworks both build directly on,
inheriting its documented scope tensions along with its universal claims.
