Trade conducted through ritual form. Where access to the market required
acknowledging the ruler's superiority, the ritual was the price of market access,
and a breach of ritual was a trade crisis. This is the commercial half of the
tributary order whose legitimacy half DIP-040 carries.

## Timing

From the Ming maritime prohibition of 1371, which made the tribute embassy the only
lawful channel of foreign trade with China, through the kangō tally trade with Japan
from 1404, the Ryukyu relay from the 1370s, the Ningbo incident of 1523, and the
Southeast Asian tribute missions, to the Qing Canton system from 1757. It closes when
the Treaty of Nanking in 1842 abolished the Cohong monopoly and the ritual gate
together.

## Mechanism

**Two circuits on one channel.** Across the Ming and Qing world, diplomacy and
commerce ran on the same track. A foreign ruler sent an embassy that performed
submission, received investiture and return gifts, and brought merchants and
cargo that traded under the embassy's protection. The ritual circuit conferred
legitimacy and the commercial circuit exchanged goods, and neither could run
without the other. A mission without proper ritual was refused, and so was its
trade.

**The ritual is the price of access.** Under the Ming prohibition, a Japanese,
Korean, Ryukyuan or Siamese court that wanted Chinese silk, porcelain, books and
copper cash had one lawful route: to accept a subordinate title and send tribute on
the court's schedule. That made ritual submission a cost many rulers paid
knowingly, because the market was worth it. Ashikaga Yoshimitsu took the title
"King of Japan" and opened the tally trade; his successor found the title
intolerable and gave up the trade. Southeast Asian courts pursued investiture as
commercial and political strategy. The court, for its part, often paid more in
return gifts than it received in tribute, and limited how often missions could come
to hold down the cost. It was buying recognition, not revenue.

**A ritual breach is a trade crisis.** Because the trade existed only inside the
ritual, a dispute over precedence, credentials or protocol closed the market:

- **Ningbo, 1523.** A fight between two Japanese missions over tallies and
  precedence closed the Zhejiang trade office and reduced Japan to one mission a
  decade.
- **Yoshimochi, 1411.** His refusal of the vassal title ended the trade for twenty
  years.
- **Canton, 1834.** Lord Napier's insistence on writing to the viceroy directly,
  rather than petitioning through the merchants, stopped all British trade until he
  gave way.

In none of these cases was the dispute about price or goods. The trade stopped
because the form had been broken.

**Restriction produces relays and smuggling.** A market that can be entered only
through a narrow ritual gate rewards whoever holds a gate pass and punishes everyone
else. Ryukyu's frequent tribute rights made it a relay, carrying Chinese goods to
Japan and Southeast Asia, and its `entrepôt share` came from its ritual status. When
Satsuma took control, it kept Ryukyu's tributary status alive precisely to keep that
channel. Trade excluded from the gate went around it. After Ningbo, Japanese
commerce shifted into the smuggling and raiding networks that MAR-150 carries, since
a prohibition converts ordinary commerce into contraband. MAR-185 records the other
workaround: Macau and Manila prospered by carrying trade the Chinese and Japanese
states would not allow directly.

**Mismatched grammars make the gate impassable.** A state that accepts the ritual
order can trade inside it. A state that rejects the ritual order's premise — that
the foreign ruler is a subordinate — cannot enter without conceding that premise.
Britain wanted sovereign equality, resident ambassadors and open ports. The Qing
classed British trade as managed "mutual trade" under ritual and procedural control.
The Macartney and Amherst embassies could not bridge that, because every act of
protocol meant something different to each side. The `intent legibility` between
them was close to zero, and DIP-055 carries the Macartney embassy as that failure.
When protocol could not open the gate, force did: the Opium War (COM-210) broke the
system and replaced the ritual gate with treaty ports.

## Social consequence

At human scale the circuit made envoys into merchants' escorts and merchants into
members of embassies. A Ryukyuan mission to Fuzhou carried officials who performed
ritual in Beijing and traders who sold cargo along the way. Japanese monks drafted
the diplomatic letters that licensed the tally ships. The Cohong merchants at Canton
bore personal liability for the conduct of the foreigners they dealt with, and some
were ruined by debts to them. A merchant community's fortunes depended on a court's
ritual calendar and on quarrels over precedence it had no part in.

The circuit also shaped who could trade at all. Private merchants on the China
coast were criminals under the Ming prohibition unless they sailed inside an
embassy or bought licences later. That turned whole coastal communities toward
smuggling and made the line between merchant, smuggler and pirate a matter of the
court's current policy rather than of what anyone was doing.

## Regional specifics

East Asia carries the core:

- **Ming prohibition.** It fused ritual and market access in 1371.
- **Kangō tally trade.** It made the ritual credential and the trade licence one
  document.
- **Ningbo, 1523.** It turned a protocol quarrel into a closed market and a
  smuggling economy.
- **Ryukyu.** It relayed Chinese goods across the region on the strength of its
  tribute rights, and kept that status as a channel after Japan took control.

Southeast Asia shows courts choosing tributary status for the trade and protection
it brought. The European encounter at Canton shows the same logic applied to
traders who were not tributaries at all. Their trade still ran through ritual and
procedural form, and breaking that form, as Napier did, closed it. Tokugawa Japan
ran a smaller version of the same circuit, with the Dutch at Nagasaki travelling to
Edo each year to present themselves at court as the condition of their trade.

Mughal India is the absence. There, trade rights were bought with grants and
customs, with no ritual subordination on which access depended, so protocol could
not close the market.

## Contested

Whether "the tributary system" was a coherent institution is disputed:

- **Fairbank** described trade subordinated to a Sinocentric ritual hierarchy.
- **Hamashita** reads tribute as the form commerce took in a regional trading
  system.
- **Perdue and others** argue the system is a later abstraction over pragmatic
  case-by-case arrangements.

Model market access as gated on ritual compliance, and treat the motive as contested.
Some polities performed ritual for the market, some traded as a by-product of
ritual, and the court sometimes paid for missions to buy prestige. A breach of ritual
should close trade, and the trade's commercial weight should decide how quickly both
sides look for a way to reopen it.

## Hook

**Market access is gated on ritual.** Access to a restricting power's market
requires ritual compliance: accepting a subordinate title, sending missions on its
schedule, following its protocol. Trade runs inside the ritual and stops when the
ritual breaks.

- **Breaches close markets.** A breach of precedence, credential or protocol closes
  the market regardless of price or goods. Commercial weight then drives both sides
  toward reopening, either through renewed compliance or by workarounds.
- **Gate passes create relays.** A polity with privileged ritual access gains
  `entrepôt share` as a relay. Trade excluded from the gate moves into smuggling and
  into third-party entrepôts.
- **Mismatched grammars block entry.** A power that rejects the ritual premise
  cannot enter the gate without conceding it, and near-zero `intent legibility`
  between the two orders makes negotiation fail. Force, not protocol, then opens
  the market, and ends the circuit.
- **The price can be a subsidy.** The restricting power may pay more in return
  gifts than it receives, buying recognition rather than revenue, and it limits
  mission frequency to control the cost.
