A diplomatic party or arbiter doesn't have to be a territorial sovereign or
its subordinate — a religious authority claiming standing above rival
monarchs, or a confederation of cities pooling collective leverage no
single member could command alone, can each negotiate, arbitrate, and even
make war as a full party in its own right.

## Timing

The Hanseatic League formally organizes from 1358, reaching peak
negotiating leverage across the Baltic and North Sea through the following
century; papal arbitration between Catholic monarchs reaches its most
consequential case with Alexander VI's 1493 bulls, formalized as the 1494
Treaty of Tordesillas, with both forms of non-sovereign diplomatic agency
persisting in weakened form through the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which
DIP-006 covers as substantially displacing this kind of religiously- or
commercially-grounded diplomatic standing with purely territorial-state
sovereignty.

## Mechanism

The Papacy arbitrates between Catholic monarchs by invoking an authority
neither party can straightforwardly claim over the other — a shared
religious framework both crowns have independent reason to publicly
respect — letting a settlement stick that neither side could have imposed
on the other unilaterally without directly testing relative military
strength. The Hanseatic League works by the opposite route: individual
member cities lack the leverage to negotiate favorable terms or enforce
trade privilege against a territorial ruler on their own, so the League
pools that leverage across dozens of cities, letting it negotiate, and on
occasion fight, as a single collective party a sovereign ruler has to treat
as seriously as it would treat another state.

## Social consequence

Because neither actor holds territorial sovereignty in the ordinary sense,
each has to substitute something else for the raw coercive capacity a
territorial state can draw on directly — the Papacy substitutes claimed
religious authority both negotiating monarchs have independent reason to
respect, the League substitutes pooled collective economic leverage no
single member city could match — meaning each actor's diplomatic standing
is real but conditional in a way a sovereign state's isn't: it holds only
as long as the parties dealing with it continue finding the substituted
authority more useful to respect than to test.

## Regional specifics

No comparable non-sovereign diplomatic actor develops in East Asia in this
era — Chinese and Japanese merchant activity stays organized within
existing state authority rather than as an independent confederated bloc,
and no equivalent claim to trans-monarchical religious arbitration exists
outside the specifically Catholic institutional context — showing this
particular route to diplomatic agency is tied to specific European
religious and commercial institutional conditions rather than a universal
option available to any sufficiently organized non-state actor.

## Contested

How much genuine independent authority papal arbitration carried, versus
how much it functioned as religious cover for a division the parties had
already substantially agreed to bilaterally, is disputed among historians
of the Iberian expansion. Model papal arbitration's binding force as
tracking the arbitrating monarchs' own continued interest in a
neutral-seeming settlement, not as an authority those monarchs had no power
to simply disregard once it stopped serving them.

## Hook

A gate: supplies the actual mechanism behind imperial oceanic diplomacy's
(DIP-007) own founding case — who arbitrated Tordesillas, and why the
parties accepted it — and establishes that a diplomatic party's standing
can rest on pooled economic leverage or claimed religious authority rather
than territorial sovereignty, conditional on that substituted authority
remaining more useful to respect than to test.
