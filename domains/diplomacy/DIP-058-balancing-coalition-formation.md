Disproportionate power generates coalitions against itself, regardless of the
powerful state's intentions. A state that grows strong enough to dominate its
neighbours gives them a reason to combine, even if it means them no harm, because
they cannot rely on its restraint lasting.

## Timing

From the Warring States coalitions against Qin from 318 BCE and the successors'
coalition against Antigonus at Ipsus in 301 BCE, through the Deccan coalition at
Talikota in 1565, the Holy Leagues against the Ottomans, the coalitions against
Louis XIV and Napoleon, the Triple Alliance against Paraguay and the Entente
against Germany, to the Cold War blocs and the coalition against Iraq in 1990-91.
The period closes around 2000, when the absence of a coalition against a single
superpower became the prediction's hardest test.

## Mechanism

**Balancing responds to capability. The spiral in DIP-055 responds to misread
intent.** This distinction is the reason the two entries are separate. In a
security-dilemma spiral, neither side is aggressive, and each reads the other's
defensive measures as a threat. The problem is illegible intent, and the remedy is
making intent legible. In balancing, the other side really is growing stronger,
and a coalition forms against that growth whether its intentions are benign or
not, because good intentions can change and capability cannot be taken back. The
problem is concentration of power, and the only remedy is redistributing it. From
outside, both look like mutual arming and hostile alignment. They have different
causes, different triggers and different ways of ending, and they should not be
modelled as one mechanism.

**Preponderance is its own provocation.** A polity's `hegemonic share` is its share
of the capability in the system it belongs to. As it rises, the other members face
a choice the Warring States strategists named: combine against the leader, or make
separate terms with it. Combining preserves their independence at the cost of
coordination. Making terms buys safety now at the cost of dependence later. The
classical statement is Hiero of Syracuse aiding Carthage so that Rome would not
command without contest. Crowe's 1907 memorandum stated it as British policy: oppose
whichever power seeks preponderance, whatever it says it intends.

**Coalitions form against the strongest, not the most hostile.** The members of a
balancing coalition are often each other's rivals: the Diadochi against Antigonus,
the Deccan sultanates against Vijayanagara, Protestant and Catholic states against
Louis XIV, the United States and China against the Soviet Union. They combine
because the leader is stronger than any of them, and they return to fighting each
other once it is reduced. That is why balancing coalitions are temporary. They rest on
sworn alliances between parties who do not trust each other (DIP-020), and their
`alliance cohesion` depends on the threat persisting. The Deccan sultanates fell to
fighting within years of Talikota, and the anti-Napoleonic coalitions broke and
re-formed six times.

**Collective action is the weakness.** Every coalition member would prefer the
others to bear the cost of containing the leader, and the leader can offer separate
terms to detach them one at a time. Qin defeated the vertical alliance by making
bilateral peace with its members in turn, and Napoleon broke several coalitions the
same way. Balancing fails when the leader can divide the coalition faster than the
coalition can combine its strength.

**Pariah isolation is balancing by other means.** A state judged aggressive or
dangerous can be contained without war, by collective refusal of trade, recognition
and membership. Sanctions (DIP-190) and derecognition (DIP-200) are the instruments.
This entry owns the mechanism that makes them collective: a coalition's shared
judgement that one state's power or conduct must be checked. Isolation works best
against a state whose power depends on others' cooperation, and worst against one
that can supply itself.

**Institutions can contain the dynamic.** The Concert of Europe (DIP-130) built a
standing arrangement on top of a balance: great powers managing spheres so that no
one of them would provoke a coalition by grasping for more. The alliance system that
replaced it turned balancing into fixed commitments. DIP-170 carries the cascade
that followed when those commitments triggered at once.

## Social consequence

At human scale balancing is expensive and intermittent. The populations of
coalition states pay for armies raised against a power that may never have meant to
attack them. The population of the leading state faces enemies it may not have
chosen to make. Paraguay lost a large fraction of its population to a coalition war
that followed its ruler's gamble. Coalition wars last because they are fought until
the leader is reduced, not until a single dispute is settled, and they end in
settlements that redraw the map to prevent the next preponderance, as Utrecht and
Vienna did.

Balancing also forces uncomfortable partnerships. Catholic and Protestant states
fought side by side against Louis XIV. The United States and China cooperated
against the Soviet Union while neither recognised the other's political order.
Governments must explain to their own populations why yesterday's enemy is today's
necessary ally, and the answer — that the greater power is the greater danger —
is rarely popular.

## Regional specifics

Balancing is not a European invention exported to other regions:

- **East Asia.** It supplies the earliest named theory, the vertical and horizontal
  strategies against Qin, and the earliest failure of balancing through collective
  action.
- **The Hellenistic Mediterranean.** It supplies a clean coalition of rivals at
  Ipsus, and the classical maxim through Polybius.
- **South Asia.** The Deccan sultanates destroyed Vijayanagara, and the Company,
  the Marathas and the Nizam combined against Mysore.
- **The Middle East.** Ottoman preponderance drew Holy Leagues and Safavid overtures
  to Europe, and a regional coalition formed against Iraq in 1991.
- **Europe.** It made balancing an explicit doctrine, written into the Treaty of
  Utrecht and the Crowe memorandum.
- **The Americas.** They supply a coalition war against Paraguay and the Sino-American
  opening against Soviet power.

East Asia under Ming and Qing hegemony is the contested absence. Where preponderant
power was accepted as a legitimate hierarchy (DIP-040), it may not have generated a
coalition at all.

## Contested

Whether states actually balance is disputed:

- **Waltz** predicts coalitions against concentrated power regardless of intent.
- **Walt** narrows the trigger to threat, which adds perceived intention and
  partly re-imports DIP-055's variable.
- **Schweller** shows states bandwagoning for gain.
- **Schroeder** shows European states often hiding or transcending rather than
  balancing.
- **After 1991** no coalition formed against the United States.
- **Kang** argues East Asia did not balance against China.

Model coalition formation as a probability rising with the leader's `hegemonic
share`. It should be lowered where the leader is accepted as a legitimate hierarchy
or its lead is too great to challenge, and offset by bandwagoning incentives for
states expecting to gain from its rise. Never make it automatic.

## Hook

**Capability is the trigger.** Each polity has a `hegemonic share` of its system's
capability. As a leader's share rises, the probability that the other members form a
coalition against it rises too, whatever the leader intends. This is the capability
trigger. It is separate from DIP-055's misread-intent spiral and must stay separate:
a benign leader can still be balanced against, and two defensive equals can still
spiral.

**Coalitions are temporary.** They form among rivals against the strongest, not the
most hostile, and their `alliance cohesion` decays once the leader is reduced. A
leader can defeat a coalition by detaching members with separate terms faster than
they can combine.

**Isolation extends the mechanism short of war.** Coalition-wide sanctions and
derecognition work in proportion to how much the target depends on others'
cooperation.

**Legitimate hierarchy suspends balancing.** A leader accepted as the legitimate
centre of an order can hold a large share without provoking a coalition, and
bandwagoning incentives offset balancing for states that expect to gain.
