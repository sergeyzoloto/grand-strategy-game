Cross-linguistic negotiation requires a trusted, competent intermediary
standing between the parties — a person whose own skill, loyalty, and
capacity for deliberate or accidental mistranslation becomes a real,
unavoidable variable in how faithfully any negotiation's actual terms
actually get transmitted at all.

## Timing

Interpreters accompanying diplomatic missions are attested wherever
diplomatic contact crosses a language boundary from the earliest
recoverable evidence; the Ottoman dragoman office institutionalizes the
role formally from around 1500; Malintzin's mediation during the 1519
Spanish-Aztec first contact is one of this record's most consequential
single-interpreter cases.

## Mechanism

Where two negotiating parties share no common language, every term of an
agreement, every threat, and every offer passes through a specific
individual's own linguistic competence and — critically — their own
incentives: an interpreter can soften a threat, sharpen a demand, or
simply fail to convey a nuance neither negotiating party would ever detect
independently, converting the interpreter from a neutral conduit into an
active participant whose own position (whom they were raised by, whom they
fear, whom they hope to please) shapes the substance of what each side
believes was actually agreed.

## Social consequence

An interpreter's own social position — frequently a cultural or linguistic
go-between belonging fully to neither negotiating side — creates a durable
structural tension: useful precisely because of a hybrid background that
also makes their loyalty a standing object of suspicion from both
directions, a position some interpreters (the Ottoman Phanariote dragomans)
convert into real independent political influence and others (Malintzin)
experience as a role assigned under coercion with no comparable personal
leverage of their own.

## Regional specifics

The Ottoman dragoman office's formal institutionalization gives linguistic
mediation a recognized diplomatic rank and career path, while Chinese
tributary missions' comparatively ad hoc reliance on border-population or
resident-foreigner interpreters shows the same underlying function can
operate without any comparable formal office. Malintzin's case shows a
single interpreter's own choices and interests can shape the actual course
of a first-contact negotiation as much as either principal party's stated
intentions.

## Hook

A modifier: converts the fidelity of any cross-linguistic negotiation into
a function of a specific, fallible, potentially self-interested
intermediary rather than a costless given — a mistrusted, absent, or
independently-motivated interpreter is a real constraint on how reliably
tribute diplomacy (DIP-040), resident embassies (DIP-080), and first-contact
colonial diplomacy (DIP-090) can actually function.
