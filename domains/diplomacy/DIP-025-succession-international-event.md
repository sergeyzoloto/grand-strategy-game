A disputed succession converts an internal question into an international one.
Every claimant needs outside recognition and every neighbour has a preferred
claimant, so a legitimacy vacuum is an invitation. Intervening in a succession is
cheaper than conquest, produces a client rather than a subject, and can be
presented as upholding a legitimate claim rather than as aggression.

## Timing

From Assyria and Elam fighting through Babylonian claimants from 703 BCE, with the
Hittite-Egyptian marriage request of the fourteenth century BCE as an earlier
instance, through Rome and Parthia over Armenia's crown and the Islamic investiture
of sultans, Chinese investiture of Korean and Vietnamese rulers, the Mughal
restoration of 1544 and Russia's Time of Troubles, to the European succession wars
of the eighteenth century and the Doctrine of Lapse in British India to 1856. The
entry closes around 1900, as dynastic succession stops being the main way states
change rulers.

## Mechanism

**The inter-polity half only.** A succession dispute has internal politics: court
factions, regents, rival heirs, law. Those belong to LAW and are not carried here.
This entry carries only what makes a succession a diplomatic event: the need for
outside recognition, the invitation to intervene, and the predictable windows that
invite it.

**Accession reopens a state's foreign relations.** Where treaties and tributary
bonds were sworn personally by rulers (DIP-020), they lapsed with the ruler who
swore them. Every accession therefore required a wave of diplomatic acts:

- **Missions** announcing the new ruler.
- **Oaths renewed** with neighbours and allies.
- **Investiture requested** from a suzerain.
- **Tribute renewed** under the new name.

These were costly and uncertain, and a refusal was a statement that the new ruler
was not accepted. Chinese investiture of tributary kings, the Islamic khutba and
coinage, and papal or imperial confirmation in Europe are all instruments of this
recognition. DIP-040 carries the tributary legitimacy relationship. This entry
carries the moment at which it has to be renewed, and what happens when renewal is
contested.

**A disputed succession invites intervention.** When two claimants both accept the
same principle of legitimacy and disagree about who holds it, each needs outside
support and each neighbour can choose. Intervention on behalf of a claimant costs
less than conquest, because the claimant brings a party inside the country, and
because the claim supplies justification. It also yields a client who owes his
throne to the intervening power, often for a price: Kandahar for the Safavids,
border districts for the Ming, recognition shared with Rome for Parthia. Marriage
alliances (DIP-060) are the upstream cause of many such claims. A dynastic marriage
made generations earlier supplies a foreign house with a lawful claim when the line
fails, which is how the Bourbon and Habsburg claims to Spain arose.

**Revolution and succession have different intervention logics.** DIP-120 carries
revolution, which changes the principle of legitimacy itself. Other powers resist
it to protect their own principle, so their instrument is non-recognition and their
aim is to restore the old order. A succession dispute contests a claim within a
principle all parties accept. Other powers intervene for advantage, not
self-protection. Recognition becomes transactional rather than principled, and
settlements come by partition, compensation, renunciation or shared recognition.
Utrecht recognised Philip V in Spain on condition he renounced France, and Rhandeia
shared Armenia's recognition between two empires. A revolution is refused; a
succession is bargained over.

**The minority and regency window is scheduled.** A child ruler, or a regency, is a
predictable period of reduced capacity, and it is predictable to rivals as well.
The age of an heir, the illness of a ruler with only a daughter, and the extinction
of a line are visible years ahead, so vulnerability arrives on a timetable. That is
why rulers tried to buy recognition in advance, as the Pragmatic Sanction's
guarantees did. It is also why those guarantees tended to fail at exactly the
moment they were needed, when the window opened and the guarantor's interest in
intervening exceeded its interest in keeping its word.

**A recognising power can weaponise the rules.** Where one power controls
recognition, it can set the rules of succession to its own advantage. The Doctrine
of Lapse refused to recognise adopted heirs in Indian states and annexed states
whose rulers died without natural sons. The recognition that had been a
precondition of rule became a mechanism of annexation.

## Social consequence

At human scale a disputed succession is borne by the court and the country the
claimants fight over:

- **Royal children** are raised abroad as future candidates.
- **Widowed queens** write to foreign kings for husbands.
- **Envoys** travel to a suzerain to beg investiture for a new king.
- **Provinces** are ceded as the price of a throne.

In Russia's Time of Troubles, a Polish garrison held the Kremlin and pretenders
rose one after another. In Vietnam, a dynasty fled north for Chinese help and
returned with an army that was destroyed. A population whose ruler owes his throne
to a foreign power lives under a client relationship it did not choose.

For the claimants themselves, outside support comes with obligations that outlast
the crisis. A king restored by a foreign army governs with that army's patron
watching, and the territorial price of his restoration becomes a grievance for his
successors.

## Regional specifics

The mechanism is not European, and the European succession wars are one form of
it:

- **Mesopotamia.** Assyria and Elam fought through Babylonian claimants in the
  first millennium BCE, and a Hittite prince died answering an Egyptian queen's
  letter.
- **Rome and Parthia.** They settled Armenia by sharing the act of recognition.
- **The Islamic world.** It recognised rulers through the khutba and the coinage,
  and a caliph with no army sold investiture to sultans with armies.
- **East Asia.** It made investiture the gate to legitimate kingship. Ming China
  took territory as the price of recognising a Vietnamese usurper. Qing China
  intervened for one Vietnamese dynasty and, defeated, recognised its rival.
- **South Asia.** A Safavid army restored a Mughal emperor for Kandahar,
  European companies fought through rival nawabs, and a colonial doctrine turned
  recognition into annexation.
- **Kongo.** Portuguese backing in a succession began a foreign relationship that
  shaped the kingdom for centuries.
- **Europe.** It made intervention a norm in the eighteenth century and showed
  advance guarantees failing when the window opened.

Tokugawa Japan is the absence: where no outside power could reach and no
investiture was sought, successions stayed internal.

## Hook

**Accession is a diplomatic event.** Where treaty and tributary bonds are personal
to the ruler, each accession voids them. The new ruler must win renewed recognition
through missions, oaths, investiture and tribute renewal, each costly and each
refusable. Refusal leaves the ruler weakened abroad and contested at home.

**A disputed succession invites intervention.** Where claimants share a legitimacy
principle, neighbours back rival claimants because intervention is cheaper than
conquest. It yields a client rather than a subject, often for territory, and is
justified by the claim. Revolutions, which change the principle, are met with
refusal instead. Successions are met with bargaining, and settle by partition,
renunciation or shared recognition.

**Vulnerability is scheduled.** Minorities, regencies and the extinction of a line
are visible years ahead. Advance guarantees can be bought, and their `alliance
cohesion` should fail at the moment of need when the guarantor's gain from
intervening exceeds its gain from keeping faith. Dynastic marriages generations
earlier supply foreign houses with lawful claims when a line fails.

**Recognition can become annexation.** A recognising power that controls the
rules of succession can refuse recognition in order to annex, and a client's
throne carries a standing obligation of `protection rent` to the power that placed
it there.

**The absence.** Where no external power can reach and no recognition is sought,
succession crises stay internal.
