This entry owns sovereignty's specific 1648 institutional package — formal
juridical equality between states, territorial integrity, and mutual
non-interference in internal (including religious, REL-013) affairs — as
a particular historical settlement, not a timeless or universal default
for how polities have always related to each other.

## Timing

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) is the settlement conventionally credited
with establishing the core package; the model becomes the presumptive
global template for international recognition through the colonial and
decolonization era, with the last wave of newly sovereign states joining
the UN-centered system by the mid-1900s.

## Mechanism

Formal sovereign equality means a small state and a great power hold the
same nominal juridical status in international law regardless of actual
power disparity, while non-interference specifically bars other states
from legitimately intervening in a sovereign's internal and religious
affairs (directly resolving REL-013's confessional-state tensions by
making religious choice a matter of internal sovereign discretion rather
than legitimate grounds for external intervention) — a package that
trades away hierarchical, religiously-unified international order for a
horizontal system of mutually recognizing, formally equal territorial
units, one that presupposes the standing professional-embassy
infrastructure and reciprocal immunity (DIP-005) already in place to let
formally equal sovereigns actually maintain continuous relations with each
other at all.

## Social consequence

Once sovereign equality and non-interference become the accepted
international norm, recognition itself becomes a scarce and consequential
diplomatic resource — a polity's claim to sovereign status matters only
insofar as other sovereigns are willing to recognize and treat it as such,
converting statehood from a simple fact of effective control into
something with an irreducibly social and diplomatic dimension.

## Regional specifics

Chinese tianxia and the tributary system it underlies (DIP-001) organize
international order as an explicitly graduated hierarchy rather than a
horizontal system of equals, a coherent and durable alternative model
operating on its own terms for millennia before eventually encountering
and adapting to the globally-spreading Westphalian template. The Islamic
ummah's vision of a single believing community sits in real normative
tension with Westphalian sovereignty's assumption that political and
religious boundaries are properly separate matters — two structurally
different ideals for how legitimate political order should relate to
religious community.

## Contested

The standard account credits the 1648 Peace of Westphalia with founding
sovereign equality and non-interference as international norms.
Revisionist diplomatic history — Andreas Osiander's argument being the
most influential version — contends this narrative is substantially a
later international-relations-theory construction: the actual 1648
settlements left the Holy Roman Empire's nested, overlapping feudal and
ecclesiastical authority largely intact, nothing resembling clean
territorial sovereign equality among formally identical units. Model the
Westphalian "package" as the template later diplomatic practice and
19th-20th-century IR theory treated as foundational and built upon,
while leaving open whether 1648 itself was ever really the clean origin
point that later theory retrospectively made of it.

## Hook

The Westphalian package converts international order from hierarchy
(DIP-001's tributary model, China's tianxia) into formal equality between
mutually non-interfering sovereigns — a specific European settlement that
later becomes the presumptive global recognition template, displacing or
absorbing structurally different prior models (tianxia, the ummah) rather
than representing how inter-polity relations have always necessarily
worked.
