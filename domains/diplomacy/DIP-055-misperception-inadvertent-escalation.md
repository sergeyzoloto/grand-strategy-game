Diplomacy is signalling under uncertainty, and a signal is not the intention behind
it. A state must infer another's intentions from actions that are ambiguous by
construction, so escalation can occur between parties neither of whom wanted it.
That is a different mechanism from escalation by design and must not be collapsed
into it.

## Timing

From Thucydides's account of the Peloponnesian War in -431, with the Amarna
correspondence of the fourteenth century BCE as an earlier instance, through the
Ming investiture of Hideyoshi in 1596 and the Macartney embassy of 1793, the Ems
telegram of 1870, the July Crisis of 1914, the Sino-Indian war of 1962, and the
1967 war, to the double scare of 1983, Brasstacks in 1987
and the Vincennes shootdown in 1988.

## Mechanism

**The signal is not the intention.** Every diplomatic action is read by a
counterpart who cannot see the intention behind it. A mobilisation meant as
deterrence reads as preparation for attack. A concession meant as reasonableness
reads as weakness inviting more. An ally's reassurance to one party reads as
encouragement to another. What one polity can accurately infer of another's aims
is its `intent legibility`, and it is low by default, because the actions available
to a state are compatible with more than one purpose. Escalation between two
defensive states follows directly from that ambiguity.

**The spiral responds to misread intent. Balancing in DIP-058 responds to
capability.** A coalition forming against a rising power responds to capability:
the other side really is getting stronger, whatever it intends. The spiral
described here responds to misread intent: neither side is aggressive, and each
reads the other as if it were. The two can look the same from outside, as mutual
arming and hostile alignment, and they have different causes and different
remedies. Balancing is answered by the distribution of power; the spiral is
answered by making intent legible. They must not be treated as one mechanism.

**The security dilemma.** Defensive preparation is indistinguishable from offensive
preparation: a fortress, a standing army, a forward post or a new warship class
can serve either purpose. A measure that increases one state's security therefore
decreases another's, and the other responds in kind. Neither is aggressive, and
both end up armed and afraid (Herz; Jervis). The dilemma is sharpest where
offensive and defensive postures cannot be told apart, and weakest where they
can.

**Commitment credibility.** A threat deters only if believed, so states take costly,
irreversible actions to make threats credible: public pledges, alliance guarantees,
forward deployments, burnt bridges (Schelling). Those actions remove the state's
own room to back down, which is their purpose and their danger. The Athenians at
Melos could not spare a neutral island because their credibility with every other
subject depended on not doing so. Alliance obligations mechanise the same logic
across a whole system, and DIP-170 carries its collapse in 1914.

**Mobilisation timetables and automaticity.** Where a military response is
pre-planned and time-critical, the decision window collapses and the machinery runs
ahead of the politics. A state that must mobilise within days or lose the race
cannot wait to find out what its rival's mobilisation means. Early-warning systems
compress the window further, to minutes, which is why the 1983 false alarm turned
on one officer's judgement. The same compression runs through the radar-based
architecture WAR-315 describes.

**Information lag and distortion.** Envoy immunity (DIP-010) makes the channel safe;
it does not make it accurate. An envoy acting on instructions weeks old, a
garbled message, a mistranslation or a self-serving intermediary can each change
what one court believes the other intends. Interpreters (DIP-030) and negotiators
shape the message. The Hideyoshi negotiators distorted it on both sides to avoid
reporting failure. Relay messaging (MED-030) sets how stale the instructions are.
The telegraph (MED-100) shortened the lag and removed the deliberation time the lag
had provided. That is a real trade, not an improvement. A slow channel let tempers
cool and gave a court time to reconsider before its reply arrived, and an instant
channel carried the first reaction.

**Mismatched ritual grammars make legibility impossible, not merely low.** Where two
states read diplomatic conduct through incompatible systems, the problem is not a
misreading that more information corrects. Tribute hierarchy against sovereign
equality, investiture against treaty: every act one side performs means something
different to the other. The Macartney embassy and the Hideyoshi investiture both
failed this way. DIP-040 carries the tributary order whose grammar Macartney could
not enter, and DIP-075 the trade that ran through it, which is why the embassy's
failure was also a failure to open a market.

## Social consequence

At human scale the mechanism concentrates on a few people in narrow windows:

- **Negotiators** who misreport to their own sovereign rather than admit
  failure.
- **Duty officers** reading a screen at night.
- **A ship's crew** under fire deciding in minutes what a radar contact is.
- **Envoys** kneeling or refusing to kneel.

Whether a crisis escalates can turn on whether one of them reads the ambiguity
correctly or passes it on, and the officer who declines to report a false attack
has no rule that tells him he is right.

For the populations of states caught in a spiral, the war arrives as something no
one appears to have chosen. Each government believes it acted defensively and was
answered with aggression. That belief makes spiral wars unusually hard to end,
because each side's account of how the war began is sincere and incompatible with
the other's. Espionage (DIP-110) is one of the few tools for reading intent
directly, and its failures, as in the Soviet search for signs of attack in 1983,
can deepen the misreading it was meant to prevent.

## Regional specifics

The entry is not a 1914 entry. Its cases:

- **Mediterranean.** Thucydides supplies the first theory: fear of growing power
  and credibility enforced by cruelty.
- **Amarna letters.** They show great kings misreading gifts as signals of status
  more than a millennium earlier.
- **East Asia.** It carries the strongest pre-modern cases. In the Ming–Hideyoshi
  investiture, intermediaries' distortions hid an unbridgeable gap and produced a
  second invasion. The Macartney embassy shows two ritual orders unable to read
  each other at all.
- **South Asia.** The 1962 Forward Policy was read as encroachment, and the
  Brasstacks exercise as preparation for attack, which makes exercises and forward
  posts as dangerous as mobilisations.
- **The Middle East.** A false intelligence report became a deterrent display
  read as a threat of war in 1967, and a radar misidentification killed an
  airliner in 1988.
- **Europe.** The Ems telegram shows deliberate manipulation exploiting inadvertent
  overreaction, and the July Crisis shows the timetables, carried in DIP-170.
- **1983.** A machine's false alarm and an exercise read as cover for attack
  brought the nuclear relationship WAR-330 describes closest to inadvertent use.

## Contested

Whether inadvertent escalation is genuinely distinct from deliberate escalation with
deniability is argued. Jervis and the perceptual school treat misperception and the
spiral as real causes of unwanted wars. Fearon's rationalist account attributes
war to private information, incentives to misrepresent and commitment problems, so
apparent misperception is often concealment. Trachtenberg argues the 1914 timetables
were less automatic than the inadvertence story holds.

Model escalation on two separate quantities: each side's actual intent, and each
side's `intent legibility` of the other. Defensive intents with low legibility
produce inadvertent escalation. An offensive intent behind low legibility produces
deliberate escalation with deniability. The model must be able to produce both, and
must not let the record decide which a particular crisis was.

## Hook

Each polity holds an `intent legibility` toward each other polity: how accurately
it can read that polity's intentions from its actions. It is low by default and
lowered by four things:

- **Indistinguishable postures.** Defensive preparation looks offensive.
- **Mismatched ritual grammars.** Legibility near zero, not merely reduced.
- **Distorting intermediaries.** Negotiators and interpreters shape the message.
- **Information lag,** traded against deliberation time as `information speed`
  rises.

Escalation is driven by two separate inputs, actual intent and read intent. Two
defensive polities with low mutual legibility can arm and fight without either
intending it. Credibility-building commitments raise deterrence while removing each
side's ability to back down. Time-critical mobilisation plans and early-warning
systems collapse the decision window, so a misread signal escalates before it can
be checked.

This is distinct from balancing (DIP-058), which responds to real capability rather
than misread intent. It is also distinct from deliberate escalation with deniability,
which the same low legibility makes possible.
