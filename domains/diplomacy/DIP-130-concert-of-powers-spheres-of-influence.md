Rather than each great power pursuing its interests unilaterally and
risking direct confrontation, a "concert" has the major powers
periodically negotiate and jointly manage potential flashpoints and
mutual spheres of influence — a standing coordination practice layered on
top of ordinary bilateral treaty-making (DIP-020).

## Timing

The Congress of Vienna (1815) establishes the Concert of Europe's
practice of periodic great-power consultation; the model persists,
with real strain and periodic breakdown, through the outbreak of World War
One in 1914.

## Mechanism

Concert diplomacy works by having the major powers agree in advance,
however informally, on recognized spheres where each has priority
interest, reducing the number of situations where two great powers stumble
into direct confrontation over an issue neither had realized the other
cared about — the system's stability depends entirely on all major
participants continuing to value the coordination more than they value
any specific gain available from breaking it, which is exactly what
eventually fails as rising and revisionist powers judge the existing
spheres arrangement no longer serves their own growing interests.

## Social consequence

For populations and territories that fall within an assigned sphere of
influence but aren't themselves party to the negotiations, the concert
system functions as a great-power arrangement made over their heads
entirely — comparable in this specific respect to the Berlin Conference's
exclusion of African polities from the negotiations that determined their
own territories' fate, since the same underlying great-power-coordination
logic doesn't require or particularly value the affected population's own
input.

## Regional specifics

The Berlin Conference shows concert-style great-power coordination applied
directly to a non-European theatre, with the same logic of avoiding
direct great-power confrontation through negotiated mutual restraint
producing the African partition covered in DIP-090. The Chinese tributary
hierarchy achieves comparable multi-power stability through an entirely
different structural principle — graduated subordination to one
acknowledged center rather than periodic negotiation among nominal
equals — showing concert diplomacy is one solution among several to the
underlying problem of managing multiple powers' potentially conflicting
interests in the same regional space.

## Hook

Concert diplomacy trades each power's unilateral freedom of action for
reduced risk of accidental direct confrontation, through negotiated
spheres of influence that function as a great-power arrangement over the
heads of whatever population or territory falls within them — a system
whose stability lasts exactly as long as every major participant
continues valuing the coordination over the specific gains available from
breaking it.
