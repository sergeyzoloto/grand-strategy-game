The engineering of the road itself is ENG-003's territory; this entry owns
what a road-and-garrison network does diplomatically — projecting a
distant center's legitimacy and administrative reach across subject
populations who would otherwise experience imperial authority as remote
and abstract.

## Timing

The Persian Royal Road's administrative and messenger function (already
noted for its engineering in ENG-003) matures alongside its -500
construction; the Inca road network's parallel chasqui relay and garrison-
provisioning system develops from around 1450 through the empire's 1533
collapse.

## Mechanism

A road network only converts into genuine political unification once it
carries administrative messaging and garrison deployment on top of its
physical transport function — the road makes rapid communication and
troop movement possible, but it's the standing practice of dispatching
messengers, provisioning garrisons at fixed intervals, and processing
administrative traffic along that infrastructure that actually converts
physical connectivity into a subject population's lived experience of
belonging to one unified, responsive political entity rather than a
distant abstraction.

## Social consequence

For populations along the route, the visible, recurring presence of
imperial messengers, garrisons, and provisioning demands makes central
authority a concrete daily fact rather than an abstract claim — this
cuts both ways, since the same infrastructure that delivers protection and
integration into a wider trade and administrative network also delivers
extraction demands and military presence a subject population has no real
ability to refuse.

## Regional specifics

The Inca chasqui relay system and mandatory storehouse provisioning
achieve unification-by-infrastructure across some of the most challenging
terrain of any empire in this record, converting Cuzco's authority into a
practically enforceable reality across vast distance. Roman infrastructure
achieves comparable reach through a more explicitly military-first design
logic, with administrative and commercial use developing as a secondary
benefit of primarily military-purposed construction — a difference in
design priority rather than in ultimate unifying effect.

## Hook

A road network only becomes a genuine unification tool once messengers and
garrisons actively use it — the physical infrastructure (ENG-003) is a
precondition, but it's the standing administrative and military traffic
riding on top of it that actually converts distant imperial `legibility`
into a subject population's lived, unavoidable daily experience of central
authority.
