Recognition, once granted, is not necessarily permanent — a state that held
internationally recognized status for years or decades can lose it, not
through military defeat, but through other states' collective decision to
recognize a rival claimant instead.

## Timing

The People's Republic of China's founding in 1949 opens over two decades of
competing claims to represent "China" internationally; the United Nations
General Assembly transfers China's seat from the Republic of China (Taiwan)
to the People's Republic in 1971; the cascade of individual states switching
recognition accordingly continues through the decade, with the United
States itself completing the switch in 1979.

## Mechanism

Where DIP-100 and DIP-120 model recognition as something a new or
revolutionary claimant has to win, this entry models the reverse: an
already-recognized state's status remains contingent on continued
diplomatic consensus indefinitely, not banked permanently once granted —
when a rival claimant's case becomes more diplomatically attractive
(greater size, market access, or strategic value, in the Taiwan/PRC case),
individual recognizing states can and do switch their recognition to the
rival, one bilateral relationship at a time, without any change in the
original recognized state's own actual internal governance or territorial
control triggering the loss.

## Social consequence

A derecognized state retains effective control over its own territory and
population but loses the diplomatic infrastructure recognition provided —
embassies close, UN and other international-institution membership
(DIP-180) becomes contested or lost outright, and ordinary international
transactions (treaties, travel documents, diplomatic protection abroad)
become harder to conduct — a real, lived cost imposed without any military
action against the derecognized state at all.

## Regional specifics

The Taiwan case proceeds through collective, cascading multilateral
derecognition — individual states switching one by one as the rival
claimant's case becomes more diplomatically attractive, with the UN vote
itself the pivotal collective moment. The US-Cuba rupture instead shows a
single power's own unilateral bilateral derecognition, severed directly
over the recognized government's own internal political transformation
rather than in favor of any rival claimant to represent the same state — a
structurally different derecognition logic (punitive bilateral severance
versus collective shift to a competing claim) reaching a broadly similar
outcome.

## Hook

A branch: recognition, once granted (DIP-100, DIP-120), remains reversible
for as long as either a rival claimant with a competing legitimacy case
exists, or a single recognizing power judges the relationship no longer
worth maintaining — the same mechanism that grants recognition to a new or
threatening claim can later withdraw it from an existing holder, with real,
lived diplomatic-infrastructure cost to the state that loses it.
