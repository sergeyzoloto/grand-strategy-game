A new regime's internal constitutional legitimacy (LAW-180) is a separate
matter from whether other sovereign states (DIP-100) actually recognize
and treat it as legitimate — a revolution has to win this second,
diplomatic battle independently of however cleanly it won the first,
domestic one.

## Timing

American independence is declared in 1776 and gains gradual recognition
through the following decade; the French Revolution (from 1789) faces
immediate coalition warfare rather than recognition; Haiti's 1804
independence faces the most severe and prolonged non-recognition of any
case in this record, unresolved even by 1862.

## Mechanism

Existing sovereign powers (DIP-100) have every incentive to withhold
recognition from a revolutionary regime whose founding claim threatens
their own domestic order — recognizing a revolution against monarchy
legitimizes the same argument against every other monarchy, and
recognizing a revolution led by formerly enslaved people directly threatens
every other slaveholding power's own domestic institution — which is why
recognition often depends less on the new regime's actual internal
stability or effectiveness than on how dangerous its specific legitimating
claim is to other powers' own continued rule.

## Social consequence

Prolonged non-recognition imposes real, lasting costs on the new state
independent of its actual internal governance — restricted trade,
diplomatic isolation, and in Haiti's case a literal extracted payment as
the price of eventual recognition — meaning a revolution's success is
never simply a domestic political achievement but an ongoing diplomatic
project that can remain unresolved and costly for generations after the
revolution itself concludes.

## Regional specifics

Haiti's case is the starkest in this record: the only successful large-
scale revolution by enslaved people against a colonial power, met with the
most severe and sustained diplomatic punishment precisely because its
legitimating claim was the most threatening to the largest number of other
powers' own domestic order. The Meiji Restoration's opposite strategy —
claiming restored continuity rather than revolutionary rupture — sidesteps
much of this recognition problem entirely by framing the new order as
already legitimate under existing international norms rather than as a
novel claim other powers might feel threatened by.

## Hook

A revolution's diplomatic recognition battle runs on a separate track from
its domestic constitutional legitimacy (LAW-180), and other sovereign
powers (DIP-100) withhold or grant that recognition based substantially on
how threatening the new regime's founding claim is to their own order —
Haiti's decades of extracted, punitive non-recognition against the
Meiji Restoration's continuity-claim strategy showing the same underlying
mechanism cutting in opposite directions depending entirely on how the new
legitimacy claim is framed.
