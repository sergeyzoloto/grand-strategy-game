An imperial center extracts resources or obedience from subordinate
polities not through direct administration but through a diplomatic
relationship that confers or withholds legitimacy in exchange — the
specific terms of that exchange vary enormously in how much genuine mutual
benefit versus bare coercion the arrangement actually contains.

## Timing

The Achaemenid Persian tribute system organizes subject satrapies from
around -550; the Chinese tributary system develops its ritual-hierarchy
form from around -200; the Aztec Triple Alliance's tribute network expands
from its 1428 founding through the empire's 1521 collapse.

## Mechanism

Tribute diplomacy substitutes recognition and (in better-functioning
versions) genuine mutual benefit for the higher cost of direct annexation
and administration — a tributary ruler keeps local authority and internal
legitimacy in exchange for formal acknowledgment of the imperial center's
superior status and a flow of goods, labor, or military support, which is
cheaper for the empire to sustain than conquest and garrisoning but only
functions as long as the tributary continues finding the arrangement's
benefits (trade access, protection, legitimating recognition) worth more
than the cost of defection or rebellion.

## Social consequence

Where the tributary relationship is genuinely mutually legitimating (the
Chinese case, where tributary status conferred real trade privilege and
prestige), subordinate rulers have a real stake in maintaining the system;
where it functions closer to extraction under standing threat (the Aztec
case), the relationship stays inherently unstable and prone to defection
the moment the threatening power's military credibility weakens even
slightly — a structural fragility with direct consequence for how fast
each type of tribute empire tends to collapse once challenged.

## Regional specifics

The Chinese tributary system's ritual-cosmological legitimation gives it
unusual durability, since tributary states have genuine reputational and
commercial stakes in the relationship beyond fear of reprisal. The Aztec
model's much thinner legitimating logic — compliance under standing threat
of renewed conquest — helps explain how quickly subordinate city-states
defected to support the Spanish conquest once Aztec military credibility
was directly challenged. Roman client-kingdoms occupy a genuine middle
diplomatic status between these two poles and full annexation, retaining
local legitimacy while formally ceding external sovereignty.

## Hook

Tribute diplomacy trades direct annexation cost for a legitimacy-and-
recognition relationship whose durability tracks how much genuine mutual
benefit versus bare coercion it actually contains — a `protection rent`
arrangement that survives a serious military challenge to the dominant
power in direct proportion to how legitimating, rather than merely
threat-based, the underlying relationship was to begin with.
