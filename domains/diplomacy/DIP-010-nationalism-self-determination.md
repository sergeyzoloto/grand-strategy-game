This entry owns nationalism specifically as grounds for international
recognition and border legitimacy — a claim that a defined "people" is
entitled to its own sovereign state (DIP-006) — not the internal
citizenship question (LAW-002) or the ideological argument for national
identity itself (closer to PHI's territory, though touching REL-003's
mirror-image universalism question).

## Timing

Nationalist claims gain significant diplomatic traction across Europe from
the 1848 revolutions; the principle of national self-determination becomes
explicit international doctrine through Wilson's Fourteen Points and the
post-World War One settlement, continuing to drive decolonization-era
border and recognition disputes through Bangladesh's 1971 independence and
beyond.

## Mechanism

Once self-determination becomes an internationally recognized basis for
statehood, any group that can successfully frame itself as a distinct
"nation" gains a diplomatic argument for independent sovereign recognition
(DIP-006) — but the principle supplies no independent method for
determining which population groupings actually count as a nation
entitled to this treatment, which is precisely the gap that produces
violent contests (Partition) wherever multiple plausible national framings
of the same population and territory compete.

## Social consequence

Nationalist border claims convert questions of political geography into
questions of collective identity with much higher emotional and existential
stakes than an ordinary territorial dispute between states — a population
asked to accept a border that splits or merges "their nation" experiences
the outcome as touching identity itself, not merely administrative
convenience, which is part of why nationalist boundary disputes prove so
much harder to resolve through ordinary diplomatic compromise than
comparable non-nationalist territorial disagreements.

## Regional specifics

Indian independence shows self-determination functioning cleanly as an
international recognition argument while simultaneously producing
catastrophic internal violence once the same principle is applied to the
harder question of which population actually constitutes the relevant
nation entitled to Partition's resulting separate states. Post-independence
Africa's deliberate, near-universal choice to preserve colonial borders
rather than redraw them along national or ethnic lines — formalized in the
OAU's 1963 border-inviolability principle — shows the self-determination
principle isn't automatically applied even where its own logic would
seem to invite border revision, when the alternative (opening every
border to nationalist contest) is judged more dangerous than living with
admittedly arbitrary colonial lines.

## Contested

Whether nationalism identifies pre-existing natural nations or itself
substantially constructs the identities it claims only to recognize is a
foundational, unresolved dispute in nationalism studies. Model national
identity and territorial claim as mutually reinforcing rather than
assuming either is simply prior to and independent of the other — a
border can generate the shared identity of everyone now living within it
at least as much as a pre-existing identity ever justified drawing the
border there in the first place.

## Hook

Self-determination supplies a diplomatic recognition argument (feeding
DIP-006) without supplying any independent method for determining which
population groupings count as a nation — a gap that can resolve
peacefully (most postcolonial Africa's deliberate border preservation) or
catastrophically (Partition) depending entirely on whether multiple
plausible national framings of the same territory end up in direct
competition.
