The same reciprocal immunity that lets a resident ambassador function
safely (DIP-005) also makes an embassy an unusually protected base for
espionage — and expulsion, not prosecution, is the host state's only real
recourse against a diplomat caught using that protection to spy.

## Timing

Resident embassies functioning as intelligence posts are attested from
close to their earliest institutionalization in the 17th century; the
practice and its characteristic response (mass or high-profile expulsions
of diplomatic personnel identified as intelligence officers) recur
throughout the following three centuries, with Cold War-era expulsions
(through the 1980s) among the most frequent and best-documented cases.

## Mechanism

Diplomatic immunity (DIP-005) protects a resident diplomat from arrest and
prosecution under host-country criminal law, which makes formal diplomatic
status an unusually safe cover for personnel actually engaged in
intelligence-gathering rather than ordinary diplomatic business — since the
host state cannot prosecute a covered individual without breaching the
reciprocal immunity norm every one of its own diplomats abroad depends on,
its only real tool is declaring the individual persona non grata and
expelling them, a purely diplomatic (not legal) sanction that costs the
sending state a trained asset and some reputational damage without ever
technically breaching immunity itself.

## Social consequence

Because expulsion is a diplomatic rather than legal remedy, it scales:
a host state discovering a broad intelligence operation can expel dozens of
personnel simultaneously as a single, dramatic diplomatic signal, doing
real damage to the sending state's embassy staffing and information access
without any individual prosecution, trial, or the escalation risk a
criminal case against a foreign diplomat would otherwise carry.

## Regional specifics

Cold War-era diplomatic expulsions between the United States, the Soviet
Union, and their respective allies become a recurring, almost ritualized
diplomatic signal — a large expulsion functioning as a calibrated,
below-the-threshold-of-war response to an intelligence provocation,
distinct from both DIP-012's economic sanctions and any direct military
response, occupying its own rung on the escalation ladder.

## Hook

A modifier/branch: converts diplomatic immunity (DIP-005) into a
double-edged capability — real intelligence access, at the cost of a
standing expulsion risk that escalates in direct proportion to how overtly
the cover is abused, with expulsion functioning as a real diplomatic
sanction that costs the sending state an asset without ever legally
breaching the immunity norm both sides continue to depend on.
