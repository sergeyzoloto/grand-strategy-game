A form of diplomatic coercion that withholds trade, finance, or
diplomatic contact (extending COM-190's tariff-as-policy-tool logic into
an explicitly punitive register) as an alternative to military action —
cheaper and less escalatory than war, but correspondingly less certain to
actually change the targeted behavior.

## Timing

League of Nations sanctions against Italy over the invasion of Ethiopia
(1935) is an early and largely ineffective case; comprehensive sanctions
against apartheid South Africa build cumulatively from the early 1960s
through the regime's early-1990s end; narrowly targeted sanctions (Cuba's
long-standing embargo, later Iran-specific measures) continue and evolve
through the 1990s and beyond.

## Mechanism

Sanctions work by imposing enough economic cost on the target that
continuing the sanctioned behavior becomes less attractive than changing
it — but this depends entirely on how comprehensively the sanctions are
actually enforced (a sanctions regime full of loopholes or lacking
multilateral participation imposes far less real cost) and on whether the
target's own leadership bears the cost directly or can shift it onto the
general population while remaining personally insulated, which is
precisely the mechanism critics point to when sanctions fail to change
the targeted government's actual behavior.

## Social consequence

Comprehensive sanctions frequently impose their heaviest cost on a target
country's ordinary population — reduced access to goods, medicine, and
economic opportunity — while a well-entrenched leadership can often
successfully redirect blame for resulting hardship onto the sanctioning
powers rather than onto their own sanctioned behavior, which is part of
why sustained comprehensive sanctions can sometimes strengthen rather than
weaken a targeted regime's domestic political position.

## Regional specifics

The gradual, multi-channel apartheid sanctions campaign — building over
three decades through trade, financial, sporting, and cultural pressure
simultaneously — illustrates comprehensive sanctions working as intended
when broadly multilateral and sustained long enough, eventually
contributing to real internal pressure for change. Iran-style targeted
sanctions aimed narrowly at a specific program rather than comprehensive
regime pressure show sanctions can instead function as a precise policy
lever, trading some of comprehensive sanctions' broader coercive power for
a more clearly defined, negotiable objective.

## Contested

Whether sanctions reliably achieve their stated objectives or primarily
impose humanitarian cost on a general population while insulated
leadership remains unmoved or even strengthened is disputed, with mixed
historical evidence on both sides. Model sanctions effectiveness as
depending heavily on design (comprehensive versus narrowly targeted) and
on multilateral versus unilateral application, rather than assuming any
sanctions regime reliably produces its intended political outcome
regardless of how it's actually structured.

## Hook

Sanctions extend tariff-as-policy-tool logic (COM-190) into explicitly
punitive diplomatic coercion, with actual effectiveness depending on
enforcement comprehensiveness and multilateral participation rather than
on the sanctions being imposed at all — a lever whose humanitarian cost
can fall on a general population while a well-insulated leadership absorbs
comparatively little of the actual pressure.
