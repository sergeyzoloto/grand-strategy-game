Territorial sovereignty and legal jurisdiction are normally assumed to
coincide automatically — whoever controls the territory makes the law
governing everyone in it — but a treaty can explicitly decouple them,
leaving a foreign power's own resident citizens answerable only to their
own consul's law.

## Timing

Ottoman capitulations grant extraterritorial legal privilege to resident
European merchants from as early as 1536; the practice reaches its most
consequential and coercively-imposed form in the East Asian treaty-port
system following the 1842-43 Treaty of Nanking, persisting in China until
its formal renunciation during the Second World War in 1943.

## Mechanism

An extraterritoriality clause establishes that a foreign power's own
citizens, resident within the host state's territory, remain subject to
their own consul's court and law rather than the host state's — a consular
court hears the case, applies the foreign power's own legal code, and the
host state's own courts and law simply don't apply to the covered
population at all, converting legal jurisdiction from an automatic
consequence of physical presence within a territory into an explicitly
negotiated, and asymmetrically applied, diplomatic variable.

## Social consequence

Because extraterritorial jurisdiction applies only to the foreign power's
own nationals, it creates a visible, legally codified dual-status
population within the host state's own territory — foreign residents
answerable to a different law and different courts than the host state's
own citizens face for the same conduct in the same place, a durable and
visible source of resentment distinct from the underlying trade or
territorial terms of whatever treaty established it.

## Regional specifics

The Ottoman capitulations originate as a unilateral grant extended to
encourage foreign trade, not imposed under military coercion — a
structurally different origin from the East Asian treaty-port case, where
extraterritoriality is a direct, coerced term of a treaty imposed after
military defeat (the Opium Wars, already covered for their commercial
dimension in COM-022). Both cases converge on the same underlying
jurisdictional mechanism despite arising from opposite starting positions
— one a grant of favor later resented, the other coercion resented from
the outset.

## Hook

A modifier: territorial sovereignty (DIP-006) and legal jurisdiction,
normally assumed to coincide automatically, can be explicitly and
asymmetrically decoupled by treaty — a durable source of resentment and a
frequent, specific target for later renegotiation once the power imbalance
that produced the original carve-out narrows.
