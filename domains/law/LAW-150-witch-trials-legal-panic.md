The same procedural machinery legitimate justice depends on — courts, evidence rules, confession-extraction — can be captured by a moral panic and turned toward systematically producing false convictions, showing legal procedure's formal legitimacy and its actual truth-finding accuracy are separable and can diverge sharply without the underlying law's text ever changing.

## Timing

Systematic, court-prosecuted witch panics scale across parts of Europe from around 1450, peaking in intensity through the 1600s; the Salem trials (1692) are a smaller but exceptionally well-documented late instance; formal prosecution largely ends by the mid-1700s as evidentiary standards tighten and elite skepticism grows.

## Mechanism

A witch trial uses the same courts, procedures, and (per LAW-030) confession-extraction techniques ordinary criminal adjudication relies on, but under a moral panic these institutions' evidentiary standards collapse: testimony that would never suffice to convict for an ordinary crime — spectral evidence, coerced confession under torture, the accusation of a socially marginal target — becomes accepted as sufficient, showing the same procedural machinery can produce systematically false outcomes purely by lowering what counts as adequate proof, without any change to the underlying law being prosecuted.

## Social consequence

Because the panic operates through legitimate-looking legal procedure rather than through extralegal violence, it draws in local elites, clergy, and neighbors as active participants (accusers, witnesses, judges) rather than requiring a small group of dedicated persecutors — implicating an entire community's ordinary legal and religious institutions in producing an outcome those same institutions typically repudiate once the panic subsides, as Massachusetts's own colonial authorities did after Salem.

## Regional specifics

Salem's exceptionally well-documented case shows the evidentiary collapse mechanism with unusual clarity — spectral evidence explicitly accepted, then explicitly repudiated by name once the panic ended. Comparable Islamic legal practice's general absence of large-scale, formally-prosecuted witch panics despite widespread belief in supernatural harm shows this specific institutional capture isn't automatic wherever the underlying belief exists — the surrounding legal and religious institutional context determines whether that belief translates into legal panic or not.

## Contested

How much the phenomenon reflects popular belief driving prosecution from below versus elite and clerical direction from above is debated, with the balance appearing to vary by region and specific panic. Model the trigger and intensity of a given panic as a variable mix of both drivers rather than committing to one as the universal primary cause.

## Hook

A branch: the same courts and evidentiary standards (LAW-030) can produce wildly different outcomes depending on surrounding social pressure rather than on the underlying law's text changing at all — legal procedure's formal legitimacy and its actual truth-finding accuracy are separable, and can diverge catastrophically under panic before institutions repudiate their own prior verdicts.
