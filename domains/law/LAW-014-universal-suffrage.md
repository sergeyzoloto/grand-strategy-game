This entry owns the specific historical process of removing property,
gender, and racial restrictions from the narrower citizenship-linked
voting models LAW-002 covers — extending the franchise from a privileged
subset of citizens to the entire adult citizenry.

## Timing

Property-qualification removal and gradual suffrage expansion proceed
unevenly across the 1800s in several states; women's suffrage extends
through the following decades; India's 1950 constitution grants universal
adult suffrage immediately upon independence, while the U.S. Voting Rights
Act (1965) addresses persistent practical suppression of a right nominally
granted a century earlier.

## Mechanism

Removing a suffrage restriction (property, gender, race) is a legal act,
but whether the newly enfranchised population can actually exercise the
right depends on separate enforcement mechanisms entirely — a formally
universal franchise can still be effectively restricted through
administrative barriers, local intimidation, or simple non-enforcement,
which is precisely the gap between LAW-002's citizenship status and this
entry's actual exercised voting right that the U.S. case exposes so
starkly.

## Social consequence

Suffrage expansion changes which population a government's decisions have
to answer to at the ballot box, and the specific sequence and pace of that
expansion (property first, then gender, then race, in most gradual cases)
reflects which excluded group had enough organized political leverage to
force its own inclusion first — the order of expansion is itself a
legible record of relative political power among excluded groups at each
stage.

## Regional specifics

India's immediate, constitutionally-mandated universal adult suffrage
upon independence compresses into a single act what most other cases in
this record spread across decades of incremental, contested expansion —
a case where the sequencing problem other suffrage histories work through
gradually simply doesn't arise because the founding constitutional moment
settles it all at once. The United States' need for a second major
legislative intervention a full century after the first formal grant
shows a legal right and its actual practical exercise can diverge for
generations when enforcement mechanisms are weak or actively undermined at
the local level.

## Hook

Removing a formal suffrage restriction and actually securing the exercised
right are separable achievements, with the gap between them capable of
persisting for a century (the American case) or effectively closing at a
single constitutional moment (the Indian case) — the sequence and pace of
expansion elsewhere is itself a legible record of which excluded group had
enough organized leverage to force inclusion first.
