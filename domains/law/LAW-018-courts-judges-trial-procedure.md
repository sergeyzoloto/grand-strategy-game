The actual institution and procedure by which a specific dispute or accusation gets adjudicated once law exists — distinct from LAW-001's codification of what the rules are and LAW-012's enforcement of order in the streets, since this is about how a case that has already reached a decision-maker actually gets decided.

## Timing

Formal court institutions and defined trial procedure are attested alongside the earliest written codes (LAW-001) from roughly -1754 onward; the Islamic qadi system matures from the 700s; English jury trial develops from 1215 as a direct replacement for trial by ordeal; procedural traditions across these regions continue developing and diverging through 1800.

## Mechanism

Once law exists, a specific dispute or accusation still has to be resolved by someone, following some procedure, weighing some form of evidence — and this can be organized in genuinely different ways with real consequences for outcomes: a single professionally-trained judge deciding alone, a panel of ordinary laypeople (a jury) rendering a verdict, or a magistrate who investigates as well as judges (inquisitorial) rather than only weighing arguments two opposing advocates present (adversarial). None of these procedural choices are dictated by the underlying substantive law itself — the same written code could in principle be administered under any of them.

## Social consequence

Trial procedure shapes who has power over an outcome independent of what the law itself says: a jury system distributes that power across a panel of ordinary community members rather than concentrating it in a single trained professional, and an inquisitorial system concentrates investigative as well as judicial authority in one official rather than splitting it between an accuser and a neutral arbiter — each choice creates a different kind of vulnerability (jury bias and susceptibility to community prejudice; a single inquisitorial official's own potential bias going unchecked by any opposing advocate).

## Regional specifics

English jury trial's direct origin as a replacement for trial by ordeal, forced by the Fourth Lateran Council's 1215 withdrawal of clerical participation, is a clean case of an external shock to one procedure producing durable innovation in another rather than jury trial being independently invented on its own merits. The Islamic qadi system and the Chinese magistrate system both organize as inquisitorial models but through different institutional logics — a religiously-trained jurist applying fiqh doctrine in the qadi case, a unified local administrative office combining multiple governmental functions in the Chinese case — showing "inquisitorial" itself isn't one single institutional form.

## Hook

A gate every other entry in this domain assumes exists once law is written and enforcement occurs: a real institutional choice (jury versus judge-only, adversarial versus inquisitorial) shapes case outcomes independent of what the underlying law's text actually says, and that choice can itself be forced by an external shock removing a prior procedure, as the English case shows.
