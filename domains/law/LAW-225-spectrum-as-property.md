A genuinely new legal object rather than an extension of an existing one.
Property law had always attached to things that can be held against others;
this attaches to a band of frequencies that nobody can hold and nobody can
fence, and it is manufactured specifically because the physics leaves no
other way to make the resource usable.

## Timing

From the Berlin radiotelegraph convention of 1906, through the United
States Radio Act of 1912 and its failure, to the Radio Act of 1927, the
Washington conference of the same year, and the establishment of the FCC in
1934 — three decades in which a physical nuisance problem was converted
into a durable category of regulated right.

## Mechanism

The electromagnetic spectrum is not scarce in the way land is, and this is
the whole of the problem. Land is rivalrous because occupying a field
excludes others from that field, and the property right recognises a
physical fact that already exists. Spectrum is different. **Two
transmitters on one frequency do not divide a resource between them — they
destroy each other's signal**, so the failure mode is not congestion but
mutual annihilation, and the outcome of unregulated use is not an
inefficient allocation but no communication at all for anyone. Nor can the
resource be fenced. A transmitter cannot direct its output only to people
entitled to receive it, and a receiver cannot be excluded by any physical
means from a signal reaching it.

So the right has to be manufactured, and what is manufactured is not
ownership of a thing but **a licence to be the only one transmitting in a
band, in a place, at a power** — a state-created monopoly on an activity,
defined negatively as everyone else's duty not to transmit. That is a
different legal animal from land tenure, chattel property, or the
intellectual-property rights LAW handles elsewhere, and it earns an entry
rather than a paragraph because the object did not exist before it was
invented and every subsequent allocated-spectrum fight inherits its shape.

The American sequence is the mechanism's best demonstration and should be
modelled as a branch rather than a story. The 1912 Act required a licence
but gave the granting authority no power to refuse one, which is a licensing
regime in form and none in substance; interference rose until broadcasting
became unusable across large parts of the band, and the 1927 Act followed.
**A right to exclude that cannot actually exclude produces the unregulated
outcome exactly**, which generalises well beyond radio and is the reason
this entry sits in LAW rather than in ENG.

Allocation also cannot be settled inside one jurisdiction, because the
resource crosses borders without transit and a domestic grant is worthless
if a neighbour's transmitter overrides it. This is what drives the entry's
diplomatic half — but the treaty work implements an allocation whose legal
character was decided domestically, which is why the entry is canonically
LAW with `diplomacy` touched rather than the reverse.

## Social consequence

Licensing decides who may speak at scale, and it does so through an
administrative process rather than a market or an election. Whoever
allocates spectrum selects the broadcasters, and every subsequent argument
about broadcast content runs through that fact — a state that need not
censor because it simply declines to renew a licence has a far cheaper
instrument than censorship, and one that is much harder to characterise as
suppression. The licence renewal is the control point, not the transmitter.

The regimes also diverge early and durably in a way that shapes what
broadcasting became. Where spectrum was allocated to private claimants,
licences acquired capital value and a broadcast industry with property
interests formed around them. Where transmission was treated as a state
prerogative extended by permission, no such interest ever formed, and the
question of who owns the airwaves was never asked because the answer was
assumed. Both arrangements persist for decades after the technical
conditions that produced them changed.

## Regional specifics

The legal object appears where private claimants existed to contest it and
essentially nowhere else, which is the finding worth carrying. The United
States had a large amateur and commercial population, hit the interference
problem first, litigated it hardest, and produced the template. European
states routed the same problem through post and telegraph ministries and
produced a handful of licensees instead of a market. Japan resolved it by
asserting the right had never left the state — not an allocation at all but
a denial that allocation was the question.

Colonial Africa and South Asia are the instructive absences. Spectrum there
was allocated by the metropolitan power as part of the imperial settlement,
so no domestic legal object was created and no domestic claimant existed to
create one. The effect outlives the colonial period, because post-
independence states inherited allocations made elsewhere under arrangements
they were never party to — a concrete instance of sovereignty arriving
already encumbered, and a better mechanic than a generic penalty because
the encumbrance is specific, documented, and long-lived.

## Hook

Manufactures an excludable right in a physically non-excludable medium, and
should behave unlike every other property mechanic in the record. It is
created to solve mutual destruction rather than congestion, so unregulated
use produces zero output for everyone rather than an inefficient split. The
right is purely negative — a licence is everyone else's duty not to
transmit — which means it is worth exactly as much as the granting
authority's capacity to refuse and enforce, and a licensing regime with no
power to say no yields the unregulated outcome precisely, not a weakened
version of it. Allocation is an administrative choice of who may speak at
scale, giving whoever controls renewal a censorship instrument that costs
nothing and does not look like censorship. And because signals cross borders
without transit, a purely domestic grant is void against a neighbour's
transmitter, forcing international coordination as a condition of the right
functioning at all.
