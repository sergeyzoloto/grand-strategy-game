The state asserting a power to break apart a private structure that has
broken no other law. Everything the combination did was lawful when it
did it — buying rivals, negotiating freight rates, building pipelines —
and the offence is the resulting shape. That is a new legal object, and
the entry covers both the shape and the instrument built to attack it.

## Timing

From the Sherman Act of 1890, through two decades in which courts supplied
the content the statute lacked, to the Standard Oil dissolution of 1911
and the rule of reason it produced.

## Mechanism

**First, what was built.** Vertical integration is a solution to
counterparty dependence. A refiner buying crude from producers, shipping
on someone else's railroad, and selling through independent distributors
is exposed at every join — each counterparty can hold up the chain at the
moment of maximum leverage, and each join is a price negotiated by
someone whose interest is opposed. Owning the pipeline, the refinery, the
tank car, and the distribution removes every one of those negotiations
and replaces them with an internal transfer. The firm stops buying
transactions and starts owning the capability to perform them.

The advantage is not primarily scale. It is **the elimination of the
hold-up problem**, and it is largest exactly where assets are specific
and counterparties few — which is why it appeared in oil first, since a
pipeline runs from one field to one refinery and is worth nothing
otherwise. The same logic produces the rebate and the freight
arrangement: a firm shipping enough volume can extract terms no
competitor can match, and that advantage compounds because the terms
improve as the volume grows.

**Second, why it is hard to attack legally.** Each step is an ordinary
lawful act. The firm bought willing sellers, negotiated rates railroads
were free to offer, and built its own infrastructure. There is no moment
at which a recognisable wrong occurs, which means an existing body of law
about fraud, contract, or property cannot reach the outcome. **The
prohibition has to attach to a structure rather than to an act**, and
that is what makes antitrust a new legal object rather than an extension
of anything. It is also what made the Sherman Act nearly content-free at
enactment — a legislature can identify the problem without being able to
state the rule, so it prohibits restraint of trade and leaves courts to
discover what that means.

**Third, the rule the courts settled on.** Standard Oil in 1911 produced
dissolution and the rule of reason together: size is not itself the
offence, conduct is. That choice made the doctrine behavioural rather
than structural, and it decided a century of enforcement — a firm may
become large by being better, and may not become large by particular
means. The line is genuinely hard to draw and has been redrawn
repeatedly.

**Fourth, the remedy's limits.** Breaking a firm reliably changes how
many firms exist. Whether it changes market outcomes depends on **whether
the advantage was the combination or something the combination merely
held** — a resource position, a transport advantage, a technical
capability — because those survive a breakup intact and reassemble under
new names. Standard Oil's successors remained the largest firms in the
industry and went on to dominate international oil, which is the
strongest evidence for the sceptical reading.

## Social consequence

Antitrust establishes that private economic power is a public question,
and that is a larger constitutional development than its commercial
content suggests. Before it, a firm's internal arrangements were its own
business under property and contract; after it, scale itself becomes
something a government may have views about. The same logic later reaches
labour, banking, and media, and the argument recurs in every generation
with a new industry attached.

It also creates a permanent class of specialist — the regulatory lawyer
and the economist testifying about market definition — because a rule
that turns on conduct rather than size requires continuous expert
adjudication of what conduct means. The cost of compliance becomes a real
barrier in itself, and one that favours firms large enough to carry legal
departments, which is an outcome the doctrine did not intend.

## Regional specifics

The regional divergence is the most useful content here, because the same
arrangement gets three different legal readings simultaneously. American
law treated combination as a conspiracy to be broken. German law enforced
cartel agreements as valid contracts into the 1920s, treating them as
instruments of industrial order. Japanese policy actively built the
zaibatsu, on the reasoning that a state industrialising fast from a small
capital base needs concentrated capital and an in-house bank rather than
a contestable market. Three jurisdictions, one commercial form, and three
incompatible verdicts — which establishes that antitrust is a political
choice about market structure rather than a technical finding about
efficiency.

The colonial absences make the complementary point. In South Asia the
managing agencies concentrated sectoral control through interlocking
directorships with no doctrine available to challenge them; in Africa the
concession company held monopoly as the administering power's own chosen
instrument. Where monopoly is the government's method, no legal challenge
to it is conceptually available, and the structures outlast the
governments.

## Contested

Whether the dissolution accomplished anything is disputed on the
evidence. Standard Oil's market share was already declining under
competitive pressure before the suit concluded, its efficiencies had
lowered kerosene prices substantially over the preceding decades, and its
successors remained dominant for generations. The traditional reading
takes 1911 as the moment private economic power became answerable to
public law; the revisionist reading takes it as a remedy that relocated
power without reducing it.

Model the structural remedy as reliably changing the number and identity
of firms and as changing market outcomes only **conditionally on the
source of the advantage** — combination-derived advantages dissolve with
the combination, while resource, transport, and technical advantages
survive it and reassemble. Whether the conduct-not-size rule is correct
at all is separately and continuously argued, and has been reopened in
every era that produced a new dominant industry.

## Hook

Creates a prohibition that attaches to a structure rather than to an act,
which no earlier legal object in this record does — every step in
building the combination is lawful and the resulting shape is not, so the
rule cannot be built from fraud, contract, or property. Vertical
integration should be modelled as an escape from counterparty hold-up
rather than as simple scale, so it pays most where assets are specific
and counterparties few, and it converts purchased transactions into owned
capability. The state gains a power to dissolve private structures, but
the remedy should be conditional rather than reliable — breaking a firm
always changes how many firms exist, and changes outcomes only when the
advantage was the combination itself rather than a resource position or
technical capability that survives intact and reassembles. And because
the doctrine is a political choice rather than an economic finding, the
same arrangement should be prosecutable in one polity, an enforceable
contract in a second, and deliberate state policy in a third.
