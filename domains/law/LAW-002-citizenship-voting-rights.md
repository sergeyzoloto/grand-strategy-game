Citizenship defines who counts as a full legal member of a polity with
standing to participate in its decisions — a status that has meant wildly
different things, from a narrow participatory privilege to an
administrative tax category to a protected but politically excluded
intermediate status.

## Timing

Solon's reforms establishing graduated Athenian citizen participation date
to -594; Roman citizenship's later imperial-scale extension (the Antonine
Constitution) dates to 212; Islamic dhimmi status as a formalized legal
category develops from the earliest Islamic conquests and persists as a
durable institution for centuries after.

## Mechanism

Every citizenship system defines a boundary between full members (with
standing to vote, hold office, or claim full legal protection) and
everyone else, and where that boundary sits determines who a state's
decisions actually answer to — a narrow citizenship base (Athens's,
excluding women, slaves, and resident foreigners) concentrates political
accountability tightly, while a broad one (Rome's post-212 extension)
trades participatory intensity for administrative reach across a much
larger and more diverse population.

## Social consequence

Where citizenship is narrow, the excluded majority lives under laws they
have no formal voice in shaping, a tension every one of these systems
manages differently — Rome's answer is to make citizenship itself
expandable and eventually nearly universal within the empire, while
Islamic dhimmi status instead creates a stable, rights-bearing but
permanently non-participatory category rather than a path toward eventual
inclusion.

## Regional specifics

The Antonine Constitution's wholesale citizenship extension converts
Roman citizenship from ancestral privilege into an administrative and
fiscal category serving imperial governance needs, a functionally
different institution from Athens's deliberately narrow participatory
design despite sharing the same word. Islamic dhimmi status formalizes a
durable middle category — protected, taxed, legally secure, but
politically excluded — that neither the Athenian all-or-nothing model nor
Rome's expanding-inclusion model has a direct equivalent for, showing
citizenship's core exclusion/inclusion boundary can itself be subdivided
into more than two tiers.

## Hook

Where a polity draws its citizenship boundary determines who its formal
decisions must answer to — and that boundary can expand toward
administrative universality (Rome), stay narrow and participatory
(Athens), or formalize a stable, rights-bearing but permanently
non-participatory middle tier (Islamic dhimmi status) as three genuinely
different long-term institutional strategies, not points on one single
inclusion timeline.
