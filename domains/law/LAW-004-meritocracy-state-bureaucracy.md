Selecting state officials through standardized competitive testing rather
than birth, patronage, or purchase — converting administrative capacity
into a function of a state's ability to identify and credential talent
rather than of who a candidate's family already knows.

## Timing

The Sui dynasty establishes China's imperial examination system in 605,
maturing and expanding across subsequent dynasties for well over a
millennium; Britain's Northcote-Trevelyan reforms, explicitly modeled on
the Chinese system, only introduce comparable competitive civil-service
examination in 1854.

## Mechanism

A competitive examination system converts bureaucratic recruitment from a
patronage or hereditary-privilege problem into a credentialing problem —
in principle, any sufficiently prepared candidate regardless of birth can
qualify, which both widens the pool of available administrative talent
beyond a narrow hereditary elite and gives the state a legible, comparable
standard for evaluating candidates at a scale no personal-network-based
system can match.

## Social consequence

Where the examination system is genuinely open (even if preparation itself
requires resources unequally available), it creates a route to elite
status through scholarly achievement rather than birth alone, producing a
distinct, credential-holding scholar-official class whose social position
depends on maintained institutional standing rather than inherited land or
title — a different elite-formation mechanism than the hereditary
aristocracy feudalism (LAW-005) produces.

## Regional specifics

China's system predates the British reform explicitly modeled on it by
well over a millennium, one of the clearest documented cases in this
record of a foundational modern administrative institution's usual Western
attribution understating a much earlier, sustained non-Western achievement
that later gets directly and openly borrowed rather than independently
reinvented. Mughal India's reliance on patronage networks and hereditary
service families instead achieves administrative capacity through
personal-relationship-based recruitment, showing competitive examination
is one viable state-capacity strategy among several rather than the only
route to effective bureaucratic administration.

## Hook

Competitive examination converts administrative recruitment into a
credentialing problem rather than a patronage one, widening the available
talent pool and raising a state's `legibility` over its own administrative
capacity — a system Britain explicitly and openly borrowed from China over
a millennium after its founding, rather than an independent Western
invention later exported elsewhere.
