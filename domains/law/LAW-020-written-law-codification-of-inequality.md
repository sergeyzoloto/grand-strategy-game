Writing law down converts custom and ruler's-whim justice into a fixed,
public, and — crucially — citable standard, which can either entrench
existing hierarchy with new permanence or, depending on who forces the
writing to happen, constrain the powerful for the first time.

## Timing

The Code of Hammurabi (-1754) is among the earliest comprehensive written
legal codes; the Manusmriti's codification of caste obligation dates to
around -200; Qin Legalist codification runs from around -350; Rome's
Twelve Tables date to -450.

## Mechanism

An unwritten legal system leaves interpretation and application entirely
in the hands of whoever currently holds authority, which written
codification fixes into a stable, citable reference — the same underlying
mechanism (making obligation and punishment predictable and checkable) can
serve opposite political purposes depending on who controls the writing
process: a ruler codifying existing custom entrenches it with new
permanence, while a subordinate group forcing codification (as with Rome's
plebeians) constrains previously arbitrary elite discretion instead.

## Social consequence

Once law is written and public, a subject can at least in principle know
in advance what a given action costs under the law, converting the
relationship between ruler and ruled from personal discretion into
something closer to a fixed, if often deeply unequal, contract — the
Manusmriti's caste-differentiated penalties make that inequality explicit
and permanent rather than leaving status distinctions to unwritten local
custom that might vary or soften over time.

## Regional specifics

The Manusmriti's explicit, systematic encoding of caste-based legal
differentiation is among the most thoroughgoing hereditary-status
codifications in this record. Qin Legalism inverts the usual pattern
entirely, using uniform codified law specifically to dismantle aristocratic
privilege rather than entrench it — codification serving centralizing state
power against existing elites rather than serving elites against everyone
else. Rome's Twelve Tables stand out as codification extracted by
political pressure from below specifically to end elite control over
secret legal knowledge, a third distinct political function for the same
basic mechanism.

## Hook

Written codification fixes legal obligation into a stable, `legibility`-
raising public reference — but whether that fixing entrenches existing
hierarchy, imposes a new one, or actually constrains previously arbitrary
elite power depends entirely on who controls the writing process, not on
codification itself carrying any inherent political direction.
