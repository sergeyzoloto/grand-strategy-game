A monarch needing revenue beyond what feudal obligation (LAW-080) or
direct administration reliably provides has to bargain for it — and the
bargaining itself, repeated over generations, hardens into a standing
representative institution with its own claim to ongoing consultation.

## Timing

The Leon Cortes (1188) is among the earliest documented cases; comparable
assemblies develop across European kingdoms over the following centuries
(the English Parliament, the French Estates-General, among several
others), with the pattern persisting in some form through the French
Revolution's abolition of the Estates-General's old structure in 1789.

## Mechanism

A ruler who needs to raise taxation beyond customary levels, especially
for an expensive war, has strong incentive to secure the affected estates'
active consent rather than simply imposing the new burden and risking
revolt or noncompliance — repeated bargaining sessions of this kind
gradually establish precedent and expectation that certain kinds of
taxation require the assembly's consent at all, converting what starts as
situational negotiation into something closer to an ongoing constitutional
constraint on the crown's fiscal power.

## Social consequence

Estates that win a durable claim to tax consent gain a standing lever over
royal policy well beyond taxation itself, since a ruler needing future
revenue has to keep the relationship functional — this is the specific
mechanism by which several European monarchies gradually accumulate
constitutional constraints on royal authority centuries before anything
resembling a written constitution (LAW-180) formalizes the relationship.

## Regional specifics

This is a genuinely Europe-specific institutional form in this era — the
Cortes-Estates-Parliament family of assemblies, organized around distinct
legal estates bargaining collectively over taxation, doesn't have a close
structural equivalent in contemporary East Asian fiscal administration,
which instead routes revenue extraction through the centralized
bureaucratic channels (LAW-100) or feudal obligation (LAW-080) already
covered in this domain, without an intervening representative body's
separate consent required at all.

## Hook

Repeated tax-consent bargaining between a revenue-seeking ruler and
organized estates gradually hardens from situational negotiation into a
standing constitutional constraint and a raised baseline of fiscal
`legibility` — the specific, slow mechanism by which several European
monarchies accumulate real limits on royal power well before any written
constitution formalizes the relationship.
