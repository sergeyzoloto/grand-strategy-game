This entry owns the general medieval legal innovation of treating an
organization itself — not any specific member — as a rights-bearing legal
person that can own property, sue and be sued, and persist across
generations; `FIN-006` owns the later, more specific commercial application
of the same underlying concept to the joint-stock company.

## Timing

The University of Bologna (founded 1088) is among the earliest bodies
recognized under the Latin universitas concept — a formally chartered
"whole" treated as one legal person distinct from its individual current
members; the same legal category extends over the following centuries to
cover cities, cathedral chapters, guilds, and eventually, by 1600, the
early chartered trading companies (COM-016, FIN-006).

## Mechanism

Without a legal-personhood concept, an organization's property and
obligations are really just its current members' personal property and
obligations, which creates an obvious continuity problem: what happens to
the university's endowment or the city's debts when this year's members
die or move on? Universitas status solves this by legally separating the
organization's rights and obligations from any specific member's own,
letting the entity itself own property, incur debt, and be sued — a
status that persists automatically across membership turnover rather than
requiring re-establishment every generation.

## Social consequence

Corporate legal personhood lets an institution accumulate property,
reputation, and legal standing across centuries in a way no individual's
lifespan could sustain — a university, cathedral, or chartered city can
own land, defend its privileges in court, and enter binding agreements as
itself, converting institutional continuity from a matter of continuous
individual succession into a matter of legal category that survives its
members automatically.

## Regional specifics

The Islamic waqf achieves a functionally near-identical solution —
perpetual property-holding independent of any individual's life or heirs
— considerably earlier and through an independent religious-endowment
legal mechanism rather than the Latin corporate-personhood category,
solving the same underlying continuity problem the universitas concept
later formalizes in Europe. Chinese legal tradition's reliance on custom,
trust, and clan-based structures rather than a comparably broad formal
legal-personhood category for organizations shows the continuity problem
this entry describes can be managed through social and customary
mechanisms without ever formalizing the organization itself as a distinct
legal person.

## Hook

Treating an organization itself, rather than its current members, as a
persistent legal person solves the continuity problem across membership
turnover — a general medieval legal innovation `FIN-006`'s joint-stock
company later specializes for commercial capital-raising specifically,
while the Islamic waqf reaches a functionally comparable solution earlier
through an entirely separate religious-endowment mechanism.
