A single written document formalizing the accumulated bargains of
estate-consent (LAW-006) and representative practice (LAW-010) into one
codified structure — explicit rules for how executive, legislative, and
judicial power are divided and check each other, rather than leaving the
balance to accumulated precedent and custom.

## Timing

The United States Constitution (1789) and its explicit separation-of-
powers design is an early and influential codification; the pattern
spreads and adapts internationally over the following century, including
Japan's Meiji Constitution (1889) and the Ottoman Empire's short-lived 1876
constitutional experiment.

## Mechanism

Writing the division of power down explicitly, rather than leaving it to
accumulated custom and precedent the way England's unwritten constitutional
practice does, makes the intended balance of power citable and, in
principle, judicially enforceable — but a written text is only as durable
as the institutions willing and able to actually enforce it against a
executive determined to violate or suspend it, which is precisely the gap
the Ottoman case exposes.

## Social consequence

A codified separation of powers gives each branch a citable textual claim
to its own authority independent of the others, creating institutional
actors (courts especially) with standing to resist encroachment in a way
that's much harder to establish under a system relying purely on
unwritten custom and mutual restraint — but that citable claim only
functions as real constraint where the relevant institutions also have
the practical capacity and will to enforce it.

## Regional specifics

Japan's Meiji Constitution deliberately hybridizes Western constitutional
form (separated functions, an elected Diet) with explicit preservation of
imperial sovereignty, a genuinely original synthesis rather than either a
faithful copy of a Western model or a purely traditional structure with
constitutional language added on top. The Ottoman 1876 constitution's
formal establishment of parliamentary limits on the sultan, followed by
the sultan's own suspension of that same constitution within two years,
is one of the clearest cases in this record of a written constitutional
text failing to constrain power because the institutions needed to enforce
it against a determined executive simply weren't strong enough yet.

## Hook

A written constitution converts accumulated estate-bargaining (LAW-006)
and representative practice (LAW-010) into an explicit, citable division
of power — but the text's actual durability depends entirely on whether
supporting institutions have the real capacity to enforce it against a
determined executive, which the Ottoman case shows can fail even with a
formally complete constitutional document already in place.
