A colonial state runs two legal orders at once, and the split is the point:
metropolitan law for settlers and citizens, and a codified, frozen version of
"customary" or religious law for the governed. It is cheaper than imposing one system,
it co-opts local authorities by giving them jurisdiction, and it produces a population
defined in law as different in kind rather than merely as subject.

## Timing

From the Spanish two-republic design of the sixteenth century and the Ottoman millet
order already running beside it, through the British codification of Hindu and Muslim
law in India from 1772, the Netherlands Indies' legal classes from 1854, the French
indigénat from 1881, British indirect rule and Japanese colonial customary law from the
1900s, to decolonisation, largely complete by 1975. The systems it built persist long
past that date.

## Mechanism

**Two orders are cheaper than one.** Imposing metropolitan law on a large governed
population means building courts, training judges and enforcing rules the population
does not recognise. Leaving the governed under their own law, administered through
their own authorities, costs far less. The colonial state needs to rule directly only
settlers, commerce and the matters it cares about most: land, revenue, public order.
Local rulers who receive jurisdiction over their people's family, land and minor
disputes gain a stake in the colonial order, which is co-optation paid in authority
rather than money. The price is that the governed are marked in law as a separate kind
of person — subjects under custom rather than citizens under law. Mamdani calls this
the bifurcated state.

**Codification freezes.** Customary law before colonial rule was flexible, locally
variable and continuously renegotiated. Different villages, lineages and judges applied
it differently, and it changed as circumstances did. An outside administrator who wanted
to apply it had to write it down, and did so by interviewing whichever authorities were
available: pandits and maulvis in Bengal, chiefs and elders in Africa, adat experts in
the Indies, old-customs informants in Taiwan. The written version became fixed, uniform
and authoritative in a way the practice never was. It also reflected what the informants
wanted it to say, which in patriarchal and gerontocratic settings meant senior men's
interests over women's and juniors'. Anglo-Hindu and Anglo-Muhammadan law, the adat law
circles and African customary law under indirect rule were each partly recorded and
partly invented. LAW-040 carries the personal-status categories this produced; this
entry carries the wider fact that codification converted living practice into state law
that could no longer adapt on its own.

**Dualism is also legibility.** A codified customary law is one the colonial state can
read. It turns the governed population's disputes, family arrangements and land tenure
into categories an administrator can apply and a court can rule on. The second track
raises `legibility` over a population the state had no intention of making equal.

**Forum shopping converts wealth into jurisdiction.** Two systems mean litigants choose,
and the choice is unequal. A party who can afford lawyers and the fees of the
metropolitan courts can take a dispute there, while a poorer party is confined to the
customary forum or has no choice at all. Indigenous communities in Mexico, merchants in
India, and women seeking to escape a customary ruling in colonial Africa all worked the
system strategically. Those best placed to shop were those with money, literacy and
connections. `contract enforcement` becomes a function of which forum a party can reach,
so legal dualism turns wealth into jurisdictional advantage.

**Extraterritoriality is the inverse.** In states that were never colonised the same
mechanism ran the other way. Foreigners in China, Japan, Siam and the Ottoman Empire were
exempted from local law and tried in their own consular courts, a population living in a
place but outside its law by treaty rather than conquest. DIP-150 owns that arrangement.
The symmetry is exact: colonial dualism puts the governed under a lesser law in their own
country, and extraterritoriality puts foreigners above the law of the country they live
in. Japan and Siam escaped extraterritoriality by adopting unified Western-style codes,
which is dualism's cure applied from the other side.

**Land is where dualism dispossesses.** In settler colonies the second track was often
recognised only so that it could be converted. New Zealand's Native Land Court turned
collective Māori title into individual titles that could be sold one owner at a time.
American doctrine held that discovery gave the colonising sovereign ultimate title.
Australia declared the land legally empty. This is the enclosure logic of AGR-200 applied
across a legal boundary: communal rights extinguished or made alienable so that land
could pass to settlers.

**The systems outlive the regimes.** Colonial legal dualism mostly survived
decolonisation, which is the entry's most important content. Specific inheritances
include:

- **India.** It kept separate personal laws for Hindus and Muslims, reformed one by
  statute in 1955-56, and still debates a uniform civil code.
- **Malaysia.** It runs civil and syariah courts side by side.
- **Nigeria.** Its northern states extended sharia jurisdiction from 1999.
- **Colonial criminal codes.** The Indian Penal Code and the codes modelled on it
  across Africa and Asia stayed in force for decades after independence, including
  provisions such as the colonial prohibition of homosexuality that India's Supreme
  Court struck down only in 2018, and the sedition section suspended in 2022.

New states inherited the machinery, the courts, the legal profession trained in it, and
constituencies whose authority depended on it, and unification threatened all three. The
model should treat these institutions as persisting past the regime that created them
unless something actively dismantles them.

## Social consequence

At household scale dualism decided which law governed a person's most intimate affairs:

- **A widow in Bengal.** Her inheritance was set by the Anglo-Hindu law the courts
  had constructed.
- **A daughter in Northern Nigeria.** Her marriage fell under a native court applying
  a customary law recorded from her elders' testimony.
- **A Nahua town council.** It could petition the General Indian Court in Mexico
  City against a local official and sometimes win.
- **An Indonesian villager.** They were governed by adat, while a Chinese trader in
  the same town was a Foreign Oriental under different rules and a Dutch official
  answered to a third court.

A person's legal category, not their conduct or their contract, decided which rules
applied.

Dualism produced intermediaries whose power rested on it:

- **Chiefs** confirmed as Native Authorities.
- **Court pandits and kadis.**
- **Adat experts.**
- **Interpreters and pleaders** who could move between systems.

Many of them became the elites of independent states, which is part of why the systems
survived. It also produced resentment at the category itself. The indigénat's summary
punishments and the subject-citizen line in French Africa were among the most bitterly
remembered features of colonial rule, precisely because they made inequality a legal
status rather than a practice.

## Regional specifics

This is majority non-European by construction, because the governed were:

- **The Spanish Americas.** They built the earliest explicit two-republic design, and
  Indigenous litigants used its courts to their advantage.
- **British India.** It produced the most influential codification and the penal code
  that spread across an empire and outlived it.
- **The Netherlands Indies.** They ran a three-class legal order and a scholarly school
  that defended adat against unification.
- **French Africa.** It carried the harshest form in the indigénat.
- **British Africa.** Indirect rule codified customary law through chiefs.
- **Japan.** It built the same architecture in Taiwan and Korea, showing the design was
  not European.
- **The Ottoman millet.** It is the pre-colonial version and prevents reading dualism as
  purely colonial.
- **New Zealand.** Its land court used dualism to dispossess.

The absences mark the alternatives. Australia's terra nullius denied that the governed
had law at all, choosing erasure where the aim was land rather than administration. The
French old colonies made their emancipated populations citizens in 1848 and so had no
second track to run. Colonial criminal codification (LAW-170) runs alongside all of this,
typically unifying criminal law even where personal law stayed plural, and REL-252
carries the partition of religious jurisdiction as a secular settlement. The civil-law
tradition applied to settlers is LAW-070's.

## Contested

Whether legal pluralism preserves autonomy or entrenches inequality is genuinely argued.
Mamdani, Chanock and Ranger show customary law codified through elite informants and
backed by colonial force becoming a decentralised despotism over rural subjects. Benton
and historians of Indigenous litigation show the governed using plural jurisdictions
strategically, and defenders of pluralism argue communal law protects autonomy against a
unifying state.

Model dualism as conditional. It preserves autonomy where the community controls the
revision of its own law and members can exit to general courts cheaply. It entrenches
inequality where the colonial state fixed the law through elite informants and exit is
costly or barred, with the costs falling hardest on women and juniors.

## Hook

**Two tracks.** A colonial state can govern a population through two legal tracks at
once: metropolitan law for settlers and citizens, and codified customary or religious
law for subjects. The second track costs far less than imposing one system, co-opts
local authorities by giving them jurisdiction, and raises `legibility` over the governed.
It marks them in law as a separate category.

**Codification freezes and bends.** Written-down custom is fixed where practice had
adapted, and it is weighted toward whichever informants recorded it.

**Forum shopping.** With two forums, `contract enforcement` depends on which forum a
party can reach, so wealth, literacy and connections convert into jurisdictional
advantage.

**Autonomy or inequality.** Whether the arrangement preserves communal autonomy or
entrenches inequality should depend on who controls revision of the customary law and
how costly it is to exit to general courts.

**Two variants.** In settler colonies the second track is recognised in order to convert
collective land into alienable title, or denied altogether where the aim is land rather
than rule. The inverse case is extraterritoriality, which exempts foreigners from local
law inside a sovereign state.

**Persistence.** The whole structure should outlast the colonial regime by default. New
states inherit plural personal law, colonial criminal codes and the intermediaries whose
authority depends on them, and unification is a costly reform rather than an automatic
consequence of independence.
