A standing body of elites or representatives that a ruler or state must
consult, persuade, or at minimum account to — converting rule from a single
actor's unconstrained decision into a coalition-management problem, whatever
the council's actual formal power.

## Timing

The Roman Senate's institutional role dates from the Republic's founding
(-509); the Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace establishes its confederational
council by around 1142; the Chinese Censorate develops as a distinct
institution from around -100, all operating on different structural
principles across an overlapping broad era.

## Mechanism

A council forces whoever holds executive authority to build and maintain a
coalition among its members rather than simply issuing commands, since a
council with real institutional standing (control over funding, formal
removal power, or simply enough collective prestige to make defiance
costly) can withhold cooperation — the specific mechanism of leverage
varies from formal removal authority (the Great Law of Peace) to investigative
and reputational sanction (the Censorate) to purely customary but very real
social pressure (African councils of elders).

## Social consequence

Council membership itself becomes a valuable, contested form of status
independent of whatever formal power the body holds, and the composition
rules — birth, wealth, age, lineage, appointment — determine whose
interests the coalition-management problem actually has to account for,
directly shaping which parts of a society a ruler's decisions end up
constrained by and which remain entirely unrepresented.

## Regional specifics

The Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace's confederational structure —
multiple sovereign member nations bound by a shared constitutional
procedure with defined consensus and leader-removal mechanisms — is a
sophisticated constitutional achievement operating on genuinely different
principles from Rome's single-polity aristocratic advisory body. The
Chinese Censorate's investigative-and-critical rather than deliberative-
and-advisory function shows the same basic coalition-constraint need can
be institutionalized as oversight of the executive rather than as a
separate representative body standing alongside it. African councils of
elders achieving comparable function through earned status and custom
rather than fixed codified office shows formal institutional permanence
isn't necessary for the underlying coalition-constraint mechanism to
operate effectively.

## Hook

A standing elite council forces coalition management onto whoever holds
executive authority — the leverage mechanism (formal removal power,
investigative sanction, or pure customary status pressure) and the
membership rules (birth, appointment, earned lineage status) vary
enormously across traditions, but every version constrains what a ruler can
do without accounting to some defined body beyond themselves.
