This entry owns trade unions specifically as a legal institution — the
right to organize, bargain collectively, and strike, and the statutory
recognition that makes those rights enforceable; the broader economic
critique of capitalism that motivates the labor movement is FIN-200's
territory.

## Timing

Britain's 1824 repeal of the Combination Acts is an early legal opening
for union organizing; sustained legal recognition of collective bargaining
and strike rights develops unevenly across major economies over the
following century, with the U.S. Wagner Act (1935) among the more
comprehensive later codifications.

## Mechanism

An individual worker has little bargaining leverage against an employer
who can simply hire a replacement, but workers acting collectively —
refusing to work simultaneously — impose a cost an employer generally
cannot absorb by replacement alone, at least in the short term; legal
recognition of this collective action (the right to organize, bargain, and
strike without being treated as criminal conspiracy) converts what would
otherwise be suppressible collective action into a protected, institutionalized
negotiating mechanism.

## Social consequence

Legal union recognition creates a durable, ongoing negotiating
relationship between organized labor and employers rather than one-off
confrontations, converting labor conditions into something bargained and
periodically renegotiated through an institutionalized process rather than
either unilaterally set by employers or won and lost through episodic,
legally risky collective action each time.

## Regional specifics

Indian trade unionism develops under colonial rule with labor organizing
and nationalist politics closely intertwined from the outset, a
combination shaping both how unions organized and how colonial authorities
responded to them, distinct from the more purely economic framing labor
organizing takes in several other regions. Middle Eastern union
recognition arriving later and under tighter state supervision than in
Europe, the Americas, or South Asia shows the same underlying legal
mechanism (collective bargaining rights) can be extended on very different
terms — more state-controlled and less adversarially independent — while
still nominally recognizing the same basic institution.

## Hook

Legal recognition of collective bargaining and strike rights converts
worker collective action from suppressible conspiracy into a protected,
institutionalized ongoing negotiation with employers — the same basic
mechanism extended on terms ranging from adversarial independence to tight
state supervision depending on the surrounding political context it
develops within.
