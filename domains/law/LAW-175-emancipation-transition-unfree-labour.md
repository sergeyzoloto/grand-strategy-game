Emancipation is a legal act. It does not by itself change the distribution of land,
capital or coercive power, so the outcome turns on what accompanies it. Freed people
without land stay dependent on former owners for subsistence: the legal status changes
and the economic relation does not. That is why emancipation reliably produces a
transitional institution designed to restrain the labour market it has just created.

## Timing

From Haiti's revolution and the French abolition of 1794, through British abolition in
1833-34 and its apprenticeship, the French second abolition of 1848, Russia's serf
emancipation in 1861, American emancipation in 1863-65 and Reconstruction's collapse
by 1877, to Brazil in 1888. Asian and African legal abolitions run through the century's
end and beyond: India's delegalisation in 1843, Siam's gradual abolition from 1874,
Korea in 1894, Northern Nigeria in 1901, French West Africa in 1905, Qing China in 1910,
and the Arabian peninsula in the 1960s.

## Mechanism

**What is being ended.** The corpus carries enslavement (WAR-035), the Atlantic trade
that supplied the plantation Americas (MIG-160), and eastern Europe's second serfdom
(AGR-190). This entry carries their legal end and what replaced them.

**Freedom without land changes the status, not the relation.** An enslaved person or a
serf works land owned by someone else, lives in housing owned by someone else, and eats
from their harvest. Declaring the person free removes the owner's legal claim to their
body. It leaves the owner holding the land, the tools and usually the local courts and
police. A freed family that owns nothing must still work for someone, and the likeliest
someone is the former owner. Whether emancipation changes lives therefore depends almost
entirely on what comes with it: land, capital, political rights, and a state willing to
enforce them.

**Emancipation produces a transitional institution to restrain the labour it frees.**
Former owners and the states they influenced reliably built a mechanism to keep freed
labour available and cheap. These transitional forms determined outcomes more than the
emancipation acts themselves:

- **Apprenticeship.** In the British Caribbean from 1834 to 1838, the formerly enslaved
  had to work for their former owners without wages for most of each week.
- **Sharecropping and crop-lien debt.** Freed families in the American South worked land
  for a share of the crop, advanced supplies against it, and fell into debt that bound
  them to the same plantation year after year.
- **Debt peonage and bonded labour.** In South Asia, Latin America and elsewhere a debt
  that could never be repaid held the worker as surely as a legal claim.
- **Vagrancy laws and Black Codes.** They criminalised being without an employer, so a
  freed person who refused a contract could be arrested and hired out as a convict.
- **Pass laws.** In southern Africa they controlled where Black workers could live and
  move.
- **Indentured migration.** It supplied replacement labour to plantations whose freed
  workers had left. Indians shipped to Mauritius, the Caribbean, Natal and Fiji, and
  Chinese to Cuba and Peru, arrived under long contracts enforced by penal sanctions.

These institutions reintroduce the `supervision cost` that WAR-035 identifies as the
term deciding whether coerced labour pays, now paid by the state through courts,
vagrancy arrests and pass inspections rather than by the owner. They keep much of the
`coerced labour stock` working under a legal form that is no longer slavery.

**The formerly unfree paid for their own freedom.** Compensation went to owners:

- **Britain** paid £20 million to slave owners, a sum repaid through the general tax
  base, and nothing to the enslaved.
- **France** compensated owners in 1848.
- **Haiti** was made to pay an indemnity to its former colonists in exchange for French
  recognition (DIP-120).
- **Russia** made serfs buy the land they already worked through redemption payments
  spread over forty-nine years.

In every case the costs of ending unfree labour fell substantially on the people freed,
directly or through taxes, and the owners' losses were covered.

**Land decides the branch.** Where land followed freedom, the outcome differed
fundamentally:

- **Haiti.** Pétion's land grants produced a peasantry that could not be driven back
  onto plantations.
- **French West Africa.** With open land available, hundreds of thousands of former
  slaves simply walked away after 1905.
- **Russia.** Communal allotment gave peasants land burdened with debt, a middle case
  that left them free and poor.
- **The United States.** It promised land, took it back, and produced a century of
  sharecropping and legal subordination.
- **Brazil.** It abolished with no land and no support.

Land was the variable that separated independent smallholding from dependent labour
under a new name.

**Reversal is a branch, not an epilogue.** Legal freedom without land or political
rights is reversible. Cases:

- **France.** It restored slavery by law in 1802 in Guadeloupe and Guiana and held it
  there until 1848.
- **The American South.** Legal freedom was hollowed out within a generation by Black
  Codes, convict leasing, disenfranchising constitutions and Jim Crow.
- **Southern Africa.** Pass laws and labour controls grew into apartheid.
- **Debt bondage.** It outlasted formal abolition across South Asia and beyond.

The model should treat emancipation as a state that can revert to legal re-subordination
achieving much of what slavery did by other means, unless land, political rights and
enforcement hold it in place.

**Formal equality turned out not to be enough.** After emancipation the question arose
whether equal legal status sufficed when a freed group started with no land, no capital
and a legacy of exclusion. The answer invented in response was group-differentiated
protection: reserved seats and posts for India's scheduled castes, anti-discrimination
law and affirmative programmes in the United States. It is the idea that some groups need
positive protection, not only the removal of legal disabilities. LAW-230 carries the
human-rights framework this fed into. The League of Nations minority-protection treaties
are a related inter-state instrument, recorded separately as a gap in the diplomacy
domain rather than carried here.

## Social consequence

At household scale emancipation was a change of name followed by the same field. A freed
family in Mississippi in 1866 might sign a year's contract with the same planter, work
the same land for a share of the crop, and owe the same planter's store by harvest. A
freed Russian peasant family owed redemption payments on land it had farmed for
generations, collected by a commune that could not let any household leave until the
debt was paid. A freed person in Northern Nigeria had to walk away or buy their freedom.
An Indian indentured labourer in Fiji or Trinidad worked five years under a penal
contract on a plantation that had lost its enslaved workforce.

Freedom still mattered, and freed people used every opening it gave. They reunited
families sold apart, founded churches and schools, withdrew women and children from
field labour where they could, bargained over contracts, moved to towns, and bought land
when land could be bought. The contest after emancipation was over whether those
openings would survive, and in most places it was fought for generations.

## Regional specifics

The transitional institutions carry the regional weight, and they are not an Atlantic
monopoly:

- **Haiti.** The only emancipation won by the enslaved themselves, and its own internal
  split between land grants and forced plantation labour.
- **The British Caribbean.** Apprenticeship, compensation to owners, and the indentured
  migration that replaced freed labour.
- **The United States.** The land promised and withdrawn, and the fullest reversal.
- **Brazil.** Abolition last in the Americas, with nothing attached.
- **Russia.** Freedom sold to the freed on forty-nine years' credit.
- **British India.** It stopped enforcing slave status and left debt bondage in place
  for a century.
- **Siam.** It abolished by a free-birth schedule to strengthen the crown.
- **Northern Nigeria.** It abolished slave status without freeing slaves, to protect the
  aristocracy indirect rule relied on. LAW-155 carries the colonial legal order behind
  that choice.
- **French West Africa.** Its exodus shows land availability deciding the outcome from
  below.
- **Korea and Qing China.** Both abolished late under reforming pressure.
- **The Ottoman Empire and Arabia.** Trade bans preceded abolition by a century.

Japan is the absence. Unfree labour there had already become term service and indenture,
so there was no slave society to transition from.

The plantation complex that emancipation dismantled is AGR-210's. The suffrage
restrictions that reversed Reconstruction are part of LAW-220's history of a formal right
diverging from its exercise.

## Contested

Why abolition happened is disputed:

- **Williams** tied British abolition to declining profitability.
- **Drescher and Eltis** showed slavery still profitable when abolished, putting
  politics and moral mobilisation first.
- **The second-slavery literature** shows the institution expanding elsewhere in the same
  century.

Model abolition as driven by political mobilisation and state interest that can run
against profitability.

What decided outcomes after emancipation is also argued, among land, political rights and
enforcement. Model the post-emancipation branch as conditional on all three. Freedom with
land and enforced rights holds. Freedom without them reverts to legal re-subordination.

## Hook

**Emancipation changes status, not relations.** It removes the owner's legal claim to the
worker's body but changes nothing about land, capital or local coercive power unless
something accompanies it.

**A transitional restraint follows.** Emancipation should reliably trigger an institution
that restrains the freed labour market: apprenticeship, sharecropping and crop-lien debt,
debt bondage, vagrancy law, pass laws, or imported indentured labour at a new
`migration cost`. It re-imposes `supervision cost` through the state and keeps much of the
`coerced labour stock` working under another legal form.

**The freed pay.** Compensation should flow to owners, with the freed paying directly or
through taxes.

**Three conditions decide the branch:**

- **Land.** Where freed people get land, or can take open land, they become an independent
  peasantry.
- **Political rights.**
- **The state's willingness to enforce them.**

Where the freed get none of the three, legal freedom should be reversible into
re-subordination, from restored slavery to Black Codes, Jim Crow and pass laws.

**Formal equality is insufficient.** It opens a demand for group-differentiated protection.
