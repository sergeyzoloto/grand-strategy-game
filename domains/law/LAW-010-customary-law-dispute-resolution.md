Dispute resolution before writing relies on socially-visible, memorized procedure rather than a citable text — the exact "before" state LAW-020's own Mechanism section acknowledges exists without ever modeling it.

## Timing

Restorative and compensatory dispute-resolution customs are plausibly as old as organized human society itself; the specific, better-attested cases this entry draws on — African lineage-council arbitration and European wergild-based feud settlement — are traceable in continuous practice from deep prehistory through the point each region's own written codification (LAW-020) begins recording, adapting, or displacing them.

## Mechanism

A functioning legal system requires no literacy at all — only a shared, memorized, and socially enforced procedure everyone recognizes as legitimate. Oath-swearing before witnesses (with the credibility of an oath-helper's testimony resting on that person's own social standing), physical ordeal (submitting to a trial whose outcome is read as divine judgment), and fixed compensation schedules like wergild (valuing an injury or death by rank so a kin-group's revenge motive is satisfied by an agreed payment instead of by unbounded retaliation) all substitute publicly legible ritual and procedure for a written, citable text.

## Social consequence

Because customary dispute resolution typically operates at the level of a kin-group or lineage rather than a state, its central social function is more often to prevent a single wrong from escalating into a self-perpetuating blood feud between whole families than to establish or defend any single abstract principle of justice — a compensation payment or a reconciliation ritual is judged successful specifically by whether it actually stops the cycle of retaliation, not by whether it matches some external, uniformly-applied standard of proportionate punishment.

## Regional specifics

European wergild-based feud settlement, though attested to us mainly through much later written law-codes that partially recorded it, functioned as a real, socially binding compensation system centuries before those codes existed. African lineage-council arbitration's continued function alongside, rather than simple replacement by, later colonial and postcolonial written law shows customary dispute resolution can persist as a genuinely parallel and still-legitimate system rather than a stage every society passes through and leaves behind. Mesopotamia's unusually early shift to written codification reflects that region's already-mature scribal infrastructure specifically, not a pace every society could or did match.

## Contested

How much continuity exists between specific customary practices — attested to us mainly indirectly, since custom itself typically leaves no direct record — and the actual content of the earliest written codes is genuinely debated, given how thin the evidentiary bridge between the two actually is. Model early written codes as a source-dependent, uncertain mix of recorded custom and codifier-driven innovation, rather than assuming either pure continuity or pure invention.

## Hook

An origin gate: establishes that dispute resolution and legal legitimacy exist and function before LAW-020's written codification, showing writing converts an already-working mechanism into a citable one rather than inventing law from nothing.
