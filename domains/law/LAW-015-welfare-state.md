This entry owns the legal and institutional architecture of state-provided
social insurance — the statutes and entitlement structures themselves;
FIN-016 already covers the Depression-era crisis pressure that motivated
much of this architecture's expansion.

## Timing

Bismarck's German social insurance legislation (from 1883) is an early
comprehensive state welfare system; the model expands substantially across
industrial economies through the following decades, with Britain's
Beveridge Report (1942) and subsequent National Health Service and social
insurance framework (from 1948) marking a particularly comprehensive
postwar consolidation.

## Mechanism

A welfare state converts social protection against illness, unemployment,
and old age from a private, family, or charitable matter into a legal
entitlement funded through compulsory contribution (tax or dedicated
social-insurance levy) and administered by the state — this pools risk
across the entire covered population rather than leaving it to individual
savings or family capacity, at the cost of requiring sustained state fiscal
and administrative capacity (FIN-001's legibility) to collect contributions
and administer benefits reliably at scale.

## Social consequence

Converting social protection into a state-administered legal entitlement
changes the underlying social relationship from one of family or communal
obligation into one of citizen-state contractual right, a shift with real
consequences for family structure and communal bonds as the state
increasingly substitutes for functions those relationships previously
carried — a transformation continuous with the individualizing habitus
shifts (privacy, urbanization) already covered elsewhere in this record.

## Regional specifics

Zakat's mandatory, proportionally-calculated redistribution toward
specified categories of need is a genuinely older legal institution
serving a structurally comparable function to modern compulsory social
insurance, grounded in religious rather than state fiscal law and
persisting for well over a millennium before Bismarck's system. Much of
colonial and early postcolonial Africa's continued reliance on extended
family and community obligation networks rather than formal state welfare
systems shows social protection can be provided reliably through informal
kin-based institutions running in parallel to, not merely preceding, the
formal state systems that develop elsewhere.

## Hook

The welfare state converts social protection from private or communal
obligation into a state-administered legal entitlement funded by
compulsory contribution — a function older redistributive institutions
like zakat already served through religious rather than state law, and one
that kin and community networks continue serving reliably in places
formal state systems haven't reached, rather than these being sequential
stages of one universal development path.
