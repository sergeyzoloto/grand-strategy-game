Legally mandated, near-universal military service obligation converts army
size from a function of how many professional soldiers a state can afford
to pay (WAR-001) into a function of total adult male population — a step
change in available manpower at the cost of a new, legally enforced
obligation binding the whole citizenry.

## Timing

The French Revolutionary levee en masse (1793) is the foundational case;
comparable universal conscription systems spread across major powers
through the 1800s, reaching their largest-scale application in the
mass-mobilization armies of World War One (to 1918).

## Mechanism

Conscription converts military manpower supply from a fiscal constraint
(how many professional soldiers a state's tax revenue can sustain, WAR-001)
into a legal and demographic one (how many draft-eligible men a state's
population contains and can compel), enabling army sizes an all-professional
model could never afford to field — at the cost of building and enforcing
an entirely new legal administrative apparatus (registration, exemption
adjudication, enforcement against evasion) touching nearly every household.

## Social consequence

Universal conscription ties state legitimacy directly to a demand it places
on nearly every family, making the terms of who serves, who is exempted, and
under what conditions a recurring and often explosive domestic political
issue — the same obligation that lets a state field mass armies also gives
its whole population direct, personal stakes in the state's war decisions in
a way a small professional army's campaigns never generated.

## Regional specifics

Meiji Japan's conscription law achieves in a single legislative act what
took France and other European states a more gradual process to
consolidate — deliberately and explicitly ending the samurai's hereditary
military monopoly and replacing it with universal citizen obligation,
directly linked to the same wholesale-institutional-import pattern already
seen in WAR-009's general staff adoption. Precolonial African mobilization
systems built on age-grade, lineage, or client structures achieve comparably
large-scale mobilization through existing social organization rather than
through an individual citizen-state legal obligation, showing mass
mobilization doesn't require the specific conscription-law mechanism to
reach large scale.

## Hook

Conscription converts army size from a fiscal ceiling into a demographic and
legal one, trading a much larger available manpower pool for a new,
household-reaching legal obligation whose terms (who serves, who's exempt)
become a direct, high-stakes domestic political fault line for the state
that imposes it.
