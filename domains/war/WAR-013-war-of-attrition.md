A strategy that deliberately or effectively trades decisive battlefield
victory for a prolonged contest of relative endurance — population, fiscal
reserves, and sustained production capacity becoming more decisive than any
single engagement's tactical outcome.

## Timing

Pericles' deliberate attritional strategy in the Peloponnesian War (from
-430) is an early explicit case; the strategy recurs across military history
wherever a decisive engagement proves unachievable or is deliberately
avoided, reaching its largest industrial-scale expression in World War One's
Western Front (to 1918).

## Mechanism

An attritional strategy shifts the deciding variable from tactical
battlefield skill to whichever side can sustain losses, resource
consumption, and continued mobilization longer — this converts the war's
outcome into a function of the underlying economies, populations, and fiscal
systems (touching COM and FIN directly) each side can draw on, rather than
of any single campaign's tactical result, and it only favors the side that
has correctly assessed it actually holds the greater underlying endurance
capacity.

## Social consequence

Because an attritional strategy imposes sustained, visible cost (casualties,
economic strain, prolonged mobilization) on the *entire* home population
rather than concentrating risk on professional soldiers in a short decisive
campaign, it tends to generate much greater domestic political strain and
war-weariness over time, tying attritional conflict directly to the
propaganda and morale-management concerns of WAR-018 in a way a short
decisive war rarely does to the same degree.

## Regional specifics

Pericles' strategy is a rare case where the attritional choice is explicitly
and deliberately articulated in advance, rather than attrition being a
retrospective label applied to a war that simply failed to produce a
decisive engagement. Japan's invasion of China is close to the inverse case:
a strategy premised on quick decisive victory that becomes an unplanned war
of attrition Japan turns out to be on the losing side of, given China's
much larger sustaining population and territory — a strategic miscalculation
about which side actually held the endurance advantage, not a deliberate
attritional choice at all.

## Contested

Whether "war of attrition" describes a genuine deliberate strategy or is
mostly a retrospective label for wars that failed to produce decisive
engagement is disputed case by case. Model attrition as a real, selectable
strategic option whose payoff depends entirely on a state correctly judging
its own relative endurance capacity against its opponent's beforehand —
letting the same strategic label produce a Periclean success or a Japanese-
in-China-style disaster depending purely on whether that underlying
assessment was actually right.

## Hook

Committing to attrition converts the war's outcome from tactical skill into
a comparison of underlying sustaining capacity (population, fiscal reserves,
production) — a strategy that only pays off if the committing state
correctly holds the greater endurance, and imposes home-front political
strain in direct proportion to how long it drags on.
