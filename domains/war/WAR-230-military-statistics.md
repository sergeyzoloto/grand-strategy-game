Applying systematic quantitative record-keeping — casualties, supply
consumption, unit strength over time — to war itself, converting a
commander's and a state's picture of an ongoing conflict from anecdotal
report into trackable, comparable data.

## Timing

Systematic military statistics develop substantially through the mid-to-
late 1800s, with the scale and administrative demands of conflicts like the
American Civil War (from 1861) and the mass-mobilization wars through World
War One (to 1918) driving much finer-grained record-keeping than earlier
eras produced or preserved.

## Mechanism

Reliable casualty and logistics statistics let a state and its commanders
make resource-allocation and manpower-replacement decisions based on
trackable trends rather than fragmentary anecdotal reports — this is the
same underlying `legibility` mechanic (WAR-040, WAR-210) applied to the
conflict's own internal state rather than to the population and territory a
state governs in peacetime, and it requires a comparable standing
administrative apparatus to sustain through the chaos of active campaigning.

## Social consequence

Systematic casualty accounting makes the human cost of a conflict newly
visible and comparable in aggregate to political leadership and, once
published or leaked, to the broader public — a state that tracks and must
eventually report its own casualty figures faces a different domestic
political pressure than one that can keep losses vague or unstated, tying
this administrative capability directly to the propaganda and morale
concerns of WAR-260.

## Regional specifics

The American Civil War's unusually thorough surviving casualty and
logistics documentation, a product of the conflict's own administrative
scale, makes it a disproportionately mined dataset for later military
statisticians relative to contemporary conflicts elsewhere — a case where
data survival and compilation effort, not the underlying event's actual
importance, shapes what later analysis is possible. Comparable East Asian
conflicts of the same era being less consistently documented in a form
enabling equivalent statistical comparison most likely reflects gaps in
surviving records and translation/compilation effort rather than a genuine
difference in contemporary administrative practice.

## Hook

Systematic casualty and logistics accounting extends a state's `legibility`
into its own ongoing war effort, enabling trend-based resource and manpower
decisions — and, once that data becomes visible beyond the military
command itself, feeding directly into domestic political pressure and
propaganda dynamics (WAR-260).
