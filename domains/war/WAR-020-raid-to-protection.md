A predator that expects to come back has an interest in what it is preying on
staying productive. Episodic taking converts into standing extraction once the
predator's time horizon lengthens and it can keep rivals off the same target —
and the target's own preference for a predictable payment over an
unpredictable loss is what makes the arrangement hold rather than merely be
imposed. This entry defines `protection rent`, the stat several entries
across this record already draw on.

## Timing

Standing payment-for-peace arrangements appear wherever raiding (WAR-010)
recurs long enough for both sides to recognise the pattern and price it. Han
payments to the Xiongnu run from -198, Danegeld from 991, the Chanyuan
Covenant fixes the Song-Liao schedule in 1005, and the Maratha chauth is a
codified revenue right by 1719 — with the mechanism continuing through
Barbary tribute extraction from European states until the 1830s.

## Mechanism

Olson's distinction is the clean statement. A roving bandit that will never
return takes everything, because nothing it leaves behind will ever be worth
anything to it; a stationary bandit that expects to collect again next season
takes less, deliberately, to keep the source producing — converting a single
maximal haul into a smaller repeated one whose sum exceeds it. Two conditions
have to hold for the conversion. The predator's horizon must lengthen, which
usually means it can no longer easily find fresh targets or has become
identifiable enough to be negotiated with. And it must hold something close to
a monopoly on the threat it is charging against, which is Lane's point about
protection rent being a real cost of doing business rather than a pure
extortion — a payer facing one protector who actually excludes rival raiders
is buying something, while a payer facing four claimants is simply being
raided on an instalment plan. Where this runs at sea it is MAR's theatre by
the boundary rules, and MAR-030 and MAR-110 carry the maritime instances;
this entry supplies the stat they draw on rather than re-deriving their cases.

## Social consequence

The target frequently prefers the arrangement, and that preference is the
hinge the whole mechanism turns on rather than an incidental detail. A
predictable, budgetable payment can be planned around, insured against, and
passed on in prices; an unpredictable destruction cannot. A protector that
genuinely excludes rivals is supplying something the payer would otherwise
have to buy by standing under arms itself, usually at greater cost. This is
also exactly why Tilly's framing is uncomfortable rather than merely cynical
— it insists a state's own tax legitimacy rests on the same comparison the
payer of khuwwa is making, not on a difference in kind. The failure modes
follow from the same structure. Extraction can exceed what the productive
base bears, at which point the stationary bandit has resumed behaving like a
roving one and destroys its own revenue. Competing protectors produce a worse
outcome than a single one, since each extracts without any being able to
exclude the others. The protector's incapacity, once exposed by a raid it
failed to stop, converts the rent from an accepted cost into a grievance
faster than never having paid at all would have. And payment is revenue — the
recipient can reinvest it in exactly the capacity that made the payment
necessary, which is the Danegeld outcome and the reason buying off a raider
is a bet on its horizon rather than a settlement of the problem.

## Regional specifics

The Song-Liao case shows the calculation explicitly, the payments running
well below the cost of the campaigning they replaced. Danegeld shows the
capitalisation failure mode in its clearest form. The Maratha chauth shows
the end state, a rent so routinised it is a tax with a different origin
story. Bedouin khuwwa shows the mechanism operating entirely below the state
level, with the monopoly condition supplied by tribal range rather than
sovereignty. Tuareg transit levies show it absorbed as priced overhead by a
commercial institution. The steppe is a structural rather than a regional
case throughout — Barfield's argument is that a confederation forms in the
first place _because_ an adjacent settled state is rich enough to be worth
extracting from, which makes MIG-110's confederation-formation mechanic and
this entry's extraction outcome two halves of one process rather than
neighbouring topics. Aztec tribute provinces show extraction substituting for
administration, and their own flower-war exception shows the conversion
failing where the object cannot be paid in goods.

## Contested

Tilly's thesis that this mechanism _is_ state formation, rather than one
pathway into it, is disputed on generalisability beyond the early modern
European cases it was drawn from. Model the conversion as a real recurring
mechanism, and make the stronger claim conditional on whether a polity had
other routes available — irrigation-management, sacred-authority, and
bureaucratic-recruitment states reach comparable capacity without this
pathway, so the generalisation should hold where those alternatives are
absent and weaken where they are present, rather than being scripted true or
false everywhere.

## Hook

A transition, not a technology: the same raiding capability (WAR-010) yields
either episodic loss or standing `protection rent` depending on the
predator's time horizon and whether it can monopolise the threat it charges
against — branching on prior institutional state per design principle 1
(is the target organised enough to negotiate and pay reliably, do rival
predators exist, can the protector be displaced) rather than on anyone's
choice. It fails in four distinct ways worth modelling separately —
over-extraction, competing protectors, exposed incapacity converting rent to
grievance, and payment capitalising the recipient into a larger threat than
the one it bought off.
