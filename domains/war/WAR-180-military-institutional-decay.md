Every other capability loss in this domain comes from being out-teched or out-doctrined by a rival; this entry covers the alternative — a standing institution's own permanence and success converting it into a political veto player the state eventually judges more dangerous to reform than to destroy outright.

## Timing

The Ottoman Janissary corps, founded in the 1300s as a centrally-controlled, meritocratic elite infantry force (WAR-060's professional-soldiery logic in an early form), begins visibly resisting military modernization and accumulating hereditary privilege from around 1600; the institution is violently abolished by Sultan Mahmud II in the 1826 "Auspicious Incident" after generations of failed reform attempts.

## Mechanism

A standing professional institution (WAR-060) that persists across many generations can convert its own permanence into political leverage: the Janissary corps progressively secures hereditary membership rights, exemption from real combat readiness requirements, and enough domestic political influence (including a documented history of deposing sultans who moved against its interests) to block exactly the modernization reforms — adopting contemporary drill, weapons, and discipline — that would have kept it militarily effective. The institution's problem isn't external technological obsolescence but its own accumulated internal veto power over the reforms needed to prevent that obsolescence.

## Social consequence

Because the corps' resistance to reform is backed by genuine domestic political power rather than mere institutional inertia, the state's options narrow over time to either accepting continued military decline or risking violent confrontation with an armed, politically entrenched body — a dynamic with no gradual reform path available once the institution's veto power is strong enough, ending, as with the Janissaries, only in the institution's forcible destruction rather than its negotiated transformation.

## Regional specifics

The Janissary corps' trajectory — from meritocratic elite force to hereditary, reform-blocking interest group — is a documented, well-studied case precisely because of how long the pattern persisted (over two centuries of documented reform attempts and resistance) before the state finally acted, making the eventual violent resolution look sudden only in isolation from that much longer preceding institutional history.

## Hook

A standing institution's own permanence and success can convert it into a political veto player the state eventually judges more dangerous to reform than to destroy outright — a capability-loss mechanic requiring no external rival, only the institution's own accumulated internal political leverage over the reforms needed to keep it effective.
