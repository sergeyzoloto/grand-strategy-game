Coal is everywhere and oil is not, and that single asymmetry converts an
industrial input into a territorial question. The mechanism is not new —
ENG-010 establishes it three thousand years earlier with Bronze Age tin —
but oil runs it at a scale and with a directness nothing before it
matched.

## Timing

From the Royal Navy's 1911 conversion, through the fuel-shaped campaigns
of the Second World War, to OPEC's inversion of the mechanism in 1973.

## Mechanism

**A militarily essential input that most powers cannot source
domestically is a different class of problem from an expensive one.** An
ordinary shortage is relieved by paying more or producing more at home.
This one cannot be: no price calls oil out of ground that does not
contain it, and no industrial policy substitutes for a geological
absence. The only remedies are securing access to someone else's
territory, manufacturing a substitute at a ruinous efficiency penalty
(ENG-275's synthetic fuel), or not having the capability at all. **An
industrial question becomes a territorial one**, and that conversion is
the entry.

This is precisely the mechanic ENG-010 describes for tin. Copper was
ubiquitous and tin was not, so no bronze-age power could arm itself from
its own ground, and the metal arrived over long routes through
intermediaries it did not control. Oil reproduces the structure exactly,
with three differences of degree that make it sharper: the consumption is
continuous rather than a stock that wears out slowly, so an interruption
bites in weeks rather than years; the dependency reaches every arm of a
modern force at once rather than only its weapons; and the substitution
options are worse. Presenting the mechanism as a twentieth-century
novelty would miss that it is one of the oldest constraints in the
record.

**Conversion to oil is a decision that trades capability for
dependency.** Churchill's 1911 choice is the template — oil-firing bought
speed, range, and a large reduction in stokehold crew, and it gave up a
domestic fuel supply for a foreign one. That trade was recognised at the
time and made anyway, which is the right shape for the mechanic: the
capability gain is immediate and certain, the dependency cost is deferred
and probabilistic, and states take the trade.

**Fuel constrains what is physically possible, not what is decided.**
This is the careful version of the claim and the one the model should
use. Aircraft that exist do not fly, armour that functions does not move,
and fleets stay in harbour — the constraint operates on execution rather
than on intent. A state without fuel cannot do certain things; it is a
larger and less defensible step to say it must therefore do others.

**Concentration cuts both ways, and 1973 is where it reverses.** For six
decades concentration was a consumer vulnerability and a prize for
whoever could control the producing ground. OPEC's discovery was that the
same concentration is seller's leverage, exercisable by withholding.
Note the difference from DIP-190's sanctions, which work through the
breadth of a coalition and fail when enforcement leaks: this works
through the narrowness of supply, and needs no coalition beyond the
producers themselves.

## Social consequence

A dependency of this kind reorganises a state's foreign relations around
a commodity. Britain's entanglement with Persian politics after 1914 was
not a policy choice revisited periodically but a standing commitment that
followed from the fleet's boilers, and it outlasted the ships. The pattern
recurs wherever the dependency does — alliances, interventions, and
tolerance of otherwise unacceptable partners all follow the supply, and a
government's room to act on other questions narrows accordingly.

Inside the producing states the effect is the mirror image. A resource
that foreign powers must have, held by a government that need not tax its
population to obtain revenue, produces a state financed from outside its
own society. The concession terms set in the 1920s and 1930s, before most
producing states had the standing to negotiate them, then became the
grievance that drove nationalisation, which is why those confrontations
were political rather than commercial — the arrangements predated the
parties.

## Regional specifics

Japan in 1941 is the sharpest case and should carry the most weight: no
domestic oil, limited high-octane refining, total military dependence on
both, and a reserve measured in months when the embargo came. Germany is
the operational case, fighting a war planned around fuel it never had,
with Ploiesti, synthetic production, and the Caucasus drive all as
answers to one question. Baku is where two combatants' needs converge on
one field. The United States is the control — the only major belligerent
with abundance, which was an instrument rather than merely the absence of
a constraint, and the reason the embargo was available as an American
policy at all.

The absences carry the durable part. No African state held production
while the framework was built, so African oil arrived into terms set
before the parties existed. South Asia's absence is of agency rather than
resource, with the dependency real and the decisions taken in London.

## Contested

Fuel access is unusually easy to credit after the fact, because shortage
appears in quartermasters' records while doctrine and intent do not.
Both the Japanese decision for war and the German failure in the east had
other sufficient causes, and the strongest versions of the fuel
explanation are probably overstated.

Model fuel as a hard constraint on **what is physically executable**
rather than as a determinant of what is chosen. A state with no fuel
cannot fly its aircraft — defensible and mechanically clean. A state with
no fuel must attack to get some — a much stronger claim that the evidence
supports far less well, and one that would make the mechanic deterministic
in a way design principle 1 does not want. The 1973 embargo is separately
contested, since the embargo and the price shock were distinct events and
the embargo's direct effect on physical supply was smaller than the price
response implies.

## Hook

`strategic input dependency` at its sharpest, and the same mechanic
ENG-010 runs on Bronze Age tin rather than a new one. The input cannot be
bought into existence or substituted domestically at reasonable cost, so
the only moves are access to foreign ground, a ruinous synthetic
programme, or doing without — an industrial constraint that resolves into
a territorial one. Conversion to oil should be an attractive trade that
is also a trap: immediate certain capability for deferred probabilistic
dependency, which states take. The constraint should bite on execution
rather than intent, grounding aircraft and immobilising armour that exist
and work, without forcing any particular decision in response.
Concentration should be readable from either side — a consumer's
vulnerability for sixty years, then a producer's lever, working through
narrowness of supply rather than the breadth of coalition that DIP-190's
sanctions require. And a state financed by a resource foreigners must
have need not tax its own population, which loosens a constraint every
other revenue mechanic in this record imposes.
