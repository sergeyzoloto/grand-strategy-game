Soldiers in combat are motivated far more by loyalty to the handful of men immediately beside them than by the larger legal or ideological framing of their service — a small-unit cohesion effect operating beneath WAR-180's legal-obligation layer and WAR-170's institutional-leadership layer, and one that can fail even in a well-equipped, well-led force that hasn't built it.

## Timing

Systematic study of what actually drives soldiers to fight, as opposed to simply serve, develops substantially from 19th-century military sociology onward, with the mid-20th-century "primary group cohesion" literature — built on both World Wars' combat experience — becoming the most influential and most contested body of evidence on the question by 1950.

## Mechanism

A soldier's willingness to actually engage in combat, rather than merely be present and equipped, correlates most strongly with bonds of loyalty to the small group of comrades fighting immediately alongside them — a primary-group cohesion effect distinct from and often stronger than the legal obligation conscription (WAR-180) imposes or the institutional leadership structure a professional NCO corps (WAR-170) provides. A unit can be legally obligated, well-led, and well-equipped, and still fail to fight effectively if this specific small-group bond hasn't formed; conversely, cohesive small units have repeatedly been observed fighting effectively under conditions (poor equipment, weak higher command) that should predict collapse.

## Social consequence

Because primary-group cohesion forms through shared risk and time together rather than through legal or institutional design, it makes unit rotation policy, replacement practices, and casualty-driven turnover a direct lever on combat effectiveness independent of training quality — an army that rotates individual replacements into existing units disrupts this cohesion in a way that rotating whole units together doesn't, a personnel-policy choice with combat consequences no amount of individual soldier training can fully offset.

## Regional specifics

The mid-20th-century American and European combat-psychology literature, built substantially on both World Wars' documented experience, remains this entry's primary evidentiary base — and its most cited specific claim is also its most disputed, per the Contested note below.

## Contested

S.L.A. Marshall's widely-cited claim that only 15-25% of American WWII infantrymen actually fired their weapons at the enemy has been seriously challenged on methodological grounds and is no longer generally accepted at face value. Model the underlying primary-group cohesion effect as well-supported by a broader evidence base, while treating Marshall's specific ratio-of-fire statistic as a discredited or unverifiable data point rather than a reliable baseline number.

## Hook

A small-unit cohesion effect operating beneath WAR-180's legal-obligation layer and WAR-170's institutional-leadership layer, capable of producing combat failure — or unexpected combat effectiveness — that neither of those entries alone would predict, and directly sensitive to personnel-rotation policy in a way training investment alone can't offset.
