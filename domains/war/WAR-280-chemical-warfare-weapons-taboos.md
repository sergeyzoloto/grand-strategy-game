A weapon proves militarily effective but so uniquely horrifying in its effects that it generates a durable international taboo suppressing its use far more thoroughly than its actual tactical utility alone would predict — a weapon neutralized by diplomatic norm-enforcement rather than by counter-technology or doctrine, unlike every other weapon this domain covers.

## Timing

The German army's first large-scale chlorine gas attack at Ypres (1915) opens sustained chemical warfare on the Western Front; both sides develop and deploy increasingly lethal agents (phosgene, then mustard gas) through the remainder of World War One; the 1925 Geneva Protocol formally prohibits chemical (and biological) weapons use in war, though not their development or stockpiling.

## Mechanism

Poison gas inflicts real tactical effect — forcing protective countermeasures, area denial, psychological terror disproportionate to actual casualty counts — but its effects (lingering contamination, indiscriminate harm to anyone without protection regardless of combatant status, and uniquely visible, prolonged suffering among survivors) generate a level of revulsion among both combatants and neutral observers that converts it into a target for formal international prohibition. Once codified in the Geneva Protocol, the weapon's subsequent battlefield fate depends less on its own continued technical effectiveness than on whether a given state judges the diplomatic and reputational cost of breaking the taboo (DIP's territory) higher than whatever tactical advantage using it would provide.

## Social consequence

A taboo weapon creates a strange asymmetry unlike any other entry in this domain: states continue researching, producing, and stockpiling the weapon (as a deterrent or hedge) while collectively refraining from its actual battlefield use for generations, converting the weapon's real value into a held-in-reserve threat rather than an active tool — a dynamic this entry's own logic anticipates the much larger-scale nuclear case (WAR-310) will later reproduce.

## Regional specifics

The Western Front's chemical warfare escalation between 1915 and 1918 produces the weapon's most extensive documented battlefield use in this record, and the same conflict's scale of civilian and combatant horror at its effects directly drives the 1925 Geneva Protocol's rapid, near-universal international adoption relative to how slowly comparable arms-control agreements usually develop.

## Hook

A branch whose outcome turns on whether a given actor judges the international taboo's reputational cost higher than the weapon's tactical value in a specific conflict, not on the weapon's raw battlefield effectiveness — a weapon suppressed by diplomatic norm-enforcement (DIP) rather than by counter-technology or doctrine, anticipating the same held-in-reserve logic WAR-310's nuclear deterrence later reproduces at far larger scale.
