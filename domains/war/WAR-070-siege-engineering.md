The engines and fortification techniques themselves (rams, towers, walls,
sapping) are ENG's; this entry owns the campaign-level decision doctrine —
when to besiege rather than storm or bypass, and how to sustain an army in
the field long enough to starve or breach a defended position.

## Timing

Systematic siege doctrine is attested from Neo-Assyrian campaigns (~-900)
through continuous refinement across the Mediterranean, Middle East, and
independently in East and South Asia, into the gunpowder-artillery era
(WAR-130, WAR-150) that eventually makes traditional wall-based fortification
obsolete by around 1500.

## Mechanism

A besieging army faces a sustained logistics problem at least as demanding
as the defenders': it must feed, house, and maintain cohesion in a large
force for a potentially long investment while defenders can ration a
smaller, static garrison — the campaign-level decision of whether to
besiege, storm immediately, or bypass a fortified position entirely turns on
a commander's assessment of relative attrition rates, not just on wall
height or garrison size.

## Social consequence

A successful siege doctrine requires sustained logistical and fiscal
capacity (feeding an army in the field for months) that only a state with
real administrative reach (WAR-060's professional standing-army capacity)
can reliably sustain — sieges of any real duration are, in this sense, as
much a test of a besieging state's own institutional depth as of its
tactical skill.

## Regional specifics

Neo-Assyrian siege doctrine's routine, standardized character — depicted
matter-of-factly in the empire's own commissioned art — shows systematic
siegecraft was an established, expected campaign tool rather than an
occasional improvisation, remarkably early. Chinese siege doctrine's
comparatively heavier emphasis on blockade and negotiated surrender over
direct assault reflects a different weighting of the same underlying
attrition calculus this entry's mechanism describes — not a lesser
engineering capability, but a different judgment about where the cost
balance favors patience over direct investment.

## Hook

Siege doctrine is a campaign-level attrition bet between besieger and
besieged logistics capacity — the decision to besiege, storm, or bypass
should weigh a commander's own sustained-supply capability against the
garrison's, not just against wall height or the raw engineering power
available (ENG's territory) to breach it.
