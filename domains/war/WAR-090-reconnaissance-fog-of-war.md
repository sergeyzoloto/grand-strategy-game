A commander's decisions are only as good as their knowledge of enemy
position, strength, and intent — and for nearly all of military history that
knowledge is fragmentary, delayed, and frequently wrong, which is as
decisive a constraint as any weapon or formation.

## Timing

Dedicated scouting and intelligence-gathering practice is attested wherever
organized warfare exists; this entry tracks its development as a systematic
doctrine, from early steppe and Mediterranean practice through the point
(around 1900, ahead of aviation-based reconnaissance, WAR-250) where
technology begins to close the fog-of-war gap rather than merely manage it.

## Mechanism

Every tactical and strategic decision in this domain is made against
imperfect information about the enemy, and the cost of that imperfection
scales with how much a commander's plan depends on assumptions that turn out
wrong — investment in scouting, spies, and standing intelligence-gathering
reduces that uncertainty at a real cost (diverted manpower, time, resources)
but never eliminates it, converting "fog of war" from a fixed handicap into
a variable a commander can spend resources to partially manage.

## Social consequence

Where reconnaissance is systematized into a standing function rather than
improvised per-campaign, it creates a specialized scouting or intelligence
role distinct from the main fighting force, whose value is hard to verify
in the moment (a scout reporting no enemy contact looks identical whether
they searched thoroughly or not at all) and correspondingly easy for
resource-constrained commanders to underinvest in.

## Regional specifics

Steppe confederation warfare's structural dependence on scouting ranging far
ahead of the main force follows directly from its own core mobility
advantage (MIG-050): speed is only tactically useful if paired with enough
advance warning to actually exploit it, making reconnaissance and mobility
mutually reinforcing rather than separate capabilities. Pre-modern European
commanders' frequently weaker systematic scouting doctrine, relying instead
on incidental local information, is a genuine documented gap responsible for
more than one costly surprise engagement — not every tradition treated
reconnaissance as a baseline competence worth standing investment.

## Hook

Investment in reconnaissance converts a fixed fog-of-war handicap into a
variable, resource-purchasable reduction in enemy-position uncertainty — a
direct `information speed` lever that compounds with mobility advantages
(steppe cavalry) but is easy to underinvest in precisely because its payoff
(avoided surprises) is invisible when it works.
