Legally mandated, near-universal military service obligation converts army
size from a function of how many professional soldiers a state can afford
to pay (WAR-060) into a function of total adult male population — a step
change in available manpower at the cost of a new, legally enforced
obligation binding the whole citizenry.

## Timing

The French Revolutionary levee en masse (1793) is the foundational case;
comparable universal conscription systems spread across major powers
through the 1800s, reaching their largest-scale application in the
mass-mobilization armies of World War One (to 1918).

## Mechanism

Conscription converts military manpower supply from a fiscal constraint
(how many professional soldiers a state's tax revenue can sustain, WAR-060)
into a legal and demographic one (how many draft-eligible men a state's
population contains and can compel), enabling army sizes an all-professional
model could never afford to field. But the binding constraint is not the
law and not the money. It is information.

**A state cannot conscript a population it cannot enumerate.** The
mechanism presupposes four capabilities that have nothing to do with
warfare and must exist before a single man is called. A register of
births, so that age cohorts can be identified at all. A name that stays
attached to one person across a lifetime — which is why surname
standardisation across much of Europe was driven substantially by this
need, since an obligation cannot be held against someone whose name
changes with generation, trade, or village. A residence record, so the
person can be found. And a bureaucracy able to adjudicate exemption
claims and pursue evasion, which is the expensive part, because every
exemption category creates an incentive to qualify for it and every
claim must be assessed against a record.

This makes conscription a **downstream application of `legibility`**
rather than an independent capability, and the historical sequencing
shows it plainly. France transfers civil registration from the parish
(REL-210) to the commune in 1792, a year before the levee en masse;
Meiji Japan legislates education in 1872 and conscription in 1873.
The enumeration comes first in both cases because it must. A state with
ample manpower and no register cannot run this mechanism at all, and one
with a thorough census can run it whether or not its population is
large — which inverts the usual assumption that conscription is a
resource question.

The dependency also runs the other way as an incentive. Building the
register is expensive and unpopular, and conscription supplies a reason
to do it that tax alone had often failed to supply, so a state that
decides to conscript typically ends up with enumeration machinery far
more complete than its fiscal needs would ever have justified — and that
machinery is then available for everything else it governs.

**Mass service then produces literacy on a scale no other institution
matches.** The barracks is the largest adult education establishment any
nineteenth-century state operates, and it reaches precisely the
population schooling reaches last — adult men from rural districts, held
for a fixed term, under discipline, with the state bearing the cost.
Prussian army schooling after 1807, French regimental instruction after
1872, and above all the Russian army after the Milyutin reforms of 1874,
which became the single largest route to reading for peasant men in the
empire. The war ministry ran a literacy programme because it was the only
institution with the reach to run one.

What that produces is larger than reading. Following Weber, army service
together with schooling is the mechanism that converts regional
populations into national ones — a standardised language displacing
dialect because orders arrive in it, national symbols encountered daily
rather than abstractly, a shared time discipline imposed by the routine,
and the direct experience of men from other regions as countrymen rather
than as strangers. **This is nation-building conducted through the
barracks**, and it is arguably conscription's largest long-run
consequence, larger than any battle it enabled.

**The demand runs down to squad level, which is a direct military
argument for compulsory schooling.** A conscript army fighting dispersed
rather than in close order needs men who can read a map, a written order,
and a signal — competence required not at staff level but at the bottom
of the chain, from NCOs (WAR-190) commanding small groups out of sight of
any officer. That requirement is one of the genuine drivers behind
compulsory primary education in several states, and it is worth carrying
as a military argument rather than a civic one: the case made to
legislatures was frequently that an illiterate recruit is a tactically
useless one.

## Social consequence

Universal conscription ties state legitimacy directly to a demand it places
on nearly every family, making the terms of who serves, who is exempted, and
under what conditions a recurring and often explosive domestic political
issue — the same obligation that lets a state field mass armies also gives
its whole population direct, personal stakes in the state's war decisions in
a way a small professional army's campaigns never generated.

## Regional specifics

Meiji Japan achieves in a single legislative act what took European states
a gradual process to consolidate, ending the samurai's hereditary military
monopoly outright, on the same wholesale-institutional-import pattern as
WAR-230's general staff adoption — and pairs it with an education ordinance
the year before, which is the clearest statement anywhere that the two
halves of this entry were understood at the time as one programme.

The Ottoman case carries the sharpest social finding and should not be
treated as peripheral. Conscription from 1839 ran by lottery on registered
populations while excluding non-Muslims, who paid an exemption levy
instead — which made liability to serve the practical test of who counted
as a full subject. Extending service to non-Muslims was therefore a
question about citizenship and not about manpower, and it shows the
general principle more plainly than any European case does: **where the
obligation falls defines the political community.** The American case
shows the same variable failing, with commutation and substitution making
the 1863 draft formally universal and practically class-graded, and riots
following within weeks. It is the exemption rule rather than the
obligation that decides whether conscription is experienced as shared
citizenship or as expropriation.

Russia is the anchor for the education mechanism rather than Prussia,
because the gap it closed was far wider — a low-literacy peasant society
in which the army became the principal institution teaching men to read.

Precolonial African mobilisation through age-grade sets, lineage
obligation, and clientage reaches comparable scale by a wholly different
route, with the obligation attaching to a person's position in a structure
the society already tracks rather than to a record the state maintains. It
is a different mobilisation system and not a deficient one — and it
requires no registration apparatus precisely because the social structure
performs the enumeration. That is the same variable this entry turns on,
solved without a bureaucracy.

## Hook

Conscription converts army size from a fiscal ceiling into a demographic and
legal one, but it should be gated on `legibility` rather than on population —
a state that cannot enumerate its subjects cannot run the mechanism at any
manpower, and one with a thorough register can run it whether or not its
population is large. The four prerequisites are a birth register, a stable
name, a residence record, and an exemption bureaucracy, and the last is the
expensive one because every exemption category creates an incentive to
qualify for it. Deciding to conscript should therefore *build* enumeration
machinery well beyond what fiscal need would justify, which then becomes
available for everything else the state does. In the other direction, mass
service should raise `literacy` at a rate no other institution matches,
reaching adult rural men that schooling reaches last — and with it deliver
standardised language, national symbols, and contact between regions, so
that the mechanic's largest long-run output is a national population rather
than an army. Dispersed tactics should then demand reading competence down
to squad level, making compulsory schooling a military requirement and not
only a civic one. And the exemption rule is the political variable: who is
liable defines who is a citizen, so a state that exempts a community by
confession or lets the wealthy commute should get shared citizenship or
riots accordingly.
