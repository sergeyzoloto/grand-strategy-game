An army's sustainable campaign range and duration is capped by how much food, fodder, and ammunition it can carry, forage along the way, or have delivered by a maintained supply train — a hard ceiling independent of tactical skill or army size that every campaign-level entry in this domain silently assumes is already solved.

## Timing

Systematic logistics planning is attested from Neo-Assyrian campaign administration (~-900) and explicit Warring States Chinese military-treatise doctrine (~-400) onward, remaining the binding constraint on campaign range and duration across the pre-railroad, pre-motorized era through the 1700s-1800s, before rail and mechanized transport (WAR-290) begin to loosen it.

## Mechanism

An army in the field consumes food, fodder for draft and cavalry animals, and (once relevant) ammunition at a continuous rate that its own carrying capacity, local foraging opportunity, and any maintained supply line back to a secure base must together cover — falling short of that consumption rate doesn't just slow a campaign, it can force retreat or surrender regardless of tactical position. This is why the campaign-level decisions this domain's other entries describe (whether to besiege or bypass, WAR-050; how to coordinate multi-corps movement, WAR-210; whether a state can actually sustain a prolonged attritional commitment, WAR-100) all rest on a logistics capacity calculation none of them separately owns.

## Social consequence

Sustaining an army's logistics over any real distance or duration requires either a friendly, well-administered territory to draw supply from or a standing commissariat capable of moving and protecting a supply train through potentially hostile ground — a state's logistics capacity is, in this sense, as direct a measure of its administrative reach as its ability to field and pay soldiers at all (WAR-040), and a besieging or invading army's supply line is frequently as attractive and vulnerable a target as its combat forces.

## Regional specifics

Warring States Chinese military treatises treat supply-line length and vulnerability as an explicit, calculable strategic variable earlier and more systematically than comparable Mediterranean writing of the same era. Steppe cavalry armies largely sidestep this entry's fixed-supply-train problem entirely, since a mounted force can forage and graze its own herds across open pasture at a pace and range no supply-train-dependent settled army can match — a structurally different logistics model built on mobile self-sufficiency, not a more advanced version of the same one.

## Hook

A resource-and-range ceiling every campaign-level entry in this domain silently assumes is already solved — a besieging army's logistics viability (WAR-050), a general staff's operational planning (WAR-210), and a state's attritional endurance (WAR-100) all rest directly on a supply capacity this entry is the first to own on its own terms.
