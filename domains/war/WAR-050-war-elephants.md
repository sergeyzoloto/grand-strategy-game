Named in this domain's own battle-formation entry as part of Mauryan combined-arms doctrine but never covered on its own terms — a distinct battlefield arm whose value rests on psychological shock against troops and horses unfamiliar with the animal, at a high individual logistics and training cost.

## Timing

War elephants are a standard component of South Asian battle doctrine from at least -1000; the practice spreads west into the Hellenistic Mediterranean and North Africa from around -280, reaching its best-known single deployment in Hannibal's Alpine crossing (-218), before declining in Mediterranean use by around 200 as opposing armies develop reliable countermeasures.

## Mechanism

A war elephant's battlefield value rests substantially on shock and psychological effect: horses unfamiliar with the animal's size and smell can panic and become unmanageable, and untrained infantry facing an elephant charge for the first time frequently break before any actual harm occurs — an effect that degrades sharply once an opposing force has faced elephants before and developed specific countermeasures (fire, caltrops, targeted missile fire at the mahout), making the same animal far less decisive on a second encounter than a first.

## Social consequence

Maintaining a war-elephant corps requires sustained investment in capture, training, and feeding a large, slow-maturing animal — a standing cost structure closer to WAR-080's cavalry-maintenance burden than to mass-infantry equipping, restricting the practice to states wealthy and organized enough to sustain it continuously rather than raise it only for a single campaign.

## Regional specifics

Hannibal's crossing of the Alps with a war-elephant contingent is this record's most extreme test of the animal's logistics burden against its psychological-shock payoff, with most of the elephants dying from cold and terrain before ever reaching Italian battlefields — a case where the logistics cost consumed most of the capability before it could be tactically exploited at all. East Asia's comparatively limited adoption of the practice, despite proximity to elephant-using Southeast Asian polities, reflects a doctrinal choice to develop along mass-infantry and cavalry lines instead, not a lack of access to the animal.

## Hook

A shock-effect battlefield arm whose value degrades sharply once an opponent has faced it before and developed countermeasures, and whose high standing logistics cost (closer to WAR-080's cavalry burden than WAR-040's mass-infantry equipping) restricts it to states able to sustain the investment continuously.
