A weapon whose primary strategic value lies in never being used — credible
possession of overwhelming, unsurvivable retaliatory capability is meant to
prevent the very conflict it would otherwise be deployed in, inverting this
domain's usual logic that military capability's value is realized in combat.

## Timing

From the first use and immediate strategic aftermath of atomic weapons
(1945) through the Cold War's peak deterrence-doctrine development, into the
post-1991 multipolar proliferation era this entry's regional variants extend
into.

## Mechanism

Deterrence works only if both sides believe the other actually will
retaliate catastrophically if attacked, and that neither side can eliminate
the other's retaliatory capability in a first strike — this requires
secure second-strike capability (survivable delivery systems, redundant
command and control) and credible communication of resolve, converting
nuclear strategy into a psychological and information problem (touching
WAR-018's territory) as much as a technical one built on the underlying
physics and engineering (NAT, ENG's territory).

## Social consequence

Nuclear deterrence between great powers redirects military-industrial
competition and diplomatic energy (DIP's territory) toward arms control
negotiation, alliance credibility management, and proxy conflict (WAR-022)
rather than direct confrontation, since direct war between nuclear-armed
states risks catastrophic escalation neither side's political leadership can
credibly absorb — a strategic environment where avoiding a certain category
of conflict becomes as important a state objective as winning one.

## Regional specifics

The India-Pakistan deterrence relationship, established by near-simultaneous
1998 tests between two states with an existing conventional conflict history
and a shared, contested border, creates a structurally higher-friction
deterrence context than the geographically separated Cold War superpower
standoff, testing whether the same deterrence logic holds under conditions
of much shorter warning time and more frequent lower-level conventional
friction. South Africa's voluntary dismantlement of its arsenal before 1993
is this record's only case of a state actually giving up existing weapons
capability, illustrating that deterrence requires an ongoing adversary
relationship to make sense of maintaining — remove the adversary context and
the strategic logic for keeping the weapons can disappear too.

## Contested

Whether nuclear deterrence genuinely prevented great-power war during the
Cold War, or whether the resulting "long peace" is better explained by other
factors with nuclear weapons as a contributing but non-decisive element, is
unresolved among international relations scholars given the small historical
sample available. Model deterrence as a real but probabilistic effect
conditional on both sides maintaining credible second-strike capability and
clear communication of resolve, capable of failing under sufficiently
degraded conditions (miscommunication, leadership crisis) rather than as an
automatic guarantee that follows from weapons possession alone.

## Hook

Credible mutual second-strike capability redirects great-power competition
away from direct confrontation and toward arms control diplomacy (DIP) and
proxy conflict (WAR-022) instead — a deterrence effect that's real but
probabilistic, dependent on maintained communication and resolve, and
capable of simply becoming irrelevant (as with South Africa) once the
adversary relationship justifying it changes.
