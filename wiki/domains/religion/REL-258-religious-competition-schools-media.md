Once the state no longer guarantees religious truth, religious institutions have
to win the next generation and the public's attention for themselves. Schooling
and media are the two instruments, which makes them the lasting battleground of
the post-settlement era. Religious institutions can win this competition, and
frequently do.

## Timing

From France's Falloux law of 1850 and the Dutch school conflict from 1857,
through Deoband in 1866, Catholic radio schools in Latin America from 1947, and
Korean Christian broadcasting from 1954, to cassette sermons in the 1970s and
satellite preachers from 1996. Pentecostal media and universities follow from
the 1980s, and online worship becomes routine by 2020. The competition begins
wherever a secular settlement (REL-252) removes the state's guarantee, and it has
not ended anywhere it began.

## Mechanism

**Pluralism turns authority into something that must be won.** Under a
confessional state, religious formation is guaranteed. The child is baptised,
catechised, and schooled inside the established faith. After a secular
settlement nothing guarantees it. `religious authority` in the next generation
has to be produced, and it is produced where children are formed and where
adults pay attention. **This is competition under pluralism, not a reaction to
decline.** Most of it happens where no decline occurred: Deoband, Korean
megachurches, Pentecostal Brazil, Nigerian universities. Reading it as backlash
would bring the discredited universal secularisation thesis back in, which
REL-255 exists to keep out.

**Schools: the state took custody of children, so custody is the prize.**
Compulsory education (LAW-165) puts every child in an institution for years, and
the institution shapes language, loyalty and belief whether or not it intends to.
Once the state holds that custody, religious bodies must either get inside it,
fund an alternative, or contest what it teaches. Prior institutional state
decides the resolution, not a choice made in the conflict:

- **Funded pluralism.** The state pays for confessional schools on equal terms
  with its own. This is the Dutch Pacification of 1917. Religious authority is
  institutionalised, conflict ends, and each community's formation is
  guaranteed by state money rather than by establishment.
- **Exclusion and its erosion.** The state removes religion from its schools
  and the church builds a parallel system. France excluded religion in the
  1880s, funded the parallel system from 1959, and could not abolish it
  in 1984.
- **Donation-funded parallel provision** where the state is neutral or weak.
  Deoband, Catholic parochial networks, Hindu nationalist schools, and African
  church universities all fall here. Authority rests on voluntary funding,
  which makes these networks durable and hard to regulate.
- **Co-optation.** The state absorbs religious schooling into its own system
  and sets the curriculum, as with Turkey's imam-hatip schools, Egypt's
  al-Azhar and Pakistan's madrasa registration. The state gains legitimacy
  from religious formation and pays by making the curriculum a permanent
  political issue.
- **Litigation.** Where a constitution forbids establishment, courts decide
  what a state school may teach. The American school prayer and creationism
  cases are the leading examples.

**Media: broadcast reaches past every intermediary.** MED-130 establishes that
radio needs no literacy and no local distributor, and that it lowers `intermediary
dependence` for whoever holds the transmitter. That is exactly what a religious
movement without institutional control of schooling needs. It can reach adults
directly, past both the state's schools and the established clergy. MED-130 also
establishes that **the funding model determines the institution**. Listener
donation was the model radio evangelism invented, and it produces independent
preacher-entrepreneurs answerable to their audience rather than to a hierarchy or
a licence authority. That is why religious broadcasting routinely escapes both
state and church control. The finding is cited here rather than re-derived.

Each later medium changes who can compete:

- **Cassettes.** Almost no production cost, no transmitter, and every copy is
  a new distribution point. They favour insurgent movements against states
  with broadcast monopolies, as in Iran in 1978 and in Egypt.
- **Television.** It raises MED-140's `production cost floor`, so only
  wealthy churches can compete, or churches that buy a network. The
  Universal Church bought Rede Record, and American televangelism rests on
  donation income large enough to fund production.
- **Satellite.** It makes `information sovereignty` defensible only by
  visible political acts, so preachers reach audiences across states from
  outside them.
- **The network (MED-150).** It drops both costs toward zero again. Every
  congregation can publish, and authority re-concentrates on the preachers
  the platforms surface.

**The varieties of mobilisation stay distinct.** Religious movements are not one
category using the same tools:

- **American evangelical organisation** is a voluntary-association movement
  in a disestablished market. It moves from broadcasting to electoral
  organisation, from the Moral Majority in 1979 onward.
- **Islamic revivalist movements**, from the Muslim Brotherhood's schools and
  welfare networks to the cassette and satellite preachers, compete with
  states that are themselves claiming Islamic legitimacy.
- **Hindu nationalism** builds a school network and uses devotional
  television to make a religious identity into a national one.
- **Liberation theology** is a left-wing mobilisation run through radio
  schools and base communities from inside an established church.
- **Pentecostalism** is the largest numerical case. It is decentralised,
  donation-funded and media-heavy, and it grows fastest in Latin America and
  sub-Saharan Africa, where it builds its own schools, universities and
  channels.

## Social consequence

At household scale the competition is a series of concrete choices.

- Which school a child attends, and what that costs. A Dutch family chose
  among state-funded pillars. A Pakistani family often chose the madrasa
  because it fed and housed a child for free. A Nigerian family chose the
  church school because the state school had no teachers.
- Which preacher is playing in the taxi.
- Which channel is on in the evening.

Formation moves from the parish, which a family was assigned, to a market, which
a family chooses from, and religious identity becomes something a household
selects and signals.

For religious institutions the consequence is a shift in the kind of authority
that works. Authority based on office loses to authority based on reach. The
preacher with the audience competes with the bishop with the see, and often wins.
Hierarchical churches respond by building their own media: Vatican Radio from
1931, the Catholic radio schools, Korean church broadcasting. Otherwise they cede
the audience to independent preachers.

The state is drawn permanently into regulating the contest. It sets curricula,
licenses broadcasters, bans miracle broadcasts, registers madrasas, and arbitrates
school prayer. Each of those acts is itself a move in the competition. The
post-settlement state is neutral among religions in principle and a participant in
practice, because every rule about what schools may teach or broadcasters may say
favours some religious body.

Religious mobilisation and technological disenchantment frequently run together,
because both reject the authority of the same modernising elite. PHI-305 carries the
Iranian case, where the revolution joined rejection of imposed technocratic
modernisation to religious revival.

## Regional specifics

South Asia carries the schooling side most fully. Deoband shows a voluntary,
donation-funded madrasa network rebuilt under a neutral colonial state and
growing without state support for a century and a half. Hindu nationalist schools
show a religious-national movement using schooling to form the next generation,
competing directly with Christian mission schools in tribal districts. Pakistan
shows the state first expanding religious schooling and then trying to discipline
it. Devotional television from Doordarshan's 1987 Ramayan (MED-140) to the
satellite channels of the 2000s shows the media side reaching a population that
print never did.

The Middle East carries the media side most fully. Cassette sermons in Egypt and
Iran built a religious public beneath states that held every broadcast channel,
and the Iranian case turned that into revolution. Satellite preaching then carried
religious authority across every Arabic-speaking state at once. Turkey and Egypt
show co-optation of religious schooling as the alternative to exclusion, with the
curriculum becoming a standing political fight.

Latin America and sub-Saharan Africa carry the largest numerical case, and it
must not be omitted. Estimates of Pentecostal and charismatic Christians
worldwide run into the hundreds of millions by 2000, with the counting disputed
by definition (Anderson, _An Introduction to Pentecostalism_, 2004, ch. 1). In
Brazil a church bought a national network. In Nigeria churches built universities
where the state could not staff schools. Latin American liberation theology shows
an established church's own left wing competing through radio schools and base
communities, and Rome restraining it.

East Asia shows Korean Christianity growing through broadcasting and megachurches
in a country with no Christian establishment, and the People's Republic shows the
competition switched off by a state that holds both instruments by law.

Europe and the United States supply the classic school settlements. The Dutch
funded pillars and extended them to broadcasting. France excluded religion,
funded a parallel system and could not abolish it. American courts arbitrated
prayer and evolution, while Catholic parochial schools, protected since _Pierce v.
Society of Sisters_ (1925), ran the largest parallel system in the country. American
radio and television evangelism supplied the donation-funding model that other
regions' religious broadcasters adopted.

The competition extends a much older pattern. Vernacular print broke clerical
control over scripture in the sixteenth century (REL-230), and religious schools
existed long before any state school (REL-090). What changes after the settlement
is that the state is also in the market. It is the dominant schoolmaster, and it
is often the licensing authority for every channel.

## Hook

A standing competition for `religious authority` across two instruments. It opens
when a secular settlement removes the state's guarantee of religious formation,
and it does not depend on religion declining.

- **Schools:** LAW-165's state custody of children is the contested asset. The
  resolution is set by prior institutional state, not chosen:
  - Funded pluralism institutionalises religious authority and ends the
    conflict.
  - Exclusion produces a parallel system that erodes the exclusion over time.
  - A neutral or weak state gets donation-funded parallel networks that are
    durable and hard to regulate.
  - Co-optation trades curriculum control for a permanent curriculum
    dispute.
  - A non-establishment constitution sends the fight to the courts.
- **Media:** `broadcast reach` and falling `intermediary dependence` let
  movements with no school system reach adults directly. The funding model
  from MED-130 decides whether the resulting authority is institutional
  (licence, membership allocation) or entrepreneurial (listener donation).
  Each medium reprices entry:
  - Cassettes and radio are cheap and favour insurgents.
  - Television's `production cost floor` favours wealthy or network-owning
    churches.
  - Satellite contests `information sovereignty` across borders.
  - The network collapses both costs and re-concentrates reach on
    platform-surfaced preachers.

The state is never neutral in practice. Every curriculum rule and broadcast
licence is a move in the competition, and a state that holds both instruments by
coercion switches the mechanism off rather than winning it.
