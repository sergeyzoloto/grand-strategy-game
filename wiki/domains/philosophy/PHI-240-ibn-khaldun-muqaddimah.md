Ibn Khaldun argues group solidarity (asabiyyah) is what lets a tribal or
dynastic coalition seize and hold power, and that the same settled,
urbanized comfort successful rule produces systematically erodes the
asabiyyah that produced it — a genuinely cyclical model, structurally
opposed to nearly every other entry in this domain's implicit
progress-narrative, where decline is the predictable mechanical consequence
of success itself, not contingent failure.

## Timing

Ibn Khaldun completes the Muqaddimah (the theoretical "Introduction" to his
larger universal history) in 1377, drawing on his own direct experience of
14th-century Maghrebi and Andalusian dynastic politics as a court official,
jurist, and occasional political prisoner across multiple successive
regimes.

## Mechanism

Asabiyyah names the group cohesion — built through shared hardship, kinship
obligation, and the practical necessity of mutual defense that tribal or
frontier life imposes — that lets a coalition successfully seize and
consolidate political power; Ibn Khaldun argues this same cohesion
predictably erodes across roughly three to four generations once the
coalition settles into urbanized rule, as its descendants trade the hardship
and mutual dependence that built their solidarity for the comfort, luxury,
and specialized administrative delegation that successful rule affords —
converting asabiyyah into a resource that peaks at the moment of conquest and
then depletes as a direct, mechanical function of the very success that
accumulated it, rather than through any external military or economic
shock.

## Social consequence

Because asabiyyah's decline is internally generated by successful rule
itself rather than by external attack, a dynasty's growing vulnerability is,
on this account, largely self-inflicted and predictable in advance — the
same luxury, specialized bureaucracy, and reliance on paid rather than
kin-obligated troops that mark a dynasty's peak material success are exactly
the conditions Ibn Khaldun identifies as actively eroding the cohesion that
peak rests on, meaning a dynasty's most visibly successful phase is also,
mechanically, the beginning of its most acute vulnerability to conquest by a
newer, higher-cohesion outside group.

## Regional specifics

Ibn Khaldun draws the model directly from repeated, personally-observed
Maghrebi and Andalusian dynastic cycles — Almoravid, Almohad, and successor
regimes each rising through frontier tribal cohesion and each, within a few
generations, growing vulnerable to the next cohesive outside group in turn —
a regional pattern of recurring tribal-dynastic succession specific enough
that whether the model generalizes cleanly beyond it is itself the entry's
own live contested question.

## Contested

Whether asabiyyah cyclicality is a serious general social-scientific model or
a period-bound observation about specific Maghrebi dynastic patterns
Ibn Khaldun overgeneralized from is disputed among historians of Islamic
thought and comparative historiography. Model asabiyyah decline as a
contestable general mechanism available to any qualifying dynastic or
tribal-coalition state, not a confirmed universal law — a state with an
unusually strong alternative cohesion source (a durable bureaucratic or
ideological apparatus) is a real, modelable exception, not a disproof of the
underlying pattern.

## Hook

A branch and a pool: models political-cultural cohesion as a resource that
peaks at conquest and then predictably depletes as a direct function of the
very settled success that accumulated it — a genuine decline clock, distinct
from PHI-170's externally-imposed capability destruction, that any
state-level entry elsewhere in the wiki can check a dynastic or tribal
coalition's position against.
