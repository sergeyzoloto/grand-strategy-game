A population's willingness to accept institutional and technological change is not
constant and is not a function of the merits of the change on offer. It rises through
periods of visible improvement, collapses after catastrophes attributed to the same
expertise that promised improvement, and recovers slowly. Because trust falls faster
than it rises, the movement is a ratchet rather than a sine wave, and where it stands
governs what a state can propose.

## Timing

From the Enlightenment progress narratives of the later eighteenth century and
nineteenth-century positivism, through the First World War as the first mass
disconfirmation, interwar pessimism and technocracy, Hiroshima and the environmental and
pharmaceutical critiques of the 1960s, the development-project failures of the colonial
and postcolonial world, and Chernobyl, to the vaccine, climate and artificial
intelligence controversies of the early twenty-first century.

## Mechanism

**The claim.** How readily a population accepts a new vaccine, dam, crop, reactor or
institutional reform depends less on that proposal than on the population's recent
experience of expertise in general. This entry names that state `expert trust`: a
population's willingness to accept change proposed on the authority of experts and in the
name of progress. It is distinct from `intellectual authority`, the standing a school
of thought holds among the learned, and it is population-facing: a scientific community
can hold high intellectual authority among elites while the public's expert trust is
collapsing.

**The cycle.** It has three phases:

- **Confidence.** A period of visible improvement — railways, vaccination, electric
  light, antibiotics, rising harvests — builds confidence that expertise delivers, and
  states can propose large changes on its authority.
- **Disconfirmation.** A catastrophe attributable to that same expertise collapses trust.
  The collapse is not confined to the domain that failed: it extends to unrelated
  proposals made on the same authority.
- **Persistence.** The loss outlasts the event by years or a generation, and rebuilding
  trust takes far longer than losing it did.

**The asymmetry is what makes it a ratchet.** A single visible failure destroys more
confidence than a decade of success builds. Slovic's work on risk perception found that
trust-destroying events are more noticeable, more credible and weigh more than
trust-building ones, and that successes are mostly invisible because their result is
nothing happening. So a population's expert trust tends to step down sharply and climb
back gradually. The spillover is as important as the asymmetry. After Britain's mad cow
crisis, the public that had been told beef was safe refused reassurance about
genetically modified crops, a different technology from different scientists.

**Conditions for a disconfirmation.** Not every disaster counts. The catastrophe must be
visible and attributable to expertise rather than to nature or enemies, and its
consequences are larger where the expertise is identified with the state or a foreign
power, because the loss of trust then attaches to the regime too.

**The first mass disconfirmation: 1914-1918.** The confidence in method of the
seventeenth century (PHI-250) became, in nineteenth-century positivism, a belief that
science and industry would improve human life without limit, and scientific societies
(NAT-150) and pragmatism's confidence that ideas are tools that work (PHI-370) belonged to
that climate. The First World War used the chemistry, engineering and organisation of
industrial society to kill on a scale nobody had imagined, through the total mobilisation
that WAR-290 carries. The same expertise that had promised improvement produced machine
guns, gas and the Somme. Spengler's pessimism found a mass readership; Liang Qichao carried
the verdict to China. The interwar years produced the counter-movement too: technocracy in
the United States, Soviet planning, modernist architecture and the managed economy, each
promising that expertise properly directed would succeed where the old order had failed.

**The postwar defining case: the bomb.** The physics of the early twentieth century
(NAT-210) led within a generation to the weapons of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the
standoff WAR-330 carries. Physics was simultaneously the most prestigious science and the
source of the greatest threat. Atoms for Peace in 1953 offered nuclear power to the world, and in 1954 the
chairman of the US Atomic Energy Commission spoke of electricity too cheap to meter; Three Mile Island in 1979 and Chernobyl in 1986 turned that promise into a
liability. Nuclear power became the clearest case of trust lost faster than it was built,
long after reactor safety had improved.

**Solutions that generate their own problems.** From the 1960s the critique shifted from
spectacular disasters to cumulative ones:

- **Antibiotics** (HLT-330) are the clean instance. Each use selects for resistance, so the
  solution creates its own problem class, as Fleming warned in 1945.
- **Pesticides.** Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) turned DDT, which had nearly
  eradicated malaria in many regions (HLT-265), into the emblem of chemical harm.
- **Drugs.** Thalidomide, prescribed to pregnant women from 1957 to 1961, caused
  thousands of birth defects and led to stricter drug regulation.
- **The Green Revolution** (AGR-280) raised yields and was criticised for inequality,
  input dependence and ecological cost.

**Colonial and postcolonial versions are essential, not variants.** For many populations
modernisation arrived as an external imposition. Where the expertise belonged to a
colonial power or an authoritarian modernising state, there was no domestic period of
confidence to fall from, and failures were read as further evidence of foreign or
authoritarian rule:

- **Colonial rule.** Gandhi rejected machine civilisation in 1909. The groundnut scheme
  failed in Tanganyika.
- **Postcolonial showpieces.** Development-state dams became focal points of opposition,
  from Narmada to the dams of the megaproject regimes (ENG-075), and Bhopal became the
  emblem of industrial risk exported to the poor (ENG-166).
- **Authoritarian modernisation.** In Iran, the Shah's modernisation fell with the Shah,
  and the rejection of imposed technology joined the religious mobilisation REL-258
  carries — religious revival and technological disenchantment frequently run together
  because both reject the authority of the same modernising elite.
- **Soviet utopianism.** Electrification and Sputnik ended in Chernobyl and the Aral
  Sea.

**The critical tradition supplied the language.** The culture-industry critique of
PHI-410 read mass technology as domination, and the structuralist and existentialist
debates of PHI-400 questioned the autonomous rational subject in whose name progress was
claimed. These arguments did not cause disenchantment, but they gave it a vocabulary
when events arrived.

**Contemporary.** Vaccine hesitancy (vaccination is carried in HLT-250), contested climate
response and anxiety about artificial intelligence show the same mechanism: acceptance of
expert-backed change split along lines of prior trust. They are noted here, not analysed.

## Social consequence

At human scale the cycle decides whether a proposal is heard as help or threat:

- **A Londoner in 1851.** They visit the Crystal Palace and expect their children to live
  better through machinery.
- **A French family after 1918.** They lost a son to the machine-gun and artillery war, and
  read that civilisation has failed.
- **A mother in 1962.** She was prescribed thalidomide and now distrusts every new drug.
- **A Minamata fishing family.** They were told for twelve years that the chemical works was
  not poisoning them.
- **A Narmada valley villager.** They are to be moved for a dam built in the name of
  national development.
- **A British shopper in 1998.** They refuse genetically modified food because they
  remember being told beef was safe.

The state's room to act follows. Where expert trust is high, governments can impose
vaccination, build reactors and remodel agriculture. Where it has collapsed, the same
proposals meet resistance regardless of their merit, and the population that suffered the
disconfirmation often carries the distrust across generations.

## Regional specifics

The cycle runs in every region, but its shape depends on who owned the expertise:

- **Europe** supplies the progress narrative, the first mass disconfirmation of 1914-18,
  interwar pessimism, and the BSE-to-GM spillover.
- **The Soviet Union** supplies technological utopianism ending in Chernobyl.
- **East Asia** supplies China's importation of European disenchantment in 1923 before
  its own industrialisation, the Great Leap's technical catastrophe, and Japan's case
  running from Hiroshima through Minamata to Fukushima.
- **South Asia** supplies Gandhi's rejection of machine civilisation and the turn of
  Nehru's dams, Bhopal and the Green Revolution into indictments.
- **The Middle East** supplies Iran's revolution against imposed technocratic
  modernisation joined to religious mobilisation.
- **Africa** supplies colonial and postcolonial high-modernist schemes met by populations
  whose trust had never been built.

The absence is the Old Order Amish, whose communal rule rather than expertise's public
record decides what change to accept, so the cycle does not run.

## Contested

Whether this is a real mechanism is a fair objection:

- **Retrospective reading.** Optimists and pessimists appear in every decade, and the
  cycle may be imposed on unrelated events.
- **Polarisation, not oscillation.** American survey confidence in science has been
  broadly stable since the 1970s while trust diverged between groups.
- **Terminology.** Weber's disenchantment is the secular loss of magic, a different
  process.

The asymmetry has better support (Slovic 1993). Model a fall in `expert trust` as
conditional on a visible, attributable catastrophe, with spillover scaled by the
expertise's identification with the state or a foreign power, and baselines that can
differ between groups.

## Hook

**Expert trust.** A population's `expert trust` sets how much change a state can
propose on the authority of expertise, independent of the proposal's merit.

- **Gains.** It rises slowly with visible improvement.
- **Losses.** It falls sharply after a visible catastrophe attributable to expertise,
  and the fall spreads to unrelated domains and persists for years.
- **Asymmetry.** Losses outweigh equivalent gains, so the stat ratchets down and climbs
  back gradually.

**Identification.** Where expertise is identified with the ruler or a foreign power,
a disconfirmation lowers the regime's legitimacy too, and populations that met
modernisation as imposition start with low trust and can join technological rejection to
religious mobilisation.

**Self-generated problems.** Some solutions create their own problem class — resistance,
pesticide harm — and erode trust cumulatively rather than in one event.

**Groups.** Baselines can differ between groups within a population, so disenchantment
can appear as polarisation rather than a uniform swing.

**Absence.** A community that decides on change by its own rule is insulated from the
cycle in both directions.
