Bhakti — devotion — holds that a personal, loving relationship with a god is a
sufficient path to liberation. It requires neither Sanskrit learning, nor the services
of Brahmin ritual specialists, nor birth into the right caste. Each of those three gates
had restricted access to religious authority in South Asia. Bhakti routed around all
three at once, and that combination is what made it a mass movement rather than a
philosophical school.

## Timing

From the Tamil Alvars and Nayanars of the sixth to ninth centuries, through Ramanuja's
theology of devotion in the eleventh and twelfth, Basava's Virashaiva movement in
Karnataka in the twelfth, and the Marathi Varkari poets from Jnaneshwar in 1290, to the
north Indian and Bengali saints of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries — Kabir,
Ravidas, Mirabai, Chaitanya, Sankardev and Tulsidas — and the emergence of the Sikh
tradition from Guru Nanak to the compilation of the Adi Granth in 1604. Tukaram, who
dies in 1649, is the late Maharashtrian case.

## Mechanism

**Devotion as a sufficient path.** The Upanishads (PHI-040) located liberation in
knowledge of the self's identity with Brahman, and Śaṅkara (PHI-190) defended that path
of knowledge with rigorous argument. The older Vedic path ran through ritual performed
by Brahmins in Sanskrit. Bhakti claimed a third path, already named in the Bhagavad Gita:
love for a personal god — Vishnu as Krishna or Rama, Shiva, the Goddess — returned by
the god's grace. Ramanuja gave it a philosophical foundation in the eleventh century: in
his qualified non-dualism the individual soul is really distinct from God though part of
God's body, so there is someone to love and someone to be loved, and devotion rather than
the dissolution of the self is the highest state.

**Three gates at once.** Religious authority in South Asia rested on three
restrictions:

- **Language.** Scripture and ritual were in Sanskrit, a language most people did not
  speak and few could learn (MED-040).
- **Specialists.** Sacrifice and temple ritual required Brahmin priests.
- **Birth.** Caste determined who could hear the Veda, enter a temple or eat with whom
  (HLT-130).

A new philosophical school challenges one of these. Bhakti's claim that love alone
suffices makes all three irrelevant to the goal, which is why its poets could be
weavers, tailors, leatherworkers, untouchables, princesses and maidservants. That raises
`salvific access` — the share of a population that can reach the tradition's highest
goal — as sharply as any doctrine in the corpus.

**Vernacular composition.** Bhakti poetry was composed in Tamil, Kannada, Marathi,
Hindi, Bengali, Assamese, Gujarati and Punjabi. That did two things. It reached people
who could not read, because the songs were sung, memorised and performed in the language
they spoke. And it made those languages vehicles for serious religious content, which
several had not been before. MED-070 carries vernacular translation as the instrument
that dissolves a sacred-language gate and creates the vernacular as a medium. Bhakti is
the stronger version of that mechanism: the vernacular works were not translations of a
Sanskrit canon but original compositions, so the vernacular did not borrow authority from
a source text — it held its own. Jnaneshwar's Marathi Gita commentary is the partial
exception, and the Tamil Vaishnavas eventually called their hymns the "Tamil Veda".

**Partial dissolution of status barriers.** This must be stated carefully. The poets
included women and low-caste and untouchable figures, and the traditions explicitly
asserted that devotion superseded birth. Some built institutions that acted on the
assertion: the Varkari pilgrimage walked by all castes together, the Lingayat rejection
of caste, the Sikh langar where all sit and eat together. But the same traditions show
the limits. Chokhamela was kept out of the temple whose god he sang; Nandanar entered
only through fire; the Pandharpur temple was opened to Dalits only in 1947; and the
Lingayats, Kabirpanthis and Ravidassias became caste-linked communities themselves.
Whether bhakti changed society is contested. The model should treat the outcome as
conditional: salvific access rose everywhere bhakti spread, and it became social change
where the movement built cross-caste institutions and was lost where it did not.

**The institutional response.** Orthodoxy did not simply resist bhakti; it absorbed it:

- **Temple incorporation.** The Tamil hymns became Chola temple liturgy, sung by
  professional singers, and the saints received shrines in the temples (REL-020). The
  poet-saint became a figure of the institution he had bypassed.
- **Sanskrit re-encoding.** The Bhagavata Purana, composed in Sanskrit in south India
  around the ninth or tenth century, carried Alvar-style devotion back into the sacred
  language, and Chaitanya's Goswamis wrote their movement's theology in Sanskrit.
- **Sampradāyas.** Ramanuja's Sri Vaishnava lineage, Madhva's, Vallabha's and others
  became organised teaching lineages with their own monasteries, succession, scriptures
  and commentaries.

The movement that routed around the gates was thus followed by new gates: lineage
initiation, authorised teachers, and the Brahmin theologians of devotional traditions.
Pilgrimage (REL-160) became one of the channels through which bhakti was organised at
scale.

**The encounter with Sufism.** Sufi orders, above all the Chishtis, spread across north
India from the thirteenth century, teaching love of God through a spiritual master and
devotional music. Bhakti and Sufi poets shared a vocabulary — love-longing, the guru or
pir, remembrance of the divine name, scorn for ritual and for religious authorities — and
they circulated in the same towns. The Adi Granth places Sheikh Farid beside Kabir and
Namdev. Sufi brotherhoods organised devotion as lay membership in a lineage (REL-200) much
as sampradāyas did. How far this was synthesis is a live dispute, and the entry should
record the shared idiom without asserting a merged tradition.

**Distinct from universalism and Mahāyāna.** REL-050 carries universalism, which removes
birth as a condition of membership. Bhakti operates inside a tradition whose membership
was already defined by birth, and removes birth as a condition of the goal. Mahāyāna's
bodhisattva ideal (PHI-175) lifted the ceiling of renunciation on the goal; bhakti lifted
the ceilings of language, specialist and birth. Pure Land devotion to Amitābha and bhakti
devotion to Krishna are close parallels in what they offered householders.

## Social consequence

At human scale bhakti put religion in the mouths of people who had been its audience:

- **A Tamil woman in the ninth century.** She sings Andal's hymns, composed by a woman in
  her own language, as a bride longing for Vishnu.
- **A Lingayat artisan in Kalyana.** They wear their own linga and need no temple or priest.
- **A Varkari farmer.** They walk to Pandharpur each year in a procession where castes walk
  together, singing Tukaram's abhangas.
- **A weaver in Benares.** They hear Kabir mock both the Brahmin and the mullah in the Hindi
  of the bazaar.
- **A Punjabi villager.** They eat at the langar beside people whose touch would have
  polluted their food at home.
- **A Mahar in Pandharpur.** They sing Chokhamela's songs outside a temple they may not enter.

Bhakti gave the excluded a religious voice and a language of equal standing before God.
It also left most of them where they were in the social order, and in some cases gave
their exclusion a new form.

## Regional specifics

Bhakti spread across South Asia in regional currents, each in its own language and with
its own institutions:

- **Tamil country:** the Alvars and Nayanars, whose hymns became temple liturgy under the
  Cholas.
- **Karnataka:** Basava's Lingayats, who rejected caste and temples and then became a
  community of their own.
- **Maharashtra:** the Varkaris, from Jnaneshwar to Tukaram, organised around the
  Pandharpur pilgrimage.
- **North India:** Kabir, Ravidas, Mirabai and Tulsidas, in Hindi and Rajasthani.
- **Bengal and Assam:** Chaitanya's congregational singing and Sankardev's priestless
  prayer halls.
- **Punjab:** Guru Nanak and the Sikh tradition, whose scripture gathers poets from across
  the milieu, Muslim and Hindu.

Sufism ran alongside as a parallel devotional current.

The absence is Jainism, which in the same society had lay devotion without the claim that
devotion saves, so liberation stayed reserved to the renunciant.

## Contested

Two disputes:

- **Social change.** Protest readings treat bhakti as a revolt against caste and
  Brahmin authority. Sceptical readings stress that orthodoxy absorbed the saints and
  movements hardened into castes. Novetzke reads it as a new vernacular public sphere.
- **Unity and syncretism.** Hawley argues "the bhakti movement" as one wave is a modern
  construction. Tara Chand's synthesis with Sufism is contested by later scholars who see
  shared idiom without shared doctrine.

Model status change as conditional on cross-caste institutions, and bhakti and Sufism as
parallel currents.

## Hook

**Three gates at once.** Devotion as a sufficient path bypasses sacred language, ritual
specialists and birth together, raising `salvific access` sharply wherever a devotional
movement spreads.

**Vernacular.** Original vernacular composition creates a religious medium with its own
authority, a stronger form of MED-070's translation mechanism.

**Conditional social effect.** Raised access becomes social change only where the
movement builds institutions open across castes — pilgrimage, congregation, communal
kitchen. Otherwise it is absorbed.

**Institutional response.** Orthodoxy incorporates the saints into temples, re-encodes
devotion in Sanskrit, and organises lineages that restore gates of initiation and
authority, partly re-closing access over time.

**Hardening.** An anti-caste movement can become an endogamous community of its own.

**Parallel currents.** Sufism and bhakti share vocabulary and audiences without merging,
and a scripture like the Adi Granth can gather both.
