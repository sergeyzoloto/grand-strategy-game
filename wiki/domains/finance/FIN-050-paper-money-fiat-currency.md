Currency backed by state trust and legal mandate rather than by the
commodity value of the material it's printed on — a trust problem in its
own right, distinct from and prior to what happens once multiple states
each issuing their own paper currency need some way to value them against
each other (FIN-250's territory).

## Timing

Chinese paper currency issuance dates to 806, maturing into large-scale
circulation under the Song and Yuan dynasties; European paper currency
experiments follow much later and less steadily, reaching a genuinely
stable, trusted everyday medium of exchange only by the early 20th century,
shortly before the international coordination problem multiple such
currencies create (FIN-250) becomes its own separate, formalized concern.

## Mechanism

Paper money removes the physical-commodity constraint the gold standard
(FIN-120) imposes, letting a state's money supply expand independent of how
much bullion it physically holds — this solves the gold standard's rigidity
problem but reintroduces the trust problem in a different form: a paper
currency's value now depends entirely on confidence in the issuing state's
restraint and credibility, with nothing physical backing it if that
confidence collapses (feeding directly into FIN-220's inflationary-spiral
mechanic).

## Social consequence

A paper currency's value being a function of trust in state restraint,
rather than of a physical commodity anyone can verify independently,
concentrates enormous quiet power in whichever institution controls that
currency's issuance — a power that cuts both ways, capable of smoothing a
crisis through controlled expansion or destroying savings through reckless
overissuance, with ordinary currency holders having no direct way to verify
which is actually happening until its effects (FIN-220's inflation, or its
absence) show up in prices.

## Regional specifics

China's paper-money maturity under the Song and Yuan, using state-backed
promissory notes as primary circulating currency at imperial scale
centuries before comparable European practice, is one of the clearest cases
in this record of a "modern" financial instrument's usual Western
attribution understating real prior achievement elsewhere. Europe's own
much later and rockier paper-currency experience — John Law's catastrophic
1720 note scheme being the paradigm cautionary case — shows the instrument's
trust-dependent fragility was a real, repeatedly-learned lesson rather than
merely a design detail Europe skipped past quickly.

## Hook

Paper money trades the gold standard's (FIN-120) physical rigidity for a
trust-dependent flexibility that can smooth a crisis or spiral into
`price level` collapse (FIN-220) depending entirely on issuer restraint —
a domestic trust problem complete in itself, with the further question of
how multiple states' independently-issued currencies get valued against
each other left to FIN-250's separate international coordination mechanic.
