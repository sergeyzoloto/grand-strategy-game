Mercantilism treated a nation's stock of gold and silver as the measure of its wealth
and trade as a contest in which one state's surplus was another's deficit. The object
of policy was a persistent export surplus and the specie it brought in. For almost
three centuries European states built the colonial trading system on that premise,
while the Ottoman, Qing, Tokugawa and Mughal states ran their trade on quite
different ones.

## Timing

From the Portuguese crown monopoly of the Cape route after 1500 and Spain's Casa de
Contratacion from 1503, through the Spanish fleet system from the 1560s, the English
Navigation Acts of 1651 and 1660 and Colbert's administration from 1665 to 1683, to
Hume's specie-flow argument of 1752 and Smith's critique in 1776. The Ottoman,
Qing and Tokugawa regimes run through the same period on their own premises. The
instruments outlived the doctrine: the Navigation Acts were repealed in 1849.

## Mechanism

**The premise and what follows from it.** Ocean navigation (MAR-160) gave European
states distant trades in Asia and the Americas worth closing by law. If wealth is bullion and the world's stock of
trade is roughly fixed, a state grows richer only by taking trade from its rivals and
bringing in more gold and silver than it sends out. Everything else follows
deductively:

- **Colonies** exist to supply raw materials and buy finished goods from the home
  country.
- **Domestic manufacture** is protected, because finished goods earn more than raw
  materials.
- **Re-export** is encouraged, so the state earns on goods it does not make.
- **Bullion export** is restricted.
- **Shipping** is reserved to national vessels, so freight earnings stay at home and
  the merchant fleet trains sailors for the navy.

The share of a state's trade legally closed to foreigners — its `trade reservation` —
is the policy lever, and the `bullion stock` is the score.

**War finance dressed as economics.** The doctrine was more than a bad theory because
of the fiscal position underneath it. An army campaigning abroad had to be paid in
coin its soldiers and suppliers would accept, and gunpowder armies and fortress
warfare (WAR-130) made campaigns very expensive. Spain paid the Army of Flanders in
silver shipped through Genoese bankers. England and later Britain paid subsidies to
continental allies. A state without specie could not campaign, and credit (FIN-110)
was a way of borrowing against specie still to come. A trade surplus was therefore a
war chest, and the policy of accumulating one was a policy of preparing to fight.

**The instruments are the modellable content.**

- **Cargo reservation.** The Navigation Acts closed colonial trade to foreign ships and
  pushed the Dutch carriers out. Spain's fleets kept the Indies trade in licensed
  hands. The immediate consequence was smuggling, which MAR-150 carries: every
  reservation created a price gap that contraband filled, from Dutch Curacao to
  Buenos Aires, where the legal route to Spain ran through Panama. The coastal
  trade (MAR-020) that ships had always carried became subject to national
  flags.
- **Chartered monopoly companies.** COM-150 carries the East India companies as
  quasi-sovereign actors. The doctrine supplied their rationale: a national company
  with a monopoly would keep a distant trade and its profits out of foreign hands.
- **Bullion export prohibitions, and their failure.** Asian goods had to be bought
  with silver, because Europe produced little that Asia wanted. The East India trade
  was therefore the system's internal contradiction: a chartered national company
  exporting the bullion the doctrine said must stay home. Thomas Mun, a director of
  the English company, argued in England's Treasure by Forraign Trade (written in the
  1620s, published 1664) that silver sent to Asia returned multiplied when the goods
  were re-exported, so the balance mattered only in total. The company had to argue
  its way around its own system's premise. The flow of silver out of the Americas
  (COM-140) made the contradiction bearable.
- **Colonial manufacturing bans.** British laws on colonial wool, hats and iron, and the
  French Exclusif, deliberately kept colonies producing raw materials. COM-160
  carries the commodity dependence that followed. England also banned Indian printed
  calicoes in 1700 and 1721 to protect its own weavers.
- **Tariffs and drawbacks.** Protective tariffs raised the price of foreign
  manufactures, and drawbacks refunded duties when imports were re-exported, making a
  port an entrepot (MAR-185). COM-190 carries protection and tariff wars from 1815;
  its instruments were inherited from this regime, not invented after it.

**Other premises in the same era.** Mercantilism was a choice, and the non-European
regimes show what else was available:

- **Ottoman provisionism** is the inverse. The state's concern was abundant, cheap
  supply for the capital and the army. It encouraged imports, restricted exports of
  grain and strategic goods, set market prices by official lists, and granted
  European merchants low duties. That was a coherent theory of what economic policy
  was for, not an absence of one.
- **Qing restriction** served order and security. The Canton system of 1757 confined
  foreigners to one port and licensed merchants, while China absorbed silver freely.
  DIP-075 carries it as a ritual gate on trade.
- **Tokugawa control** confined foreign trade to Nagasaki and a few domain channels
  for security and religious reasons from the 1630s. Once Japanese silver and copper
  output fell, Arai Hakuseki's regulations of 1715 capped ships and metal exports —
  a bullion restriction reached by a different route, from the depletion of home
  mines rather than a theory of wealth.

The branch variable is fiscal position: states whose armies fought abroad and whose
revenues depended on trade needed specie and adopted the premise; land-revenue
empires with their armies at home did not.

**The critique and its limits.** Hume's essay "Of the Balance of Trade" (1752) showed
the bullion premise was self-defeating. A country that accumulates specie sees its
`price level` rise, which makes its exports dear and imports cheap, so the surplus
corrects itself. Smith (1776) argued that wealth lay in productive capacity, not metal,
and that trade benefits both sides. FIN-140 carries the policy that followed. But
mercantilist policy achieved several of its actual objectives. Britain's reserved trade
built the shipping and seamen of the largest navy in the world. The Anglo-Dutch Wars
and the Navigation Acts took carrying trade from the Dutch. Protection against Indian
cotton textiles gave British cotton manufacture a sheltered home market before it
could compete. And the infant-industry argument came back as respectable doctrine in
Hamilton's Report on Manufactures (1791) and List's National System (1841). The shift
to free trade was contested and conditional, not a correction of an error.

## Social consequence

The system reached people through prices, prohibitions and enforcement:

- **A Seville merchant.** They hold a licence to the Indies trade and profit from
  scarcity in the colonies.
- **A Boston merchant in the 1750s.** They buy molasses from French islands in breach
  of the law and bribe or evade customs officers, as their neighbours do.
- **A London weaver in 1700.** They riot against Indian calicoes, and Parliament bans
  them.
- **A French artisan at the Gobelins.** They work in a royal manufacture under
  privilege and state inspection.
- **An Istanbul baker.** They sell bread at a price fixed by the kadi's list, under
  a regime designed to keep the city supplied rather than to earn a surplus.
- **A Chinese Cohong merchant at Canton.** They are licensed to deal with foreigners
  and held responsible for their conduct.

Mercantilism concentrated gains on licensed merchants, shipowners and protected
manufacturers, and pushed costs onto colonial consumers, foreign carriers and anyone
whose trade the law excluded. The smuggler was its most common rebel.

Specie shortage also produced default: Philip II's armies abroad were paid with
bankers' loans against silver fleets, and when the fleets failed the crown suspended
payment. FIN-067 carries sovereign default.

## Regional specifics

The European regimes varied among themselves:

- **Colbert's France** is the developed type: manufactures, tariffs, a company, a navy
  and a closed colonial trade built as one programme.
- **England** reserved shipping, enumerated colonial goods and restricted colonial
  manufacture.
- **Spain** ran a single-port monopoly and convoy system built around silver.
- **Portugal** ran the Cape route as a crown monopoly and sold passes in Asian waters.
- **The Dutch Republic** allowed bullion export and preached free seas at home while
  enforcing monopoly in the Moluccas, and was the target the other systems were built
  against.

Outside Europe the regimes rested on different premises, and they are not variants of
the European case:

- **The Ottoman Empire's** provisionism inverted the policy from a coherent premise of
  supply.
- **Qing China** restricted trade for order and absorbed silver freely.
- **Tokugawa Japan** restricted trade for security, then limited bullion exports when
  its own mines declined.

The absence is Mughal India. A land-revenue empire with low customs, open to merchants
and receiving silver for its textiles, had no fiscal reason for the premise.

## Contested

Two disputes:

- **Coherence.** Heckscher treated mercantilism as a unified system. Coleman argued it
  was a label invented by its critics. Later work reads it as a shared economic
  language rather than a doctrine.
- **Effects.** Its central premise was wrong, but British shipping, naval power and
  protected textile manufacture plausibly served British growth.

Model mercantilism as a bundle of separable instruments adopted where fiscal position
made specie scarce, and the move to free trade as a contested shift whose payoff
depends on competitive position.

## Hook

**The premise branches on fiscal position.** A state whose armies campaign abroad and
whose revenue depends on trade should be drawn to accumulate `bullion stock`; a
land-revenue empire with its armies at home should not.

**Instruments.** Mercantilism is a set of separable instruments, each with a cost:

- **Cargo reservation** raises `trade reservation`, building shipping and sailors, and
  generates smuggling in proportion to the price gap.
- **Chartered companies** hold distant trades as national monopolies (COM-150).
- **Bullion export bans** fail wherever the imports a state wants must be paid in
  silver.
- **Colonial manufacturing bans** trade colonial development for home industry and
  produce commodity dependence.
- **Tariffs and drawbacks** protect manufacture and reward the entrepot.

**Self-correction.** Accumulating specie raises the `price level` and erodes the
surplus, so the premise defeats itself over time.

**Alternatives.** Provisionism, order-first restriction and open land-revenue trade
are equal alternatives with their own payoffs, not stages before mercantilism.

**The critique.** The shift to free trade pays best for the leading economy and can
cost a catching-up one (FIN-140).
