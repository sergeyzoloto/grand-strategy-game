A bank holds only a fraction of deposits as immediately available cash,
lending the rest out — which works perfectly until enough depositors
suspect it won't, at which point the rush to withdraw first becomes
self-fulfilling regardless of whether the bank was actually sound to begin
with.

## Timing

Recurring panics punctuate 19th-century banking history in the United
States and Europe (notably 1837 and 1907), driving the eventual push toward
central banking's lender-of-last-resort function (FIN-100); the pattern
recurs in different form at the international level with the 1997 Asian
Financial Crisis.

## Mechanism

Fractional-reserve banking is stable as long as only a normal, predictable
fraction of depositors want their money back at once — a panic occurs when
depositors' fear that a bank might fail becomes self-validating, since a
bank facing simultaneous mass withdrawal demands cannot pay everyone even if
its underlying loans are perfectly sound, converting a mere rumor or
suspicion into an actual failure through the rush to exit alone. A credible
lender of last resort (FIN-100) interrupts this specifically by guaranteeing
liquidity is available, removing the rational basis for the initial rush.

## Social consequence

Panics fall hardest on ordinary depositors and borrowers who have no way to
distinguish a genuinely insolvent bank from a solvent one experiencing a
self-fulfilling run, and the resulting credit contraction as surviving banks
pull back lending to protect their own reserves spreads damage well beyond
the banks that actually failed — a panic's economic cost routinely exceeds
the actual underlying losses at the failed institutions themselves.

## Regional specifics

The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis reproduces the same self-fulfilling exit
dynamic at the level of international capital flows and currency pegs
rather than individual depositor bank runs — investors rushing to
withdraw capital and abandon pegged currencies before others did, spreading
across multiple economies through financial linkage rather than depositor
psychology at a single institution. India's greater insulation from that
same crisis, owing to more restrictive capital controls and less
international financial integration, shows reduced financial openness can
function as an incidental panic firewall, complicating any simple
"more open markets are always better" reading of financial liberalization.

## Hook

A banking or currency panic is self-fulfilling independent of underlying
solvency — the rush to exit first is individually rational and collectively
catastrophic, arrestable by a credible lender-of-last-resort backstop
(FIN-100) or, per the Indian case, simply by capital controls limiting how
fast the panic can actually propagate in the first place.
