The state sells the right to collect a tax or customs revenue to a private bidder, who pays the agreed sum up front and keeps whatever he gathers above it.

## Timing

Roman tax farming is traditionally dated to 215 BCE, when the censors let the collection of revenue from newly held territory to companies of publicani (Livy, Ab Urbe Condita, books 23-24; the year and the Sicilian detail to be checked against the text). The Republic let these contracts at Rome for the censors' five-year period. Under the Empire, direct provincial taxes passed largely to imperial procurators, while customs (portoria) stayed with lessees for longer (Badian, Publicans and Sinners, 1972; Hopkins, "Taxes and Trade in the Roman Empire", JRS 1980). Ottoman iltizam leases are documented from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and the lifetime malikane form was introduced in the 1690s (Genc on the malikane system in Ottoman finance; exact year to be checked, see Pamuk, A Monetary History of the Ottoman Empire, 2000). The French Ferme générale was consolidated in 1726 and abolished in 1791 (both dates confirmed in general sources; the Bosher reference, French Finances 1770-1795, 1970, still needs its page checked). The end date of 1856 is an editorial marker, not a single event: Tanzimat decrees from 1839 aimed to replace Ottoman tax farms with salaried collectors, and the change was gradual. Some farmed revenues survived past 1856, and the creditor-run Ottoman Public Debt Administration of 1881 (FIN-067) revived the farm model under foreign control.

## Mechanism

The contract replaces a variable collection cost with a fixed price. The state does not need to know who owes what, only how much of a revenue head it wants this season. That is why the device appears where assessment is weak. The farmer's bid is a forecast of the base, so the state buys foresight and immediate liquidity from a bidder who carries the forecast error.

Three effects follow.

First, the upfront payment is credit. The farmer pays the treasury at the start of the period and recovers it over the year. In France and the Ottoman lands the advance was often borrowed from financiers, so the state's short-term borrowing rested on the farmer's balance sheet. A poor harvest then becomes a failed bid instead of a visible fiscal shortfall. This raises sovereign credit capacity in the short term, and it moves the risk of default onto private intermediaries, which is the root of the debt links in FIN-067.

Second, the farmer keeps the residual: collections minus the bid. Every unit collected above the assessed amount is pure surplus (farm surplus), so the farmer's incentive is to push collection past the legal base. The over-extraction rate is highest where the state cannot see the base and where a local collector can enforce arrears. Competition at auction limits the bid but not how the farmer collects once he holds the lease. The state's only control is audit, and audit needs the very assessment the device was meant to avoid.

Third, the bid is a yield estimate. Overbidding ends in default and a re-auction; underbidding loses the lease to a rival. Successful bids rose when the previous lessee's surplus was large, so surplus was recycled into the next bid, and the state captured much of the farmer's margin by re-auctioning at the higher price.

States kept the system because it needed no cadastre, no salaried collectors and no inspection apparatus, and it delivered revenue before the year began. States dropped it because over-extraction drove taxpayers into flight and revolt and shrank the base, because the cost of inspecting the farmer grew until it matched the saving, and because a lifetime or hereditary lease turned the collector into a local power with a clientele of his own (the Ottoman ayan).

## Social consequence

The household meets a private collector with a claim to more than the assessed rate and, often, powers of distraint and collective liability for village arrears. Arrears carried from earlier years and the farmer's own agents made the burden feel arbitrary even when the nominal rate was modest. The Roman publican was a despised figure in the Gospels, where "publicans and sinners" are named together (Matthew 9:10-11), and Badian's study takes the phrase as its title for this reason.

Households with weak legal standing bore most of the over-extraction. Peasants who could not pay fled, which reduced the base the next lessee could bid on. Where the farmer was also a creditor (through the advance or through loans to villagers), collection and debt merged into one relationship, and the household had no separate forum to contest either.

## Regional specifics

Rome relied on companies of publicani, organized as partnerships with shares, that bid for the censors' contracts and built a capital base in provincial collection. The state tolerated its margins because the alternative was a provincial administration it did not yet have. Nero's proposal to abolish indirect taxes in 58 CE was, according to Tacitus (Annals 13.50-51), resisted in the Senate, which shows how much the revenue depended on lessees; the passage should be checked against the Latin text.

The Ottoman iltizam leased revenue units for a term, and the mültezim paid the muqata'a in advance. The malikane gave lifetime tenure for a fixed fee, which made the lease a form of property that could be bought and passed on. Holders of such leases built local power, and this helped to produce the provincial notable class (ayan) that later challenged the centre.

The French Ferme générale held six-year leases of the indirect taxes, advanced money to the treasury, and maintained armed guards with search rights against salt smuggling in the gabelle districts (Kwass, Privilege and the Politics of Taxation in Eighteenth-Century France, 2000). Its unpopularity was one of the grievances of 1789, and the execution of its members in 1794 is the sharpest social consequence on record.

Bengal's Permanent Settlement of 1793 fixed the revenue in perpetuity and made zamindars liable for it. Their surplus was the margin between what they collected and the fixed demand, which resembles tax farming, but the zamindar held land by a title that predated the settlement and was not chosen at an auction. The entry treats it as a relative (Guha, A Rule of Property for Bengal, 1963; date and terms to be checked).

Imperial China did not farm the land tax. The state set county quotas, and its own officials collected them through intermediaries. Its salt merchants held licensed monopolies, which is the nearest analogue, and FIN-068 covers that case.

## Hook

Once mechanised, a farmed revenue head is a contract the player sells at auction: the bid reaches the treasury at once and raises sovereign credit capacity for the period, and the farm surplus (collections minus bid) goes to the lessee. Each period the lessee's over-extraction rate pushes collections above the assessed base, and when cumulative over-extraction crosses a revolt threshold, the head's legibility falls and future bids drop, so the state trades near-term liquidity for a shrinking base.
