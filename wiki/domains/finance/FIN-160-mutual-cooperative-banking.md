A mutual or cooperative financial institution is owned by its own depositors or borrowers rather than by outside shareholders — a structurally different ownership and incentive design from FIN-070's investor-owned joint-stock model, aimed specifically at savers and borrowers the commercial banking and equity system finds unprofitable to serve.

## Timing

Savings banks organized on a mutual, non-profit basis for ordinary depositors develop across Europe from around 1810; the Raiffeisen cooperative credit model, extending the same mutual-ownership logic to small-farmer lending specifically, matures in Germany from the 1860s-1870s and is widely imitated, including in colonial India from 1904.

## Mechanism

A mutual institution has no outside shareholders demanding a return on invested capital — it is owned collectively by the depositors or borrowers who use it, so any operating surplus is returned to members (as better interest rates or reduced fees) rather than paid out as dividends to investors uninvolved in its day-to-day use. This lets a mutual serve depositors and borrowers too small or too risky by commercial standards to be profitable for an investor-owned bank, since the institution's purpose is member service rather than shareholder return.

## Social consequence

Because a mutual's governance rests with its own membership rather than outside capital, it can extend credit on criteria (character, local reputation, community standing) a commercial bank's more impersonal underwriting wouldn't accept, directly reaching populations — small farmers, urban laborers — the joint-stock commercial banking sector (FIN-070) structurally underserves.

## Regional specifics

The Raiffeisen model's export to colonial India, deliberately intended to give smallholders an alternative to local moneylenders' terms, shows the mutual-ownership structure travels as a replicable institutional package much like other financial innovations covered elsewhere in this record. American consumer credit developing predominantly through commercial and installment channels (FIN-150) rather than a comparably large mutual-banking sector shows the underlying credit-access problem can be solved through either ownership model, with different societies weighting the two differently.

## Hook

A modifier: the same credit-pooling function as commercial banking (FIN-070), achieved through a member-owned governance structure that serves depositors and borrowers the investor-owned model finds unprofitable to court.
