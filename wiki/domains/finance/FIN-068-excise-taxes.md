A duty on the production, sale or consumption of a named good, collected where the good is made or sold rather than at the border (FIN-066) or from income (FIN-035 covers the farmed forms).

## Timing

The clearest early excise is the Dutch accijns on beer, wine and other goods, introduced during the Revolt against Spain in the 1570s. Holland's 1574 date is the usual reference point but should be checked against the Dutch fiscal histories (Gelderblom, Cities of Commerce, 2013; Tracy, Holland under Habsburg Rule, 1990). The date_precision is decade for that reason. A related Spanish tax on food and drink, the millones, was granted in the 1590s (source to be checked: I. A. A. Thompson, War and Government in Habsburg Spain, 1976). Parliament's excise on ale, beer and cider in England dates from 1643, borrowed from the Dutch model (Ashworth, Customs and Excise, 2003). England's excise crisis of 1733 and the Gin Acts of 1736 and 1751 show the tax reaching its political limits within a century.

The entry is open-ended, so date_end is left blank. Excise is still in force in most states.

## Mechanism

An excise taxes a good at the point it is made or sold: a rate per barrel of beer, per pound of salt, per gallon of spirit, or per pound of tobacco, usually paid by the producer or licensed seller who then passes it on in price.

The economist's case for it rests on Ramsey's rule (Ramsey, "A Contribution to the Theory of Taxation", 1927). A tax distorts least on goods whose demand does not fall much as price rises. Beer, spirits, salt and tobacco are staples for which demand is inelastic, so they raise a large revenue for a small loss of consumption per unit of revenue. A short list of goods gives a broad tax with a narrow administration.

Administration is cheap because production is concentrated. A brewery or distillery is one site with one output record, so the excise officer inspects a short list of premises rather than every household. Gauging casks, checking licences and holding the bonded store are the main tasks. The cost falls per unit of revenue as the number of producers falls, which is the reason excise appears in states that cannot yet assess land or income.

The price of this efficiency is that the state needs legibility of production, not only of trade. At a border the state sees goods once; in production it needs access to premises and a continuing record of output. Where that access is refused, the duty evasion rate rises with the rate, and evasion moves into unlicensed production: home distilling, unstamped barrels and illicit salt. The concentration that made the tax cheap also makes evasion a small-producer problem that is visible to the village around it.

Excise and customs can apply to the same good. Imported tobacco can pay customs at the port and excise when it is processed or sold. The two taxes share the same good but not the same site, so the state needs two sets of records, and the rate on one is capped by what the other already collects.

## Social consequence

An excise falls on what households buy, so it weighs most on the poor, who spend a larger share of income on beer, salt and spirits. A household that spends a fifth of its income on drink pays the excise on that fifth, and the tax is regressive in exactly the goods its members consume daily. The burden is also paid in price, so it is invisible to those who pay it.

Illicit production follows high rates. Rural communities turned to home distilling and smuggled spirits, and the gin craze of the 1720s and 1730s was fed by cheap, unlicensed spirits and by the duty that made the legal product expensive. The 1736 Gin Act sought to suppress retail sale and provoked disorder rather than compliance. Walpole's 1733 proposal to extend the excise to wine and tobacco was withdrawn after riots, which gave the tax a lasting reputation as a tool of state intrusion into the household.

Salt is the sharpest case. Where the state reserved salt for itself and farmed the sale, as the French gabelle was farmed through the Ferme générale (FIN-035), the household was obliged to buy a quota and was liable to searches. Salt smugglers became a recognised social type, and the search rights were a daily grievance of the poor (Kwass, Privilege and the Politics of Taxation in Eighteenth-Century France, 2000).

## Regional specifics

In Europe, the Dutch accijns and the English excise of 1643 set the pattern, and the French system of indirect taxes (the gabelle on salt, the aides on drink) ran through the farm described in FIN-035. The English excise was extended to new goods over the eighteenth century, and the state's excise officers became a permanent local presence. Ashworth's history of English customs and excise (2003) is the main reference for the administrative detail and should be used for any numbers.

In East Asia, the imperial salt monopoly was the main consumption-based revenue before the nineteenth century, with no broad production excise on common goods (the absence above). The likin, introduced in 1853, was a transit levy on goods in movement, levied at internal barriers. It was not a production excise, but it was administered in the same way, at fixed points on the good's path, and it became a large source of provincial revenue that weakened central control (Wang Yeh-chien, Land Taxation in Imperial China, 1750-1911, 1973; Mann, Local Merchants and the Chinese Bureaucracy, 1987; both need checking for the exact figures).

## Hook

Excise is a per-unit tax on a short list of licensed producers: each period, collected revenue = licensed output x rate, with output read through inspection coverage. Raising the rate on salt, beer or spirits raises revenue at low administrative cost, but it lifts the duty evasion rate and moves output into unlicensed production; past a threshold the licensed-producer count falls, revenue drops, and the share of household income spent on taxed goods becomes a driver of unrest.
