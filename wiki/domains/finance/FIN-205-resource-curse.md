A state that earns its revenue from a resource in the ground rather than from taxing
its people can grow poorer, less accountable and more unstable than a state without
the resource. The curse runs through three separate channels, and whether it takes
hold depends less on the resource than on the institutions a state already had when
the rents arrived.

## Timing

From Peru's guano age in the 1850s and Chile's nitrate revenue from the 1880s,
through Venezuela's oil state from the 1920s and the renegotiation of Middle Eastern
oil concessions from 1950, to the oil booms and busts of the 1970s and 1980s, the
Dutch gas case that named the disease in 1977, the counter-cases of Botswana,
Indonesia, Chile and Norway, and the commodity cycle that ended in the price collapse
of 2014. The same mechanism ran four centuries earlier in Spain's silver (COM-140).

## Mechanism

**The boundary.** COM-160 carries colonial commodity traps: a colony's production
narrowed to export crops chosen or imposed from outside. COM-140 carries American
silver as a market shock. This entry carries what resource rents do to the state and
the rest of the economy of the country that receives them, whether colony or
sovereign, and it treats three channels separately because they have different
causes and different remedies.

**Channel 1: Dutch disease.** A resource export brings in foreign currency, which
raises the value of the domestic currency or, under a fixed rate, raises the domestic
`price level`. Either way every other export becomes more expensive to foreign buyers.
Agriculture and manufacturing lose markets not because anyone neglected them but
because the resource boom priced them out. Nigeria's cocoa, groundnut and palm oil
exports collapsed during the oil boom; Dutch manufacturing lost ground to Groningen
gas; Castilian industry declined while silver flowed in. When the resource runs down
or its price falls, the sectors that could have replaced it are gone, and rebuilding
them takes a generation.

**Channel 2: fiscal delinking — the most important channel for a game.** A state
funded by resource rents does not need to tax its population, and a state that does
not tax does not need consent. LAW-140 carries the bargain that produced representative
institutions: rulers who needed new taxes had to win the consent of estates and
parliaments, and repeated bargaining made that consent a constitutional constraint.
Resource rents break that bargain from the revenue side. The state receives its money
from a foreign buyer through a concession or a state company, and it needs from its
citizens not taxes but acquiescence, which it buys with public employment, subsidies
and services. No taxation, no representation. The consequences compound:

- **The state does not need to see its population.** Without a tax to collect, it
  under-invests in the registers, assessments and local administration that
  `legibility` requires, and those capacities are missing when rents fall.
- **Politics becomes distribution.** Groups compete for a share of the rent rather
  than bargaining over taxes, so control of the centre is the prize, as in Nigeria's
  coups.
- **Rents finance coercion and challenge alike.** Resources that can be seized — oil
  fields, alluvial diamonds — pay for rebels as well as governments.

Peru shows the channel in its plainest form. Guano revenue let the state abolish the
head tax on indigenous communities in 1854, and the fiscal relationship between state
and rural population largely lapsed. The Gulf states levy no income tax at all. WAR-275
notes the same effect from the side of the oil-importing powers: a government financed
by a resource foreigners must have is financed from outside its own society.

**Channel 3: volatility.** Commodity prices swing far more than manufactured goods
prices, and a state that depends on one commodity has `revenue volatility` to match.
Oil prices quadrupled in 1973-74, doubled again in 1979-80, fell by half in 1986,
reached about ten dollars a barrel in 1998, rose to nearly one hundred and fifty in
2008 and halved again in 2014. Planning is nearly impossible, and the pattern of
spending is pro-cyclical. In a boom, revenue looks permanent, so states commit to
payrolls, subsidies and projects and borrow against future output, because lenders
are eager and `sovereign credit capacity` looks high. In the bust, revenue falls,
commitments do not, and boom-time commitments become bust-time debt:

- **Peru** borrowed against guano and defaulted in 1876.
- **Mexico** borrowed against new oil and defaulted in 1982.
- **Venezuela and Nigeria** carried 1970s borrowing into 1980s austerity and unrest.

Exchange-rate and savings policy connect this channel to the monetary regime:
Gulf states peg to the dollar in which their oil is sold (FIN-270), and petrodollar
surpluses reshaped the international monetary system (FIN-250).

**The same mechanism four centuries early.** COM-140's Spanish case is not a separate
phenomenon. American silver was a state-owned resource rent taxed at the point of
extraction. It produced Dutch disease in Castile, as prices rose and Castilian
manufactures lost ground to imports (Drelichman, 'All that glitters', Explorations in
Economic History, 2005). It gave the crown revenue outside the consent of
the Cortes. And its volatility, set by fleet arrivals and mine output, led the crown to
borrow against future shipments and default repeatedly from 1557. The resource curse
is a mechanism that runs whenever a state's revenue comes from a rent rather than from
taxing its own economy.

**The branch variable is institutional state at discovery.** Per design principle 1,
the same resource leads to different outcomes depending on what the state had built
before the rents arrived:

- **A state with established fiscal institutions**, which already taxes its population
  and answers to an assembly, keeps taxing, treats rent as a supplement and can build
  a fund and a rule. Norway found oil as a tax democracy and saved it abroad under a
  spending rule.
- **A state with constraining local institutions** can bind the rent. Botswana's
  diamond revenue went into public investment and savings under leaders accountable
  through institutions that predated the discovery.
- **A state that learns from a prior bust** can build a stabilisation rule. Chile, after
  losing its nitrate revenue, based spending on a long-run copper price set by
  independent experts.
- **A state without them** — a new state, a military government, a colony at
  independence, a fiscal system never built on taxation — takes all three channels
  at full strength.

Where the resource cannot be substituted for, it is also a curse that can end abruptly:
synthetic nitrogen (ENG-320) ended Chile's nitrate state.

## Social consequence

At household scale the rent state is a patron, not a tax collector:

- **A Kuwaiti citizen.** They pay no income tax, hold a public-sector job, receive
  subsidised housing, fuel and utilities, and live alongside foreign workers who
  outnumber citizens (MIG-270).
- **An indigenous Peruvian community after 1854.** The head tax that had tied it to the
  state is abolished, and so is much of the state's reason to know it exists.
- **A Nigerian cocoa farmer in the 1970s.** They find their crop unprofitable at the
  new exchange rate and move to the city, where oil money is being spent.
- **A Venezuelan in 1989.** They see subsidised petrol prices raised under austerity,
  and join the riots that follow.
- **A Keralan family.** They live on a son's remittances from Dubai while the paddy
  fields beside the new house go uncultivated.

Resource rents change what citizens expect from the state and what they owe it. They
expect distribution and owe loyalty, not taxes. When prices fall, the state must
either impose taxes it has no machinery or legitimacy to collect or cut the
distribution its legitimacy rests on. Rapid rent-funded urbanisation and food imports
also reshape diet and health (HLT-350).

Boom-time borrowing against resource revenue often ended in default, as in Peru in
1876 and Mexico in 1982. FIN-067 carries sovereign default and why defaults cluster
when commodity prices fall and interest rates rise.

## Regional specifics

The mechanism appears in every region with rents, in different forms:

- **The Americas** supply the earliest modern cases and a counter-case. Peru's guano
  state abolished tribute and defaulted, Chile's nitrate state was ended by synthetic
  substitution, and Venezuela and Mexico borrowed on oil. Chile's copper rule is the
  counter-case.
- **The Middle East** supplies the rentier state in pure form, from the Gulf's
  distributive monarchies to Iran's revolution after a boom.
- **Africa** supplies both Nigeria's Dutch disease and Botswana's diamond governance, and
  the resource-financed wars of Angola and Sierra Leone.
- **Europe** supplies the disease's name in the Netherlands and the model counter-case
  in Norway.
- **Southeast Asia** supplies Indonesia's agricultural use of an oil windfall under
  authoritarian rule.
- **South Asia** receives the rent indirectly as Gulf remittances (MIG-200), with
  Dutch disease in Kerala.

The absence is resource-poor East Asia, where states had to tax their populations and
export manufactures and built developmental states in the process. Sanctions against
rent states (DIP-190) work differently for the same reason: they cut a revenue stream
that never passed through the population.

## Contested

The curse's existence and direction are disputed:

- **The curse account.** Sachs and Warner found slower growth in resource-rich
  economies; Ross found oil linked to less democracy and more civil war.
- **Reverse causation.** Brunnschweiler and Bulte argue dependence reflects weak
  institutions and growth rather than causing them.
- **No effect.** Haber and Menaldo, using long within-country series, found no link
  between resource income and authoritarianism.

Model the curse as conditional on `rent dependence`, `revenue volatility` and the
institutions present at discovery, not as a fixed penalty for holding a resource.

## Hook

**Three channels, modelled separately.**

- **Dutch disease.** Resource exports raise the `price level` or exchange rate and
  shrink other export sectors, which are slow to rebuild after the bust.
- **Fiscal delinking.** As `rent dependence` rises, the state's need to tax — and so to
  win consent and build `legibility` — falls. Politics shifts from tax bargaining to
  distribution, and seizable rents finance rebellion.
- **Volatility.** `revenue volatility` drives pro-cyclical commitments and borrowing in
  booms, and they become debt and unrest in busts.

**Branch on prior institutional state.** A state that already taxes and answers to an
assembly, or has constraining local institutions or a learned fiscal rule, can save the
rent and damp all three channels. A state without them takes the full effect.

**Substitution.** A resource can be displaced by a synthetic or a new technology,
ending the rent abruptly.

**Old mechanism.** Spain's silver ran the same channels in the sixteenth century and
should use the same mechanic.
