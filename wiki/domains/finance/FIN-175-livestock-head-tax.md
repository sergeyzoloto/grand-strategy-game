A tax levied per head of livestock (horses, cattle, sheep, goats, camels) owned by pastoralists, treated as a fiscal system. Reading adopted here: "поголовный налог" is taken as a per-head tax on a herding population's livestock. It is not a poll tax on people, which is FIN-033.

## Timing

The candidate start date is 1822, the Statute on the Siberian Kirghiz (Ustav o sibirskikh kirgizakh), the usual starting point for Russian statutory regulation of the Kazakh steppe. **The statute and its date are confirmed (1822, Speransky's Statute on the Siberian Kirghiz); the tax it sets is not verified.** The statute's operative tax is not verified in this draft: it may have been a per-head livestock levy, a per-household (tent or yurt) levy, or a levy in kind, and the statute text has not been checked. If the per-head livestock levy came in by a later regulation, the start date must move. Any date in the 1810s or 1820s keeps the entry inside the FIN-170 to FIN-180 slot (1811 to 1830); a later date would need renumbering. Precision is decade for that reason. The entry has no stated end date.

## Mechanism

A per-head tax needs a count of the animals it charges. Herds are mobile, seasonal and unfenced. They are also a continuous producer, not stock held for sale: milk, wool, hides and traction come from living animals (AGR-030 covers this secondary-products economy). Three problems follow.

- **Counting.** A herd can be counted only where it gathers: winter pasture, watering points, markets. The state either counts at a gathering, a costly set-piece, or accepts a headman's estimate. Counting cost scales with the number of herding households, not the number of animals.
- **Unit equivalence.** Horses, cattle, sheep, goats and camels differ in value and labour. A flat rate per head needs an equivalence scale, and the scale moves burden between species. A single rate per head weighs most on owners whose animals are cheapest per head (sheep and goats). This is an inference from the tax's structure, not a sourced finding.
- **Concealment and movement.** A herd can be moved. A higher rate raises the return on hiding animals from the count, or on moving them across a boundary into another district. AGR-140 describes how herd movement across cultivated land already produces recurring conflict; a tax that adds a boundary to the route adds another reason for dispute.

Collection design matters more than the rate. Tribal or volost-level assessment sets a quota for the group, which headmen divide. That lowers the state's counting cost and makes the group liable as a whole, but it gives the headman a share of what is gathered, the intermediary problem FIN-032 describes for land. A per-head count by the state is more accurate and far more expensive, and it is the design most exposed to concealment.

The key difference from FIN-032 is that a land base cannot move and a herd base can. A land tax is fixed to the parcel; a herd tax is fixed to the animal, which walks away.

## Social consequence

For a herding family the tax follows the animals it owns, and that number changes from year to year. In a hard winter, when animals die in large numbers, the head count falls but a quota fixed in an earlier year does not. The household then pays in animals sold at a loss, or in debt. The herd is the household's capital, so a per-head tax cuts directly into the stock that produces its milk, wool and traction.

Group liability changes a family's position inside the aul or volost. The group pays the quota and the headman distributes it, so a family's share depends on its standing in the group. The solidarity that lets a group meet a collective quota is also what lets it hide animals. Families with fewer animals can be asked to carry more than their share, which creates inequality inside the group as well as between the group and the state.

Mobility is the household's response. Families move herds to avoid registration, to other pastures, or across a boundary. The state's picture of which group owes what weakens with each move, and the cost falls on whoever remains in the register.

## Regional specifics

**Central Asian steppe.** Russian statutory regulation of the Kazakh (Kirghiz) steppe from the 1820s is the anchor, and the 1822 Statute on the Siberian Kirghiz is the candidate date (see Timing). Russian and Qing frontier jurisdictions met on the steppe, so transhumant groups could face different regimes on either side of a boundary; the specifics are to be checked against primary regulations. The herd levy contrasts with the land tax (FIN-032) and the head tax on people (FIN-033) used in settled societies.

**Europe (absence).** Herders in European lordships paid grazing dues and tithes on the young of the flock (REL-130), not a count of the whole herd.

## Hook

Once mechanised, a herd tax charges a per-head rate on each animal unit a pastoral polity holds, under a species equivalence scale. Counting is a seasonal, costly act, so each herd carries a visibility rate: the tax raises `herd concealment` (the share of units not declared) and cross-boundary movement when the rate exceeds a herd's margin in a bad winter. Delegating collection to headmen lowers the state's counting cost, raises the headmen's share of what is gathered, and moves losses onto the group (`group liability share`). Where counting is cut back, the steppe population's `legibility` falls.
