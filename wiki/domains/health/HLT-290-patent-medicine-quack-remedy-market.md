Mass advertising extends the authority-without-efficacy mechanic HLT-150,
HLT-260, and HLT-320 already establish directly into a consumer product
market, selling confidently-marketed compounds with no requirement that
their claimed effect be real.

## Timing

Commercially mass-marketed patent medicines scale across Britain and the
United States through the 19th century, reaching peak market volume and
advertising sophistication in the second half of the century, before the
US Pure Food and Drug Act (1906) begins imposing the first systematic
regulatory check on unverified therapeutic claims.

## Mechanism

A patent-medicine seller markets a proprietary compound directly to
consumers using confident, often elaborate claims of therapeutic
efficacy, with no institutional credentialing, licensing, or independent
verification standing between the claim and the sale — a direct,
unmediated instance of HLT-150's authority-without-efficacy split, except
here the authority is manufactured entirely by advertising rather than
inherited from a coherent theoretical system or institutional pedigree.

## Social consequence

Consumers purchase confidence and marketed hope alongside, or instead of,
any genuine therapeutic effect, with many popular patent medicines
containing significant alcohol, opium, or cocaine content (tying directly
to HLT-260's addictive-substance mechanic) that produced a real, if
unadvertised, physiological effect distinct from whatever condition the
product claimed to cure.

## Regional specifics

The market reaches comparable scale and sophistication in both Britain
and the United States, with the US case ending this entry's window
specifically because it produces the first major regulatory response
(the 1906 Act) directly targeting unverified therapeutic advertising
claims.

## Hook

A modifier, mostly flavor: mass advertising extends the authority/efficacy
gap into a direct consumer market, with no lever this domain's other
entries don't already pull.
