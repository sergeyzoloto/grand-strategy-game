A shared public or semi-public bathing venue becomes, across widely
separated traditions, a durable social institution combining genuine
hygiene function with status display and sociability — the same
commensality-and-boundary logic HLT-050 and HLT-130 track for food and
drink, applied to washing instead.

## Timing

Roman public bathing culture matures from around -600 through the
imperial period; the Ottoman hammam adapts the same underlying
infrastructure from around 700 onward; the European spa-town circuit
(Bath, Baden-Baden, and comparable resorts) develops as a distinct,
later elite social institution through the 18th and 19th centuries.

## Mechanism

A bathhouse combines a real hygiene function (regular washing measurably
reduces certain skin and parasitic conditions) with an extended social
occasion — bathers linger, socialize, and are seen — converting a
routine bodily maintenance activity into a recurring venue for status
display and information exchange, structurally similar to HLT-180's
coffeehouse mechanic but anchored on washing rather than a stimulant.

## Social consequence

Where bathing culture is most developed, access, quality, and
segregation (by gender, by status, by payment tier) become their own
legible social markers layered on top of the underlying hygiene benefit,
much as HLT-130's commensality rules mark status through who eats with
whom.

## Regional specifics

The Ottoman hammam elaborates the inherited Roman-Byzantine
infrastructure into a distinctly gender-segregated institution with its
own ritual-purity function tied to Islamic practice, showing the same
underlying venue can be adapted to carry a religiously-grounded function
the original Roman case didn't have.

## Hook

A modifier at best — a status- and sociability-linked hygiene venue,
mostly flavor on top of the commensality (HLT-050) and boundary-marking
(HLT-130) mechanics this domain already has.
