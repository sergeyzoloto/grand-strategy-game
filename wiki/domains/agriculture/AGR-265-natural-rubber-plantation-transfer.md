Rubber was a wild forest product that became a plantation crop within a
single lifetime, and the move changed everything attached to it. Where the
trees grew, and how close together, decided who tapped them and under what
compulsion. Carrying the trees to another continent moved the monopoly, the
labour regime and the risk of disease with them.

## Timing

Goodyear's vulcanization (discovered 1839, patented 1844) and Hancock's in
England (1843–44) turn latex into an industrial material that stays elastic
in heat and cold. Amazonian exports grow from the 1850s, and the pneumatic
tyre (Dunlop, 1888) turns growth into a boom. Wickham ships Hevea seed from
the Amazon to Kew in 1876, and seedlings reach Ceylon and Singapore in
1876–77. The Congo Free State's rubber regime runs from 1891 to 1908.
Plantation output overtakes wild output around 1913, and the Amazon boom
collapses between 1910 and 1913. Restriction schemes run from 1922. Ford's
Amazon plantations fail between 1928 and 1945. The entry closes in 1945,
when Ford sells them to Brazil and synthetic rubber (ENG-285) has capped the
natural product's market.

## Mechanism

**Dispersion sets the labour regime.** Wild Hevea grows scattered through the
Amazon forest, and most of the Congo's rubber came from wild vines. A tapper
works alone along a forest trail for months, out of anyone's sight. That is
the most expensive supervision geography there is, WAR-035's `supervision
cost` at its maximum, so no one paid wages to be watched. Wild extraction ran
on one of two substitutes for supervision:

- **Debt.** The Amazonian aviamento advanced goods against future rubber at
  prices the creditor set, and the tapper could not leave while he owed.
- **Terror against the collector's community.** Congo villages owed rubber by
  quota, enforced by hostage-taking and mutilation, and the Putumayo's
  indigenous collectors were enslaved.

Both coerce the person or the kin rather than supervising the work. Where the
resource itself was being used up, a fixed quota made coercion escalate. The
vines nearest each village went first, the same weight took longer to find,
and violence rose as the forest emptied.

**Moving the plant moves the monopoly.** Brazil held the monopoly by nature,
not by law. Wickham's seeds, germinated at Kew and sent to Ceylon and
Singapore, moved it to whoever held planted land in Asia. This is the act
HLT-265 leaves to this entry: a state seizing a monopoly by moving the plant.
The same India Office network had moved cinchona out of the Andes in the
1860s. It is the deliberate, state-directed successor to the crop
transfers AGR-220 records as accidents of contact. The origin region got no
warning. Plantation output arrived on the timescale of tree growth in Asia,
not of anything that happened in the Amazon, and the boom ended in three
years. What was left was ENG-265's `stranded capital`: Manaus, the river
fleets and the trading houses, and a workforce of tappers whose skill had no
other buyer.

**The transfer works where the plant's enemies are not.** South American leaf
blight, a fungus native to the Amazon, destroys Hevea planted densely in its
homeland, and Asia has never had it. This is `pathogen escape`: a transferred
crop can outperform its homeland because it has left its co-evolved enemies
behind. It is what made Southeast Asia the world's rubber belt and kept the
Amazon from ever becoming a plantation region. The advantage lasts only until
the pathogen follows, as Ceylon's coffee found in 1869. Almost all the world's
natural rubber now grows outside the blight's range, in dense plantings that
have never met it. That is a `crop failure correlation` held in reserve, the
failure mode AGR-270 describes for the potato, waiting on a single arrival.

**No processing bottleneck, no estate advantage.** Sugar needs a mill within
hours of cutting, which is why AGR-210's plantation beats the smallholder.
Latex needs a cup, acid, a pan and a hand mangle. Once estates had proved the
crop, smallholders in Sumatra, Borneo and Malaya planted it among their food
crops and produced at lower cost (Bauer, The Rubber Industry, 1948). They
could also stop tapping when prices fell and live off their food crops, which
an estate with a wage bill cannot do. Estates kept their share through
political allocation: restriction quotas that fell hardest on smallholders,
land rules, and imported labour. Estate survival here is a political
variable, not a technical one.

## Social consequence

The seringueiro lived alone or with one family in a clearing on his trail. He
walked it before dawn to cut the trees and again to collect the latex, then
smoked it over a fire into a ball. He bought everything at the trading post
and was paid in credit against it, so the debt held him, not a guard. Many
were drought refugees from Ceará with nowhere to return to, and malaria and
beriberi killed them at rates the trading houses did not record.

In the Congo the quota fell on the village. Men went into the forest for
weeks while women were held as hostages until they returned, and the fields
went unplanted, so famine followed the rubber. The campaign against it began
with Casement's report of 1904 and was carried by the Congo Reform
Association, using photographs of mutilated people as evidence (MED-105).
It is often counted among the first international human-rights campaigns,
and it ended Leopold II's personal rule with Belgian annexation in 1908.

On the Asian estates supervision was cheap and coercion moved into the
contract. Tamil workers came to Malaya through kangani recruiters from their
own villages and owed the kangani the cost of their passage. They lived in
estate lines with the estate's shop, a toddy shop and a Tamil school. Many
meant to return and did not, which is where Malaysia's estate Tamil
communities come from. In Sumatra, leaving an estate before the contract ended
was a crime. The indenture and debt that replaced slavery on sugar estates
(LAW-175) reappear here on a new crop.

The smallholder household is the other human scale. It tapped at dawn before
field work and kept rubber as a savings crop, tapped when the price justified
it. The wild tapper also outlived the boom. In Acre the seringueiros
organised from 1976 to block ranchers clearing their forest. Chico Mendes was
murdered in 1988, and in 1990 Brazil created extractive reserves, turning the
wild tapping regime into a form of forest tenure.

## Regional specifics

The Americas hold both ends of the story. Mesoamerica processed rubber for
three and a half thousand years without an extraction regime, because demand
stayed local. The Amazon supplied the industrial world by debt, and at the
Putumayo by enslavement. It then lost the market to its own transferred tree
and could not plant that tree at home. Fordlândia is the test of that
failure: capital, management and a company town were not enough against the
blight. When Japan took the Asian plantations in 1942, the Allies'
alternatives split by prior capability (design principle 1). The United
States built synthetic rubber. Brazil sent tens of thousands of northeastern
workers, the rubber soldiers, back into the forest to tap wild trees, and
many never returned.

Africa carried the worst of wild extraction, in the Congo Free State and the
concession companies of the neighbouring French Congo. It later received a
plantation designed to escape a cartel. After Britain's Stevenson scheme
raised prices, Firestone took a ninety-nine-year lease on up to a million
acres of Liberia in 1926. That is a buyer facing a producer cartel and
acquiring its own plantation territory: `strategic input dependency`,
answered by concession rather than by conquest.

Southeast Asia won the transfer and then divided it between estates and
smallholders, with colonial quotas deciding the split more than cost did.
South Asia supplied the labour and, in Ceylon, the warning that a transferred
crop's escape from its pathogens can end. China, cut off by the 1951 embargo
on strategic goods, planted rubber on Hainan and in Xishuangbanna beyond the
crop's usual northern limit. The same crop was pushed to its climatic margin
for security rather than profit. The price exposure the Amazon and later
Malaya suffered as single-commodity exporters is COM-160's trap mechanic and
is not re-derived here.

## Contested

**Why the Amazon never planted rubber.** Dean (Brazil and the Struggle for
Rubber, 1987) makes the leaf blight decisive: dense stands of Hevea cannot
survive in the fungus's range, so plantation rubber was impossible there
whatever anyone did. Weinstein (The Amazon Rubber Boom, 1850–1920, 1983)
locates the failure in the social relations of the boom. The merchants and
patrons who profited from debt-bound extraction had no reason to invest in
planting, and labour was too scarce to staff estates. Fordlândia supports
Dean on whether plantations could work. The half-century in which no
Brazilian capital tried supports Weinstein on why no one found out sooner.
Model plantation in the homeland as blocked by either of two independent
conditions, the pathogen's presence and an extraction economy whose creditors
gain nothing from planting, so that removing one does not open the path.

**The Congo death toll.** Hochschild (King Leopold's Ghost, 1998) follows
estimates that the population fell by about half, some ten million people.
Critics answer that the earlier population is unknown, and that sleeping
sickness and other epidemics spreading along the new routes caused much of
the loss. Model the regime's mortality as large and uncertain, divided among
direct violence, famine from labour taken off the fields, flight, and disease
spread along the rubber routes. Weight the causes by region, and never fix the
toll as a single number.

## Hook

**Dispersion forces coercion at a distance.** A wild resource worked by lone
collectors across a forest can be extracted at scale only through debt
bondage or collective terror against the collectors' communities, because
`supervision cost` is at its maximum and a wage buys nothing that can be
checked. When the resource depletes under a fixed quota, coercion rises as
the stock falls.

**Moving the plant moves the monopoly.** A state or firm that carries a crop
to land it holds takes the monopoly from the origin region. The origin's
capital, towns and skills are stranded on the plantation's timescale, with no
signal inside the origin economy (`stranded capital`).

**Pathogen escape.** A transferred crop outperforms its homeland wherever its
co-evolved pests and pathogens are absent, and the homeland cannot plant it
densely at all. `pathogen escape` is lost when the pathogen arrives. At that
point the transferred monoculture's `crop failure correlation` is at its
maximum. When that happens should stay hidden from the player and
unforeseeable.

**Processing decides who plants.** Where a crop needs no central processing,
smallholders match estates on cost and can stop tapping in a slump. Estates
hold their share only through political allocation of quotas, land and
labour.

**Answers to a cut-off supply.** A buyer facing a producer cartel or a lost
source can take a concession, conquer the plantations, synthesise a
substitute (ENG-285), or return to wild extraction. Which of these is open is
decided by the chemical industry, fleets and territory the buyer already holds
when the shock arrives (design principle 1). `strategic input dependency`
measures how badly it needs one.
