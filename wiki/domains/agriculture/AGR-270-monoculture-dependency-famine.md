AGR-220 establishes the potato's genuine agricultural windfall — a crop
raising `yield ceiling` on marginal land across Europe; this entry owns
the same windfall's mirror-image catastrophic failure mode, which the
identical genetic uniformity behind the yield gain is what makes possible
in the first place.

## Timing

The Phytophthora infestans blight first strikes Irish potato crops in
1845, recurring in successive growing seasons through 1852, with the
worst mortality and emigration concentrated in 1846-1849.

## Mechanism

A population that shifts dependence onto one high-yield but genetically
narrow crop variety (rural Ireland's reliance on the "lumper" potato
variety specifically) gains `yield ceiling` in ordinary years at the cost
of correlated failure risk in a bad one: because the dependent
population is growing substantially the same variety, a pathogen
specifically suited to that variety's genetic profile doesn't damage a
fraction of the food supply the way a pathogen limited to one crop among
several diversified ones would — it can strike the entire dependent
population's food supply simultaneously, converting what would be a
contained, survivable crop failure into a systemic one.

## Social consequence

Where the affected population has no comparable alternative staple to
fall back on, a monoculture failure of this kind converts an agricultural
event directly into a demographic one — mass mortality from starvation
and associated disease, and mass emigration (feeding directly into this
record's Irish diaspora migration patterns) at a scale and speed no
gradual crop-substitution response can match, since the population had
no accumulated experience or infrastructure for shifting to an
alternative staple at the scale suddenly required.

## Regional specifics

The same 1845 blight outbreak strikes Highland Scotland, Belgium, and
other continental European regions without producing anything close to
Ireland's mortality — showing the pathogen's own reach and the resulting
demographic catastrophe are separable variables, with dependency depth
and political/market response, not the blight's geography, determining
the outcome. The pathogen's probable American origin, and the comparable
American crop losses of the same decade without comparable mortality,
reinforces the same point from the opposite direction: the same shock
hitting a population with a diversified diet produces ordinary
agricultural loss, not demographic catastrophe.

## Contested

Whether the catastrophic death toll and emigration should be attributed
primarily to the blight itself or to the contemporary political and
economic response — continued food exports from Ireland during the
worst famine years, and the adequacy of relief efforts — is a genuine,
long-running historiographical dispute, reflected in the contested
choice between "Famine" and "Great Hunger" as the event's own name in
the literature. Model the blight as the triggering shock and the
political/market response as an independently-weighted multiplier on
the resulting death toll, rather than a fixed, natural consequence of
the pathogen alone.

## Hook

Genetic uniformity behind a monoculture's `yield ceiling` gain (AGR-220)
converts a pathogen's damage from crop-specific to population-wide,
producing a `crop failure correlation` that turns an ordinary bad
harvest into a demographic catastrophe — with the actual death toll and
displacement resolving as a branch on prior political and market
institutions (design principle 1), not as a fixed multiplier on blight
severity alone.
