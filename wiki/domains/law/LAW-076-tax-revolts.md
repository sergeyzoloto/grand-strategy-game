A tax grievance becomes a revolt when a community's burden departs from what it takes to be owed, and the centre answers with a fiscal concession that is cheap now and dearer each time it is repeated.

## Timing

The anchor is the Chen Sheng and Wu Guang rising of summer 209 BCE in the Qin. Conscripts bound for the frontier were held up by rain, and Qin law made lateness a capital offence, so the rising began as a refusal of labour service. Taxation shares that grievance family without leading it (Sima Qian, *Shiji*, ch. 48). The anchor is verified in outline only.

Tax revolts recur across the whole timeline wherever a collector's demand moves faster than the community's measure of what is owed: the Yellow Turbans (184 CE), the Ming surtaxes (from 1618), England in 1381, the Celali risings (from 1598), Túpac Amaru II (1780), and colonial hut taxes into the 1890s. The process has no end date, so `date_end` is left open.

## Mechanism

The economist's read starts from a reference point. A community tolerates a tax at the level it takes as customary. What provokes revolt is the gap between demand and that assessment, and above all its unpredictability: a heavy fixed burden can be planned against, an arbitrary demand cannot.

Five tax forms open the gap fastest:

- **Arbitrary surcharges**, such as the liaoxiang military levies kept on the schedule with no sunset. Grievance accumulates, and no one can predict the next addition.
- **Tax farming** (FIN-035). The farmer keeps what he extracts above the assessment, so extraction has no ceiling, and the agent causing it is local and visible. A revolt can target a person, which makes it cheap to start.
- **Flat poll levies** (FIN-033). A per-head charge is regressive, and enforcing it means counting every head (FIN-020). Enquiry turns concealment into an offence.
- **Land-tax reassessment** (FIN-032). A new measurement moves liability between communities in one step, a dated and visible trigger.
- **Service commuted to cash.** When the centre sets the buy-out price of labour or conscription (WAR-200), the poor carry a fixed money debt in place of unpaid time that could absorb a bad harvest.

Revolt follows when grievance crosses a threshold. The threshold falls with coordination capacity (village and guild networks, sect ties, the group solidarity of PHI-240), with armed men released from service (WAR-037), and with a trigger such as a collector's abuse. It rises with the centre's `legibility` and garrison presence, and with the absence of appeal or exit. Grievance does not decay on its own; it persists until the assessment is reset.

A revolt is usually a demand to renegotiate the assessment, not a rejection of the centre, so the centre can often answer cheaply. It weighs repression (costs as in WAR-240) against concession: remission of arrears, reduction, amnesty, a new assessment, a replaced collector, or a charter handing local self-taxation to a town (LAW-120). A concession lowers the customary assessment, so the next grievance is measured from a lower base. It also leaves a concession precedent: revolt pays, and its networks survived. The centre's learned price of collection, the revenue forgone per assessed unit, rises with each cycle. The first remission is cheap; the third is a standing discount.

Co-optation of rebel leaders and the repeated loop of revolt, repression and concession belong to the open cross-domain gap recorded in the 2026-09-14 gaps file and are not written here.

## Social consequence

The household that pays most has fixed fields and no way to leave. It cannot move a herd, cannot hide a head from the count, and pays the flat levy whatever its harvest. Herding households often escape a land-based assessment only by paying through a headman, which links this entry to AGR-140.

Joining a rising costs more than a week's work: fields go unworked, property is distrained if the revolt fails, and men face the repression. A remission helps the households in arrears most, since they gain most from it. Households that paid on time gain little and learn that delay is rewarded, which seeds the next arrears. The headmen who negotiated the terms become gatekeepers of the new assessment.

## Regional specifics

**East Asia.** The Qin anchor is labour-first. The Yellow Turbans combined tax, corvée and famine with sect organisation (*Hou Hanshu*; source to be checked). After An Lushan the Tang replaced its registration-based tax (LAW-075). The Ming liaoxiang surtax is the arbitrary-surcharge case.

**Europe.** England's 1381 rising followed a poll tax collected by enquiry, and the crown's manumission charters were then annulled (Dobson 1970; Hilton 1973). Muscovy's salt and copper riots and the Habsburg Croatian-Slavonian rising of 1573 show tax and labour grievance on the periphery. Rákóczi's Hungarian revolt is a sponsorship case (DIP-115).

**Middle East.** The Celali risings drew on extraordinary levies, tax farms (FIN-035) and demobilised sekban musketeers (WAR-037), with harvest failure compounding them (AGR-230). The Ottoman state settled them mainly through pardon and office.

**South Asia.** Mughal zamindar and rural risings were fed by revenue demands and disputes with revenue officials, alongside religious and status grievance.

**Americas.** Túpac Amaru II's rising (1780) answered forced sales by corregidores and Bourbon tax increases. New Granada's 1781 Comunero rising ended in capitulations the crown repudiated, the precedent problem in its plainest form.

**Africa.** Colonial hut taxes (LAW-155) produced risings such as the Sierra Leone Hut Tax War (1898), put down militarily.

## Contested

Whether tax is the cause or only the occasion of these risings is disputed in every major case. For 1381, one reading treats the poll tax as the trigger for a rising that carried older labour and land grievances; another gives it the central role (Hilton 1973; Dobson 1970). For the Celali, Barkey's state-and-bureaucracy reading (*Bandits and Bureaucrats*, 1994) competes with harvest-and-climate readings in which tax is one strand. For the Mughal risings, Habib's revenue thesis (*The Agrarian System of Mughal India*, 1963) is weighed against accounts stressing zamindar autonomy and religious grievance. Page-level pointers still need checking.

Model this as a conditional, not a verdict. The fiscal grievance term enters the revolt threshold with a weight drawn per case from a range: high where a customary assessment is measurable and a cadastre exists (FIN-020), lower where labour service, religion or prices carry the grievance.

## Hook

Each community carries a fiscal grievance, the gap between demand and customary assessment, which each tax form adds to at its own rate: surcharges and tax farms fastest, reassessment in one step, poll levies by head count. When grievance crosses a revolt threshold, which falls with `asabiyyah` and `veteran claim` and rises with `legibility`, the community revolts. The centre then pays a repression cost or grants a concession that lowers `customary assessment`, adds one `concession precedent` (making the next revolt cheaper), and raises the `price of collection`.
