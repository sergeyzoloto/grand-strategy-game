Institutions resist change because whoever benefits from the current arrangement can
block reform. Catastrophe does not persuade those blockers; it removes them. So
institutional change clusters immediately after disasters and is nearly impossible
between them. This is a claim about when change is possible, not a claim that
catastrophe is good.

## Timing

From Athens after the Sicilian disaster in 413 BCE and Justinian's legislation after
the plague of 541, through Tang China's two-tax system of 780 and Europe after the
Black Death, to the reforms that followed defeat in Prussia in 1806, Russia in 1856 and
Turkey in 1918. The world wars' fiscal and social settlements, famine codes in India,
the Ethiopian revolution of 1974 and the security laws after 2001 carry it to the
present.

## Mechanism

**Incumbents hold a veto, and catastrophe lowers it.** Every institutional arrangement
has beneficiaries: landlords under a labour regime, office-holders under an
administration, the elites who led a state into its present order. They can usually
block changes that would cost them, through office, wealth, courts and violence. Their
collective capacity to block is the order's `incumbent veto`, and it is why institutions
change slowly in ordinary times even when reform would benefit most people. Olson calls
these blockers distributional coalitions, and they accumulate the longer a society is
stable. Catastrophe works on the veto directly. It destroys the capital incumbents
controlled, kills or discredits the people who held power, breaks the fiscal position
that sustained the old bargain, and creates an obligation the state must answer. Reform
becomes possible in the window before a new set of incumbents forms.

**This is a claim about timing, not about value.** Catastrophe is not good, and the
opening it creates is not reliably used for reform. The mechanism predicts that large
institutional change happens after disasters rather than between them. It does not
predict which direction the change will take.

**Channel one: labour scarcity.** Mass mortality raises the price of labour and
collapses the bargaining position of those who own land. After the Black Death wages
rose across western Europe and customary labour obligations decayed, even as
authorities legislated to freeze wages, as Justinian had tried after the plague of 541.
East of the Elbe the same shock let lords re-impose serfdom. AGR-190 carries that
divergence and HLT-220 carries the pandemic's rupture of habit. This entry generalises
from it: scarcity opens the labour bargain, and prior institutional strength decides
who captures it.

**Channel two: fiscal emergency.** War finance forces taxation that is impossible in
peacetime. Cases:

- **Tang China.** It replaced a registration-based tax with a tax on land and wealth
  after rebellion destroyed the registers.
- **Britain.** It introduced income tax in 1799 to fight France.
- **The United States.** It taxed incomes and built a national banking system in the
  Civil War.
- **Mass income tax.** Most belligerents built it in the world wars.

The administrative capacity built to collect these taxes outlives the war, and spending
ratchets up and does not return, which is Peacock and Wiseman's displacement effect. War
finance (FIN-110) and total mobilisation (WAR-290) are the settings in which it happens.
The emergency weakens `sovereign credit capacity` so badly that new revenue becomes
unavoidable, and the resistance of those who would pay collapses with it.

**Channel three: obligation incurred.** A state that conscripts its population, draws on
its `mobilisable manpower` in mass war, or asks it to endure catastrophe incurs a claim it
must eventually answer. Mass conscription (WAR-200) makes the claim universal. Outcomes
included:

- **Suffrage.** It expanded after both world wars, as LAW-220 records.
- **The welfare state.** The Beveridge report was drafted in 1942 in the middle of the
  war, and the welfare settlements (LAW-210) came after it.
- **Colonial promises.** Britain promised India progressive self-government in 1917,
  in the middle of a war India was helping to fight.
- **Famine relief.** India's Famine Codes turned relief into standing administrative
  duty after the famine of 1876-78.

**Channel four: elite discrediting.** Defeat delegitimises the people who led a state
into it, and that is why reform after defeat is more thorough than reform after
victory:

- **Prussia after Jena** opened the officer corps to merit and abolished hereditary
  serfdom.
- **Russia after the Crimea** emancipated its serfs (LAW-175).
- **Japan.** The Tokugawa order's visible failure against Western power preceded the
  Meiji transformation, and defeat in 1945 permitted land reform and a new constitution
  under occupation.
- **Turkey after 1918** abolished the caliphate and replaced religious law with a civil
  code in a few years.

**The counter-case: the same opening admits consolidation.** A catastrophe lowers the
veto for anyone prepared to use the moment, including those who want to concentrate
power:

- **Emergency powers** granted in a crisis do not lapse: the Reichstag fire decree of
  1933 suspended civil liberties and was never repealed while the regime lasted.
- **Scapegoating.** After the Tokyo earthquake of 1923, rumours that Koreans were
  poisoning wells led vigilantes, with police acquiescence, to kill thousands of Korean
  residents. The same collapse of evidentiary standards drives LAW-150's legal panics.
- **Security law.** After 2001 the United States enacted surveillance and war powers that
  outlived the emergency by decades.
- **Ethiopia.** The 1974 revolution used a famine's discrediting of the monarchy to
  install a military dictatorship.
- **Athens.** The oligarchs of 411 used the Sicilian disaster the same way.

## Social consequence

At human scale the opening arrives through suffering, and the people who pay for it are
rarely the ones who design what follows:

- **An English labourer in 1350.** They could demand higher wages because so many had
  died, and could be fined or imprisoned under the Statute of Labourers for doing so.
- **A British soldier's widow in 1945.** The war that took her husband also delivered
  national insurance.
- **A Russian serf.** They were freed because their state had lost a war, and then
  charged for their land.
- **A Korean worker in Tokyo in 1923.** The earthquake that could have opened reform
  opened a massacre instead.

Catastrophe also changes what people believe is possible. Reforms dismissed as utopian
before a war become obvious after it, because the old arrangement has visibly failed and
the sacrifices demand an answer. This is why programmes prepared in advance matter so
much: the Beveridge plan, the Prussian reform memoranda and the Meiji oligarchs' borrowed
institutions were ready when the moment came, and a reform not already drafted rarely
survives the few years before new incumbents entrench.

The narrowest technical form of the opening is the building code. ENG-166 carries the
fire, earthquake and industrial disasters after which the standard to which things
are built was raised and not lowered again.

## Regional specifics

The mechanism runs in every region, and in each it produced both outcomes:

- **The Mediterranean.** Athens turned a military disaster into an oligarchic coup, and
  Justinian answered a pandemic with wage controls.
- **East Asia.** Tang China rebuilt its tax system from a rebellion's ruins, and Japan
  reformed after defeat in 1945 and massacred Korean residents after the earthquake
  of 1923.
- **Europe.** It supplies the Black Death's divergence and Prussia's reform after defeat.
- **South Asia.** Famine became codified administrative obligation in the 1880s, and a
  colonial state with no obligation to the governed shows the limit in 1943.
- **The Middle East.** Ottoman defeat enabled the Turkish republic's rapid replacement
  of religious law.
- **The Americas.** Civil War finance, the New Deal, and emergency powers after 2001.
- **Africa.** Ethiopia shows famine opening both land reform and dictatorship within
  months.

China's Taiping war is the absence. Catastrophe on an enormous scale strengthened the
gentry who defeated the rebellion and produced a conservative restoration, not an
opening, because the incumbents won.

## Contested

Whether crisis enables reform or only accelerates change already under way is argued:

- **For crisis.** Olson says stable societies accumulate blocking coalitions that
  defeat clears. Scheidel finds large reductions in inequality almost only after war,
  revolution, collapse or pandemic. The displacement effect shows wartime spending
  ratcheting permanently upward.
- **For gradual change.** Historians of institutional change such as Streeck and Thelen
  show deep transformation through gradual layering, and point out that post-crisis
  reforms are often drafts from before the crisis.
- **For consolidation.** Klein and theorists of emergency powers stress crises exploited
  to concentrate authority.

Model catastrophe as a temporary reduction in `incumbent veto` whose outcome is
conditional on prior institutional state, per design principle 1. It should depend on
which groups the catastrophe removes or discredits, which programmes are already
prepared, and whether the state faces an obligation it must answer. AGR-190's divergence
is the demonstration: one shock, two opposite results, decided by what existed before.

## Hook

**The veto.** Each institutional order carries an `incumbent veto`, the capacity of its
beneficiaries to block reform. It is high in stable periods and should make large
institutional change nearly impossible between shocks.

**The window.** Catastrophe lowers the veto temporarily. The window closes as new
incumbents form, so change prepared in advance is what gets through. It opens through
four channels:

- **Labour scarcity.** Mass mortality shifts bargaining power toward labour.
- **Fiscal emergency.** Collapsing `sovereign credit capacity` forces new taxation and
  administrative capacity that outlast the crisis.
- **Obligation incurred.** Drawing on `mobilisable manpower` in mass war creates claims
  for suffrage and social insurance.
- **Elite discrediting.** Defeat removes those who led into it.

**The outcome is not predetermined.** The same window should admit reform, consolidation
through emergency powers that do not lapse, scapegoating and repression, or conservative
restoration where the incumbents survive or win. The branch depends on which groups the
catastrophe removed, which programmes were ready, and whether the state owed its
population an answer.
