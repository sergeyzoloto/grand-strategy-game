The camera made an image without a hand. A photograph was taken as a trace of
whatever stood in front of the lens, not as someone's account of it. That
authority, deserved or not, let officials identify people they had never
met, let a distant public witness what it could not see, and let a household
keep its dead.

## Timing

The camera obscura had projected images for centuries: it is described in the
Mohist canon and was investigated by Ibn al-Haytham, with NAT-080's lens optics
sharpening it later. What was missing was a way to fix the image. Niépce made
the first surviving photograph in 1826–27. Daguerre's process was announced in
January 1839, and Talbot's negative-positive process followed in 1839–41. The
carte de visite (1854) made portraits cheap. The halftone put a photograph in
a newspaper in 1880 and made press photographs routine by the late 1890s. The
Kodak (1888) and the one-dollar Brownie (1900) put cameras in households, and
the passport took a photograph during the First World War. Moving pictures
began as photographs in rapid sequence (MED-115). The entry closes in 1991.
That year a bystander's camcorder recording of police beating Rodney King in
Los Angeles showed the witness camera at its most powerful, a year after
Photoshop made manipulating an image cheap. The camera phone and the network
(MED-150) take over from there.

## Mechanism

**An image made without a hand.** Before 1839 every picture of a person, place
or event passed through a hand, and its truth rested on the maker's skill and
honesty. A photograph is made by light acting on a chemical surface, so
audiences took it as a trace of the thing itself rather than a report of it.
Daston and Galison call this mechanical objectivity (Objectivity, 2007). It
gave the photograph `evidentiary authority` no drawing had, in the laboratory,
the courtroom, the newspaper and the file. Once the halftone let presses print
photographs, the same authority reached a mass public through MED-090's
newspapers.

**Authority belongs to the medium, not to each picture.** A photograph's
authority comes from the public's trust in cameras in general, so any single
image can borrow it. Several kinds of image all draw on credit the medium
earned elsewhere:

- **Staging.** Gardner moved a corpse at Gettysburg for a better picture.
- **Retouching.** The picture bride's husband appeared younger than he was.
- **Erasure.** Nikolai Yezhov was removed from photographs beside Stalin after
  his execution in 1940.
- **Outright fabrication.** The Cottingley fairies of 1917 convinced Conan
  Doyle.

This is design principle 3: authority and veracity are separate quantities,
and the more authority a medium carries, the more a fake in it is worth. The
authority is a stock. The medium's reputation builds it slowly, and it is
drawn down when manipulation becomes cheap and widely known, which is what
Photoshop began in 1990.

**The face binds a document to a body.** A photograph lets someone who has
never met a person check that they are who a document says. MED-085's files
found a person by measurement and later by fingerprint, which needed a trained
clerk and a cabinet. A face can be checked at a glance by any official at a
counter or a frontier. That is why the passport took a photograph in the First
World War (Britain from 1915, standardised by the 1920 Paris passport
conference), and why the police card carried a front-and-profile photograph
beside the measurements. The photograph is the cheap, fast, fallible check and
the fingerprint the slow, certain one. It raises `legibility` wherever a state
photographs its population. It is also what makes MIG-230's documents belong to
one bearer rather than to whoever holds them.

**Who holds the camera decides whose account carries the authority.** The first
cameras were costly and needed trained operators, so the authority served those
who could pay: states, empires, newspapers, scientists and the portrait studio's
customers. Colonised peoples were photographed long before they photographed.
Then the `production cost floor` of an image fell in steps:

- **The carte-de-visite studio** (1854) made portraits cheap.
- **The Kodak** (1888) and the one-dollar Brownie (1900) put cameras in
  households.
- **8mm film and then the camcorder** put moving witness in a bystander's hands.

Each fall moved the authority further from its first holders. Abraham
Zapruder's home movie became the central record of Kennedy's assassination in
1963. George Holliday's camcorder recording of the Rodney King beating in 1991
contested an account the police had written themselves.

This entry owns the camera as witness and identifier, still or moving. MED-115
owns film as recorded performance.

## Social consequence

Photography made the likeness ordinary. A painted portrait had been a
privilege of the rich. The carte de visite let clerks, servants and soldiers
own pictures of themselves and exchange them, and the miniature painters and
engravers who had made likenesses by hand lost their trade on COM-180's
pattern.

The household gained an archive. Families kept albums, hung portraits of the
dead, and photographed dead infants for want of any other picture of them.
They sent portraits across oceans: emigrants to kin at home, and picture
brides to husbands they had never met.

Being photographed also became something the state did to people. The
mugshot, the identity photograph and the ethnographic type each fixed a face
to a category chosen by someone else. In the medium's first century, the
people most often photographed were often the least able to choose how. The
studio reversed that for those who could pay: in Bamako, Accra and Lagos,
clients posed as they wished to be seen.

Portable cameras took pictures of people who had not agreed to be
photographed. Warren and Brandeis's 1890 article founding the American right
to privacy (Harvard Law Review) named instantaneous photographs and newspaper
enterprise as the threat. The snapshot camera lowered `privacy`, and a legal
right was invented to defend it.

## Regional specifics

Europe supplies the invention and the first decision about the gate. France
gave the process away, while England's patents held back the negative that
could make copies. The Americas supply the war photograph (Antietam, 1862),
the snapshot industry (Kodak), and the bystander's camera (1963 and 1991).
South Asia shows the camera classifying before it identified, feeding the
categories that registration later administered, while Indian court
photographers served the princes. The Middle East shows rulers using the camera
as foreign policy, in Abdülhamid II's albums and Naser al-Din Shah's court.

East Asia supplies the picture brides, a migration conducted by photograph
inside the restriction regime MIG-220 records. It also supplies the Empress
Dowager Cixi's portraits of 1903–04, which were made to repair her image with
foreign governments after the Boxer war and given to diplomats. Africa
supplies the photograph as evidence against a regime, in the Congo campaign
that AGR-265 records, and after 1948 the camera in the hands of the
photographed. The absences mark two limits. Aboriginal mourning custom shows
what the medium assumes, that the dead should stay visible. Saudi women's
identity documents show legibility stopping where a population is not
photographed.

## Contested

**Does a photograph move anyone?** Sontag (On Photography, 1977) argued that
images of suffering, repeated, dull the response they seem to demand, and
Moeller (Compassion Fatigue, 1999) documented the effect in news coverage.
Against them stand the cases where photographs anchored campaigns that changed
policy:

- **The Congo reform campaign.**
- **Lewis Hine's child-labour photographs** for the National Child Labor
  Committee.
- **The Biafra famine photographs of 1968.** Médecins Sans Frontières grew out
  of that famine in 1971.

Sontag herself revised her view (Regarding the Pain of Others, 2003). Model an
image's mobilising effect as conditional on an organisation ready to use it,
and as decaying with repetition. Shock is then a pool that exposure draws down
(design principle 4), and it turns into action only where a campaign already
exists.

**Is a photograph evidence by itself?** The Rodney King video looked
conclusive to a national audience. The defence then slowed and parsed it frame
by frame, and the jury acquitted the officers in 1992. Courts long treated
photographs as pictorial testimony, admissible only to illustrate what a
witness swore to. They moved only gradually to the silent-witness view, under
which a reliable process can make a picture evidence on its own. Model
`evidentiary authority` as conferred by the audience and procedure that
receive an image, not carried inside it. The same picture should be able to
convince a public and fail in a courtroom, or the reverse.

## Hook

**`evidentiary authority`.** The camera gives a record the authority of a
trace. The medium's authority is a stock, built slowly by reputation and drawn
down when manipulation becomes cheap and exposed. Each image's truth is a
separate quantity (design principle 3), and a fake is worth most where
authority is highest. What a picture proves is decided by the audience and
procedure that receive it.

**Face-checked identity.** Photographic documents and files raise
`legibility`: any official can check a stranger against a face, cheaply and
fallibly, where a fingerprint needs a bureau. A population the state does not
photograph, such as secluded women, stays identified through others.

**Whoever holds cameras holds the witness.** While the `production cost floor`
of an image is high, photographic authority serves states, empires and
newspapers. As it falls, bystanders acquire that authority and can turn it
against its first holders.

**Shock depletes.** An image's power to mobilise decays with repetition and
turns into action only through an existing campaign.

**Cameras lower `privacy`**, and privacy law is invented to answer them.

**The household archive.** Families keep the dead and the absent as images,
and a migration can run on photographs, as the picture brides' did.
