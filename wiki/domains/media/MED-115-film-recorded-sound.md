Before 1877 a performance existed only where and while it was given.
Recording made it a copy that could be stored, pressed, shipped and sold. That
did to voice, music and the moving image what the press had done to text:
the cost of copying, not the cost of performing, came to decide who could
reach whom.

## Timing

Edison's phonograph (1877) grows out of his work on the telegraph and
telephone (MED-110) and records and plays back sound. Berliner's gramophone
(1887) puts sound on flat discs that can be pressed from a master by the
thousand. Eastman's celluloid roll film (1889), ENG-285's first plastic put
to a new use, supplies the substrate for moving pictures, which are MED-105's
photographs taken in rapid sequence. The Lumières show them to a paying
audience in Paris in December 1895. Recording companies send
engineers around the world from the 1900s; Fred Gaisberg records in Calcutta
in 1902. Electrical recording (1925) and synchronised sound film (1927)
follow. Magnetic tape arrives in the 1930s and becomes a studio medium after
1948, the year of the long-playing vinyl record. The compact cassette (1963)
and the videocassette (1976) put copying machines in homes. The entry closes
in 1999, when file sharing detaches recorded sound from any physical copy and
MED-150 takes over.

## Mechanism

**Performance becomes a copy.** Before recording, the only source of a
performance was the performer, and the audience had to be in the room. A
recording fixes the performance on a substrate that can be duplicated, so it
can be stored, shipped, sold and replayed after the performer has stopped or
died. MED-080's press did this for text in the fifteenth century. Recording
does it for the things writing could only describe: a voice, a tune, a
gesture. Performance stops being a service consumed where it is produced and
becomes a stock of goods.

**The best performance goes everywhere.** When a copy costs little next to a
live performance, audiences buy the best recorded performer rather than the
nearest live one. Income concentrates on a few performers and on the firms
that own their recordings (Rosen, The Economics of Superstars, American
Economic Review, 1981). The ordinary paid work of local musicians shrinks on
COM-180's pattern: the café band, the dance orchestra, the pit orchestra that
accompanied silent film. Sound film ended most cinema musicians' jobs within
about three years of 1927, and the American musicians' union banned its
members from recording between 1942 and 1944 to hold back canned music. The
same mechanism works outward for local styles. A recorded local music travels
as far as its copies, so recording both displaces local performers and turns
local repertoires into exports.

**The copy cost is the gate.** What it costs to make a copy decides who can
publish and who can pirate. A pressed disc needed a master, a pressing plant,
and shellac or vinyl. A film print needed a laboratory, and a film reached
audiences only as physical reels. Recording therefore began as an oligopoly of
firms with factories: Victor, Columbia and the Gramophone Company in sound,
and Pathé and then the Hollywood studios in film. A state could control it
cheaply by licensing or seizing a few plants.

Magnetic tape put the copying machine in the home. The cassette and the
videocassette let small labels, regional repertoires, preachers, dissidents
and pirates copy on the same machines, and a state that wanted control had to
confiscate copies instead of licensing factories. That is `copy cost`, and
each fall in it arrives as a democratisation and a piracy crisis at once. It
is separate from MED-140's `production cost floor`, the cost of making the
master. Songs are cheap to originate and films are not, so cheap copying
decentralised music long before film, and film followed only where
production cheapened too.

**Sound brings back the language border.** A silent film crossed languages
with translated intertitles, so the largest producer could sell everywhere,
and Hollywood held most of the world's screens by the 1920s (Thompson,
Exporting Entertainment, 1985). Synchronised sound put language back into the
product. Films now had to be dubbed or subtitled, and songs had lyrics in one
language, so national and regional industries grew behind their language
borders: Indian talkies with songs from 1931, and Cairo for the
Arabic-speaking world. The largest single-language market still spread its
costs across the most viewers, which is MED-140's amortisation arithmetic
applied to film. Markets without a language border to shelter them answered
with screen quotas, Germany's from 1925 and Britain's from 1927.

## Social consequence

At home the gramophone replaced the parlour piano. Music in the house had
meant someone in the house who could play. Now it meant a machine and a shelf
of discs, and listening displaced playing as the household's musical habit
(design principle 2). The wind-up gramophone needed no electricity, which
carried recorded music into households years before broadcast radio (MED-130)
could reach them.

The cinema made a new public space. American nickelodeons charged five cents
from 1905, and silent films needed no English, so immigrant workers and their
families filled them. Young women went without chaperones, and courtship moved
into the dark. The star, a face and voice known to millions who never met the
person, is recording's social invention. The playback singer is its strangest
form: in Indian film the voice on the record belonged to one person and the
face on the screen to another.

Recording also made a new kind of memory. For the first time an unwritten
performance tradition could outlive its performers. The Berlin
Phonogramm-Archiv (1900) and field recordings from Bartók to Lomax preserved
music that had no notation, so an oral tradition became recoverable after it
had stopped being passed on.

The people displaced were ordinary working musicians rather than stars.
Recording made the few richer and the many redundant, and live performance
survived as the scarce, premium form rather than the ordinary one.

## Regional specifics

Asia shows the mechanism's range. Japan's benshi made every copy a live
performance again and delayed sound by years. India fused film and record into
one industry of film songs under a pressing-plant monopoly, then watched
cassettes break it open. The Middle East shows the language border producing
a hub, with Cairo supplying the whole Arabic-speaking market. It also shows
the cassette producing a public the state could not police; Egyptian and
Iranian cassette sermons belong to REL-258 and are not re-derived here. Africa
shows a style made continental by small merchants' labels, and in Nollywood
the first case where video lowered production and copying cost together. The
Soviet case shows a state that controlled every factory losing control to
X-ray film and tape. The Americas supply the industry's commercial core and,
in the race records, the segregated catalogue.

Europe began as the producer and lost that position within a decade. Pathé
was the largest film company in the world before 1914, and the war that
halted European production handed the world market to Hollywood; quotas were
the European answer. Propaganda film and the newsreel as instruments of
state belong to WAR-280. The argument that industrially produced film and
music pacify their audiences belongs to PHI-410.

## Contested

Whether recording homogenised the world's music and film or multiplied them
is disputed. The cultural-imperialism account (Schiller, Communication and
Cultural Domination, 1976; Tunstall, The Media Are American, 1977) holds that
a few metropolitan industries used recording to flood other markets with
their products, and Lomax warned of a cultural grey-out as local traditions
gave way to recorded imports. The opposite evidence is just as concrete.
Cheap recording created new local and regional industries and genres: cassette
culture in North India, Congolese rumba and Nollywood. It often did so through
piracy that also widened the market.

Both happened. Model the direction as conditional rather than picking a side.
Where copy cost is high and no language border protects a market, production
concentrates in the largest exporter. Where copy cost is low and language or
taste protects a market, local production multiplies. The same medium should
be able to produce both outcomes in different places and decades.

## Hook

**Performance becomes stock.** Recording converts a performance from a service
consumed on the spot into copies that can be stored, shipped and sold. A
performer's reach detaches from presence and keeps earning after the
performer stops, and unwritten traditions become recoverable once recorded.

**Superstar concentration.** With cheap copies, demand concentrates on the best
recorded performers. Local live performers lose ordinary paid work (COM-180),
and live performance survives as the premium form. A local style that gets
recorded becomes an export.

**`copy cost` is the gate.** High copy cost (pressing plants, film
laboratories) means few producers, and control is cheap for a state that
licenses or seizes the plants. Low copy cost (tape) admits small producers,
minority repertoires, preachers, dissidents and pirates together. A state
defending `information sovereignty` must then confiscate copies, at a cost
that rises as copies multiply. Falls in copy cost should always bring piracy
with them.

**Origination still costs.** MED-140's `production cost floor` governs the
master. Music decentralises when copying cheapens; film does so only when
production cheapens too, as it did with video.

**Language borders.** Silent film crosses languages and concentrates the market
in the largest producer. Sound divides markets by language and grows a hub
behind each border. Quotas are the defence of markets that lack one.
