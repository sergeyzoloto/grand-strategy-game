Sign count collapses from hundreds or thousands to a few dozen. The
training cost of literacy collapses with it, and literacy can therefore
stop being a profession. That is the entire mechanism, and the entry's
main work is refusing everything that has been claimed on top of it.

## Timing

Proto-Sinaitic from around -1800; Phoenician standardised at 22 signs by
around -1050 and spreading with trade; Greek vowel notation from around
-800; Aramaic and its Asian descendant family from the same period; Brahmi
and the Indic scripts from around -300; Ge'ez in the Horn on its own track.

## Mechanism

**A script with 22 signs can be learned in weeks. A script with two
thousand takes years.** Everything follows from that ratio. MED-010
establishes that early writing arrives bundled with a scribal class,
because the cost of entry is high enough that reading is a career. Reduce
the sign count by two orders of magnitude and the bundle comes apart: the
skill can be acquired by someone who intends to do something else with
their life, and the profession loses its basis.

The consequence is a fall in `intermediary dependence`. A merchant who can
keep their own accounts does not need a scribe, and does not have to trust
one. A soldier can read an order. A creditor can check a contract they are
party to. None of this makes a society literate — it makes literacy
*available* to people with a reason to want it, which is a different and
much weaker claim, and the difference between those two claims is the
subject of this entry's Contested section.

**The economy is in sign count, not in structure.** Three distinct designs
achieve it: the consonantal abjad (Phoenician, Aramaic, Arabic, Hebrew),
the full alphabet with vowels (Greek and its descendants), and the
alphasyllabary (Brahmi and the Indic family, and Ge'ez independently).
That three unrelated structures all produce the same effect establishes
that the mechanism is the number of things to memorise, not any particular
elegance of design — which matters, because the overclaim below usually
attaches to one specific structure.

**Who invents it, and who resists it, both follow from the mechanism.**
Proto-Sinaitic appears to come from people outside the scribal
establishment adapting its signs; Phoenician spreads through a merchant
network rather than an administration. Conversely, a literate class whose
position rests on an expensive script has a direct interest in keeping it
expensive, which is what the Korean case demonstrates with unusual clarity.
The gate falls when people outside the gate build the tool, and it is
defended by the people standing on it.

## Social consequence

The scribe's monopoly weakens but the scribe does not disappear, and the
distinction matters. What ends is the claim that reading *requires* a
specialist; what survives is that composing, formatting, and interpreting
official language still rewards one. The profession moves from holding a
technical monopoly to holding a conventional one — knowing the forms, the
formulae, and the register — which is a weaker position and a more durable
one.

Access also becomes uneven in a new way. Under a high-cost script, literacy
maps onto the scribal profession and nothing else. Under a cheap one, it
maps onto whoever has a use for it and a few weeks to spare, which means it
spreads through commerce, through soldiery, through religious practice, and
through households at quite different rates. Literacy stops being a
credential and becomes a distribution, and the shape of that distribution
is a fact about a society rather than about its script.

## Regional specifics

The Aramaic family deserves the weight the Greek one usually receives. As
the Achaemenid administrative script it spread across an empire and seeded
Hebrew, Syriac, Arabic, Sogdian and eventually Mongolian and Manchu
writing — a descendant family larger by reach than the Greek line and
routinely treated as a footnote to it. Brahmi seeded South and Southeast
Asia by a third structural route. Ge'ez acquired vowel marking
independently of Greek. The distribution of this technology is
overwhelmingly non-European and the entry should be built that way.

The two absences are both about constituency rather than access.
Mesoamerican scripts stayed with an elite that had no reason to cheapen
writing. East Asia had alphabetic scripts available for centuries and
declined them, partly because the logographic system was doing something
the alphabet cannot, and partly because the class positioned to
adopt it was the class it would have devalued. Hangul, designed in 1443 on
explicit phonemic principles and then resisted for centuries by the
literati, is the cleanest instance in the corpus of a cheaper technology
refused by the people whose standing depended on the expensive one.

## Contested

The strong causal claims — alphabet produces rationalism, abstraction,
democracy, or literacy — are not supported and the entry exists partly to
refuse them. Qing examination literacy was substantial on a logographic
script; alphabetic Europe was largely illiterate for two millennia. Either
fact alone is fatal to the strong version.

**Model script complexity as a modifier on `literacy training cost` and
nothing else.** It sets the price of producing a reader. What a society
does with readers is determined by institutions — schools, examinations,
church requirements, commercial demand — on which the script has no
bearing. The defensible residue is permissive: a low-sign-count script
makes non-professional literacy possible, so where other conditions favour
a reading public, one can form quickly. Necessary sometimes, sufficient
never.

## Hook

Cuts `literacy training cost` by roughly two orders of magnitude, which
unbundles literacy from the scribal profession and lowers `intermediary
dependence` for anyone with a reason to read — a merchant keeping their
own books, a soldier reading an order, a party checking their own contract.
It should be strictly permissive: cheap literacy makes a reading public
*possible* and never produces one, so a model in which adopting the
alphabet yields literacy, rationalism or representative institutions is
wrong on the evidence of both Qing China and most of European history.
The invention should come from outside the scribal establishment and the
resistance from inside it, since the class whose standing rests on an
expensive script has a direct interest in keeping it expensive — which is
what makes Hangul's centuries of elite refusal the diagnostic case rather
than an anomaly. And the economy is in sign count alone, not structure:
abjad, alphabet and alphasyllabary all deliver it.
