The vapor-compression cycle mechanically moves heat against its natural
gradient — compressing and expanding a working fluid to draw heat out
of an enclosed space and release it elsewhere — converting cooling from
something requiring harvested natural ice and insulated storage into an
on-demand, location-independent mechanical process, the direct
technical precondition `COM-230`'s later refrigerated shipping already
assumes exists.

## Timing

Jacob Perkins patents the first practical vapor-compression
refrigeration cycle in 1834; Ferdinand Carré's ammonia-absorption
system follows at mid-century; industrial-scale mechanical refrigeration
matures through the following decades, directly enabling the long-
distance refrigerated shipping `COM-230` documents from 1876.

## Mechanism

Moving heat from a colder space to a warmer one doesn't happen on its
own — it requires mechanical work, which the vapor-compression cycle
supplies by compressing a working fluid (raising its temperature above
the warm exterior, so it can shed heat there) and then letting it expand
and cool below the target space's temperature (so it can absorb heat
there instead), repeating continuously. This converts cooling capacity
from a fixed, seasonal, storage-dependent quantity (however much ice a
region could harvest and keep insulated through summer) into a
continuously-renewable mechanical output limited only by available
power and equipment, not by climate or season.

## Social consequence

Mechanical refrigeration removes the natural-ice supply chain's hard
seasonal and geographic ceiling entirely, letting food preservation and
long-distance perishable trade happen anywhere sufficient mechanical
power is available regardless of local climate — directly enabling the
export-oriented agricultural restructuring `COM-230`'s Argentine beef
case and `AGR-260`'s broader mechanization-era transformation both
depend on already existing.

## Regional specifics

John Gorrie's Florida ice-making machine pursues mechanical cooling for
an explicitly medical motive — treating fever patients — years before
the technology's later commercial food-industry application, showing
the same underlying mechanism can be developed toward quite different
initial purposes. Persian yakhchals achieve real, sustained ice storage
across a hot arid climate for over two millennia using no mechanical
process at all, a sophisticated passive-engineering answer to a related
problem this entry's active mechanical technique later supersedes for
industrial scale rather than being the first workable solution to
cooling.
