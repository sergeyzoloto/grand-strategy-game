Abraham Darby III's 1779 bridge at Coalbrookdale, the first major
structural span built from cast iron rather than stone or timber, is a
single, narrow, well-documented demonstration case of structural metal
replacing masonry at bridge scale — the same underlying substitution
`ENG-310`'s much later steel-frame buildings apply to walls rather than
spans.

## Timing

Built in 1779 across the River Severn at Coalbrookdale, using iron cast
at Darby's own family ironworks.

## Mechanism

Cast iron carries load in compression and tension differently from
stone (strong in compression, weak in tension) or timber (workable but
limited in span and durability), letting a single-arch iron span cross
a distance and bear a load that comparable stone arch construction
would require substantially more material and support to match — a
narrow, specific engineering demonstration of structural metal's
advantage over masonry at exactly the bridge scale this one crossing
was built to prove.

## Social consequence

The bridge becomes an immediate technical showpiece and a direct
advertisement for the surrounding ironworking district's own
capability, drawing visitors and imitators specifically because it
demonstrates a structural possibility (a single iron arch of that span)
no one had built before.

## Regional specifics

Comparable cast-iron structural bridge-building in the United States
lags Britain's example by several decades, consistent with Britain's
broader early Industrial Revolution lead in iron production capacity.

## Hook

A single-arch cast-iron span demonstrates structural metal's load-
bearing advantage over masonry at bridge scale — a narrow, specific
proof of concept anticipating, on a much smaller scale, the same
metal-replaces-masonry substitution `ENG-310`'s steel-frame buildings
apply to walls a century later.
