The size of a city is set by how far its residents can travel to work in the time they
are willing to spend. Every faster mode — omnibus, horse tram, railway, electric
streetcar, underground, automobile — extended that radius, opened new land to urban
use and raised its value. The value went to whoever owned the land, not to whoever
built the line, and that split shaped who built transit, who kept it and who tore it
out.

## Timing

From the omnibuses of Paris, London and New York around 1830 and the first horse
tramway in New York in 1832, through the railway suburbs of the mid-nineteenth
century, London's underground from 1863, the electric streetcar after 1888, the land
railways of Osaka and Tokyo from 1910 and Heliopolis from 1905, to the automobile city,
the urban expressway building of the 1950s and 1960s, and the removal of most tramways
outside a few regions by about 1970.

## Mechanism

**The commuting radius sets the city.** People everywhere have tended to spend about
an hour a day travelling, roughly half an hour each way. A walking city therefore
cannot extend much more than a few kilometres from its centre, which is why
pre-industrial cities were dense and crowded (HLT-070). Each faster mode enlarged the
`commuting radius` the same hour could cover: horse omnibuses and trams a little,
steam railways a great deal along their lines, electric streetcars (ENG-300) in every
direction, and the automobile (ENG-290) across the whole territory between the lines.
The area of land within reach grows with the square of the radius, so a modest gain in
speed opened a very large area of new urban land, and for arriving migrants that land
was a major part of a city's `urban absorption capacity` (MIG-170).

**The land-value gradient.** Land close to the centre is valuable because it saves
travel. When a line reaches distant farmland, the saving there is capitalised into its
price. A field worth little as pasture becomes worth many times more as building lots
the day a station opens. Rents fall with distance from the centre along a gradient, and
every new line flattens it, raising values at the edge and restraining them at the core.
Where tall steel buildings (ENG-310) concentrated office jobs in the centre, the lines
that carried workers to them made the concentration possible.

**Value goes to the landowner, not the line.** This is the core problem. A transit
company can charge a fare, but the fare cannot capture the value the line creates,
because riders can choose other lines or other modes, and fares were usually regulated
or fixed by franchise. The landowner near the station receives the rise in land value
without paying for the line. There are three responses:

- **The land play.** The builder owns the land. Huntington's Pacific Electric in Los
  Angeles ran at a loss and his land companies made the money. Empain built a railway
  to Heliopolis to sell desert lots. London's Metropolitan Railway built Metro-land on
  its own surplus land.
- **The integrated railway.** The Japanese private railways of Osaka and Tokyo owned the
  land, the houses, the terminal department stores and the entertainment at the end of
  the line, so value created by the trains came back to the railway, which stayed
  profitable for a century without subsidy. This third model is the most durable
  solution the period produced.
- **Regulation without capture.** Most North American street railways were private
  utilities with fares fixed at five cents and obligations to pave and maintain streets.
  They captured none of the land value they created, and once inflation after the First
  World War raised costs they deferred maintenance (ENG-065), cut service and failed.

**Rail shaped the city in lines; the car filled the spaces between.** A rail suburb
grows around stations, with walking distance to the station as its limit, so a rail
city grows in fingers along its lines. Underground railways (ENG-270) added capacity
inside the already-built core without taking surface land. Steam railways (COM-200)
first made commuting from separate towns possible. The automobile removed the
constraint of lines altogether, making all land within the radius reachable. Densities
fell as the built-up area spread across the land between the fingers.

**The reversal.** From the 1920s and above all after the Second World War, the
automobile city remade the city it inherited:

- **Tramways removed.** Many cities removed their trams: London by 1952, Paris by 1938,
  and nearly every North American city by the 1950s. The rails became `stranded
  capital`, and the buses that replaced them ran on streets they shared with cars.
- **Expressways through neighbourhoods.** Urban expressways were driven through existing
  neighbourhoods, usually poor and often Black in the United States: the Cross Bronx
  Expressway, I-95 through Miami's Overtown, I-94 through St Paul's Rondo. Clearance
  for roads and urban renewal displaced the residents with the least political
  power.
- **Density fell.** Suburbanisation subsidised by highway building and mortgage
  insurance lowered North American urban densities to levels far below those of
  European and East Asian cities.

**Branch on the city's prior state.** The automobile did not produce one outcome. It
depended on what the city was when the car arrived:

- **Shape fixed before the car.** Cities whose shape was set by rail, whose land-use
  law restricted edge growth (London's Green Belt), and whose states taxed fuel heavily
  kept dense cores and working transit even after removing trams.
- **Rail that paid for itself.** Cities with rail systems that captured land value kept
  building rail (Tokyo, Osaka).
- **Growth in the car era.** Cities that grew mainly in the automobile era, with cheap
  fuel, abundant land and transit companies already bankrupt, spread at low density
  (Los Angeles, Houston).
- **Cheap buses without a land play.** Cities that grew fast with cheap buses and no
  land company opened peripheries of self-built housing (Sao Paulo, and later most
  large cities of Latin America, South Asia and Africa).

The mode matters less than the institutional and fiscal state it met.

## Social consequence

At human scale the commuting radius decides where a household can live and how long
its working day is:

- **An artisan in London before 1860.** They live in a crowded court near their
  workplace because the walk is the only commute they can afford.
- **A clerk after the 1883 workmen's fares.** They take a cheap early train from a new
  terraced suburb.
- **A Boston family in 1890.** They buy a house on a streetcar line in a suburb that did
  not exist a decade before.
- **An Osaka salaryman in the 1930s.** They buy a Hankyu lot on instalment, ride the
  company's trains and shop at the company's store at Umeda.
- **A Soweto worker.** They spend hours each day travelling to a job in a city they are
  not allowed to live in.
- **A Sao Paulo family.** They buy a lot on the periphery and spend years building a
  house served by a private bus.
- **A resident of Overtown in the 1960s.** They watch an expressway demolish the
  neighbourhood to carry commuters past it.

Transport created a new geography of class. Those who could afford time and fare moved
outward to cheaper and larger homes. Those who could not stayed near work in crowded
housing, or were pushed to the far periphery. The landowner at the new station gained
without doing anything.

## Regional specifics

The regions are distinct routes, not variations on the North American suburb:

- **Japan.** Its private railways supply the most successful model of capturing
  transit value.
- **Europe.** London supplies workmen's fares, the underground, Metro-land and a
  legally fixed edge.
- **North America.** It supplies the streetcar land play, the fixed-fare utility's
  collapse, and the expressway that remade the core.
- **The Middle East.** Heliopolis is the land play outside the West, and Istanbul an
  early underground.
- **Latin America.** The colectivo and the private bus opened self-built peripheries.
- **South Asia.** Bombay's suburban rail made a linear city of long commutes, with the
  poor staying near work.
- **South Africa.** Apartheid imposed the commuting radius as segregation.

The absences are cities without land markets. Danwei China housed workers beside their
workplace and cycled them to it, and Soviet Moscow planned housing onto the periphery
and ended with density that rose away from the centre, the inverse of every market city.

## Contested

The decline of North American streetcars is disputed:

- **The conspiracy account** holds that General Motors, through National City Lines,
  bought and dismantled systems to sell buses. The companies were convicted in 1949 of
  conspiring to monopolise supply to transit companies.
- **Transport historians** argue the companies were already failing from fixed fares,
  deferred maintenance and automobile competition.

Model transit decline as conditional on the fare and maintenance regime.

## Hook

**Commuting radius.** A city's extent is set by its `commuting radius`, the distance
covered in about half an hour each way. Each faster mode enlarges it, and the new land
opened grows with the square of the radius, feeding `urban absorption capacity`.

**Land value.** Transit raises land values near stations, and the gain goes to
landowners, not line operators.

- **Land capture.** A transit builder that owns the land or the terminal businesses —
  the land play, the Japanese integrated railway — can fund lines indefinitely.
- **Fixed fares.** A fixed-fare utility captures nothing, defers maintenance, and
  fails when a competing mode arrives.

**Shape.** Rail cities grow in fingers around stations. The automobile fills the land
between, lowering density.

**Branching.** The automobile's effect should branch on the city's prior state: shape
set by rail, land-use law and fuel taxes preserve dense cores. Growth in the car era
with bankrupt transit produces low-density sprawl, and fast growth with cheap buses and
no land company produces self-built peripheries.

**Demolition.** Roads through existing neighbourhoods fall on residents with the least
political power.

**No land market.** Without a land market there is no gradient to capture, and planning
can invert the density profile.
