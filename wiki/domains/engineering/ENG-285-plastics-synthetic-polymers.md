Plastics are materials made to a specification from a stored hydrocarbon
stock instead of being grown, hunted or dug up. That one change ends the
land-bound supply of a whole class of materials, makes them cheap enough to
throw away, and makes what is thrown away last for centuries.

## Timing

Parkesine, a moulded nitrocellulose, was shown in London in 1862 and failed
commercially. The industry begins with Hyatt's celluloid, patented in the
United States in 1870. Baekeland's Bakelite (1907) is the first fully
synthetic plastic. Staudinger's macromolecular hypothesis (1920) and
Carothers's research at DuPont (nylon, 1935) turn polymers into designed
materials. Polyethylene (ICI, 1933), PVC, polystyrene and synthetic rubber
reach industrial scale in the 1930s and the war. The Ziegler and Natta
catalysts of 1953–54 make polyethylene and polypropylene cheap commodities.
World production rises from about 2 million tonnes in 1950 to about 380
million in 2015 (Geyer, Jambeck and Law, Science Advances, 2017). The entry
closes in 2019, when amendments to the Basel Convention put most traded
plastic scrap under its prior-consent procedure: the first global rule on
where land-based plastic waste may be shipped, arriving a century and a
half after the first plastic.

## Mechanism

**Specified, not found.** Before synthesis every material came from
somewhere. It was grown (timber, fibre, resin, silk, rubber), taken from
animals (ivory, horn, tortoiseshell), or mined and transformed (metal,
glass, ceramic). It came with whatever properties its source gave it, in
the quantity its land, herds or territory could yield. Polymer chemistry
reverses both terms. The properties become a specification: rigid or
flexible, clear, waterproof, insulating, moulded to any shape in one
operation. The supply becomes a function of plant capacity and hydrocarbon
feedstock. Staudinger's hypothesis and the research programme Carothers
ran at DuPont from 1928 moved polymer-making from trial and error to
chemistry. Nylon came out of a programme to understand how long molecular
chains form, and was then aimed at the market silk held.

**Semi-synthetics relocate the dependency; synthetics exit it.** The first
plastics were natural polymers chemically modified. Celluloid is nitrated
cellulose, which is guncotton, plasticised with camphor. The ivory it
replaced was therefore exchanged for camphor from the forests of Taiwan: a
new land-bound input under a new monopoly. Fully synthetic polymers break
the chain. Bakelite was made from coal-tar phenol (ENG-165). Petrochemistry
began around 1920 as a use for refinery gases and natural-gas liquids that
were otherwise burned or flared, a surplus of ENG-275's fixed ratio finding
a market. After 1950 almost the whole industry ran on ethylene and
propylene cracked from oil and natural gas. These are stored stocks that
cost no acreage, so plastics are the materials-side counterpart of the exit
ENG-055 describes for energy, and the same move ENG-320 made for nitrogen.
Fibres, rubber, resins and the ivory-and-horn class were the last large
families of materials still bound to land and animals after coal replaced
wood as fuel and iron replaced timber in structure. Synthesis brings them
across too.

The cost is concentration. Dozens of separate dependencies on particular
territories — plantations, forests, herds — collapse into one dependency on
hydrocarbons and on the plant that converts them. That is why oil (WAR-275)
becomes a question of materials as well as of fuel.

**A synthetic takes function, not status.** Substitution is never total,
and where it stops is predictable. A synthetic displaces the part of a
natural material's demand that bought what the material does, and leaves
the part that bought what it is. Nylon took stockings and parachutes from
silk, and silk kept the luxury trade. Celluloid took ivory's billiard balls
and combs, and the carving trade went on buying ivory. Where the natural
material holds a property the synthetic cannot yet match, it keeps a niche:
natural rubber still makes up a large share of world rubber because heavy
tyres need its resistance to heat build-up. Each substitution therefore
strands a specific share of an incumbent's capital rather than all of it,
through ENG-265's `stranded capital`. It lands on the places that grew,
tapped or reeled the natural material.

**Cheap enough to throw away.** Polymer prices fell with the scale of the
plant. Once a container or wrapper cost less than the labour of washing,
returning or mending it, using it once became the rational choice.
`disposability` is therefore a price and not a property of the material. It
rises as plastics cheapen against wages, and falls wherever goods are
scarce or labour is cheap. That is why the same polyethylene bag was washed
and dried for reuse in a Soviet kitchen and discarded in an American one.
Packaging, the most disposable use, became the largest single market for
plastic (Geyer, Jambeck and Law, 2017).

**The property that makes it useful is the property that makes it last.**
Plastics are made to resist water, rot and most microbes, so the product
outlasts its use by centuries. About 8,300 million tonnes had been made by
2015. Of the roughly 6,300 million tonnes that had become waste, about 9%
had been recycled and 12% burned, and 79% sat in landfills or the open
environment (Geyer, Jambeck and Law, 2017). The waste is a stock, not a
flow: HLT-085's `persistent contaminant stock`, with one behaviour that
lead does not have. **Plastic fragments.** Sunlight and abrasion break it
into ever smaller pieces without reducing its mass. The number of
particles, and the range of organisms small enough to ingest them, keeps
rising after additions stop.

Like lead, it is invisible at the scale that matters until instruments
exist. Plastic particles were recorded on the surface of the Sargasso Sea in
1972 (Carpenter and Smith, Science). Fragments too small to see were named
microplastics in 2004 (Thompson et al., Science). Particles were found in
human blood in 2022. The stock's geography follows water and trade rather
than use. It settles where rivers, currents and the waste trade deliver it,
and it grows fastest where consumption arrives before municipal collection
does.

## Social consequence

At household scale plastics arrived as better versions of ordinary things.
Across Africa and South Asia, within a generation, the bucket, the basin,
the jerrycan and the bag replaced the clay pot, the gourd, the basket and
the tin. They were lighter, did not break, and were cheap enough that
losing one did not matter. The potter, the basket-maker and the tinsmith
lost their trade on COM-180's pattern, with no factory anywhere near them:
the goods that displaced them arrived through the market already made. In
the United States the material entered the home through women's social
networks, with Tupperware sold at parties in kitchens from the late 1940s,
and nylon stockings drew queues of thousands when they returned after the
war.

Disposability changed household habit, not only household goods. Life
magazine celebrated "Throwaway Living" in 1955 as freedom from washing up.
Mending, returning bottles and keeping containers gave way to discarding
them, and the change came from the price of the goods rather than from any
campaign to change behaviour (design principle 2). In hospitals
disposability is the point. The plastic blood bag (1950) and the disposable
syringe made sterile single use cheap, turning asepsis from a discipline
into a matter of supply. Where scarcity forces their reuse, they spread
blood-borne infection instead.

The waste made a livelihood. Informal collectors, such as Cairo's zabbaleen
and the pickers and reprocessing workshops of Mumbai's Dharavi, sort by
hand and are paid by weight. The recycling that exists in poor countries is
manual labour priced by what a tonne of scrap fetches. In rich countries
recycling carries more authority than effect. From 1988 the chasing-arrows
resin code put a recycling symbol on almost every plastic item, while about
9% of all plastic waste has ever been recycled. That is design principle 3
in a new register. Whether the industry promoted recycling knowing it could
not work at scale has been alleged (NPR and PBS Frontline, 2020) and taken
to court (California's suit against ExxonMobil, 2024), and should not be
scripted.

## Regional specifics

Asia stands on both sides of this entry. The first synthetics were aimed at
materials Asia supplied: Taiwanese camphor, Indian shellac, Japanese silk,
and Malayan and Indonesian rubber. Each substitution stranded producers
whose income rested on one crop or forest product, on FIN-205's pattern of
a rent ended by a synthetic. Half a century later the same region became
the destination of the waste. China took roughly 45% of the world's traded
plastic scrap from 1992 (Brooks, Wang and Jambeck, Science Advances, 2018)
until its National Sword ban took effect in January 2018. The flow then
moved to Malaysia, Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia and Turkey, which
restricted it in turn. Exporting scrap as recyclables sent the stock to
wherever enforcement was weakest. That is `regulatory arbitrage` on the
pattern of MAR-337's shipbreaking beaches, and it lasted until the Basel
amendments of 2019.

The feedstock follows the endowment. The United States built on oil and
natural gas. Germany, coal-rich and oil-poor, made Buna rubber and early
plastics from coal. The Soviet Union made its first synthetic rubber from
potato alcohol. The Gulf held the cheapest feedstock on earth and flared it
until the 1980s. China has built coal-to-olefins plants since 2010,
reopening the German route for another coal-rich, oil-poor economy. The
result is the same polymer at a different cost, not the same deficit
(design principle 5). Africa's ivory marks the limit on the other side: a
substitute that existed for a century did not touch demand for what ivory
signified.

The sea carries the stock to places that never used it. MARPOL Annex V
banned the dumping of plastics from ships from the end of 1988, but most
marine plastic comes from land. An estimated 4.8 to 12.7 million tonnes
entered the ocean from coastal countries in 2010 alone (Jambeck et al.,
Science, 2015), and the Pacific islands receive it from rivers and cities
they have never seen.

Restriction follows a state's position in the supply chain. Bangladesh
banned thin polyethylene bags in 2002 after bags had choked drains during
floods. Rwanda (2008) and Kenya (2017) followed with some of the world's
strictest bans. A state that imports resin loses little by banning; a state
that exports oil and petrochemicals favours managing waste over limiting
production. That split has divided the global treaty negotiations opened
in 2022.

## Contested

**Harm.** Plastic particles and additives are measurable in water, food,
air and human tissue. Whether they damage health at the doses populations
carry is not established. The strongest evidence is associational: patients
whose carotid plaque contained plastic particles had more heart attacks,
strokes and deaths over the following three years (Marfella et al., NEJM,
2024), which does not show that the plastic caused them. The measurement
methods are disputed too. Samples are easily contaminated, and some
pyrolysis-based methods have been challenged for reading body fats as
polyethylene. Regulators disagree about additives such as bisphenol A and
the phthalates. What the stock does to bodies is HLT's read. HLT-085 carries
the stock mechanic; no HLT entry yet carries plastics. Model the stock's
size as known and its per-unit penalty as a hidden, world-specific draw from
a wide range. Detection research narrows the range but never fixes it.
Never script it as harmless or as a collapse.

**Comparison.** Life-cycle assessments often find a plastic bag or package
lower in energy and carbon than the glass, metal, paper or cotton it
replaced. It is lighter, and packaging cuts food waste. The UK Environment
Agency's 2011 carrier-bag assessment found that a cotton bag has to be used
many times over to match a single-use polyethylene bag on climate impact.
Those assessments count persistence poorly or not at all, so the verdict
depends on how impact categories are weighted rather than on any disputed
fact. Track energy and persistence as separate stats, and never net them
into one environmental score. A material can be better on one and worse on
the other at the same time.

## Hook

**Synthesis turns a territorial dependency into a plant.** Polymer
synthesis should be gated on chemistry (NAT-160) and a hydrocarbon
feedstock: coal tar first (ENG-165), then refinery and cracker streams
(ENG-275). Each synthetic substitute takes over the functional share of a
natural material's demand. It cuts `strategic input dependency` on the
territory that supplied it, and strands that territory's land, capital and
skill (`stranded capital`). Whether a cut-off natural supply can be replaced
at all is decided by the chemical industry and feedstock a state already
holds when the shock arrives (design principle 1).

**Relocate, then exit.** Semi-synthetics move the dependency to a new
land-bound input, as celluloid moved it from ivory to camphor. Only full
synthesis removes it, and in removing it concentrates many separate
material dependencies onto one hydrocarbon supply. An oil embargo should
cut materials as well as fuel.

**Function, not status.** The synthetic takes the share of demand bought
for what a material does. The share bought for what it is survives, and
can rise in value. A natural material with a property the synthetic lacks
keeps a niche.

**Feedstock follows endowment.** The same polymer is buildable from coal,
farm alcohol, oil or gas at different cost. A state rich in coal pays more
for the product; it is not barred from it (design principle 5).

**Disposability is a price.** `disposability` rises as polymer prices fall
against the labour cost of reuse, and falls where goods are scarce. It moves
household habit from repair to replacement (design principle 2). It sets the
inflow to the `persistent contaminant stock`.

**The stock fragments and travels.** The plastic stock never decays. It
breaks into smaller particles, so its ingestible share rises after
additions stop. It settles where rivers, currents and the waste trade carry
it rather than where it was used, and accumulates fastest where consumption
arrives before collection. It feeds `toxic burden` at a rate hidden from the
player and uncertain even after detection research (see Contested).

**Recycling's authority exceeds its effect** (design principle 3). Recycling
capacity should raise public acceptance of the material far more than it
lowers the stock. Exporting scrap moves the stock abroad under `regulatory
arbitrage` until the importing state closes the route.
