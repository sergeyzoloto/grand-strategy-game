This entry owns a mill's power source and drive-train as engineering
technique — waterwheel or windmill sail, and the gearing that steps
that rotation down to a millstone's working speed — distinct from
`AGR-060`'s ownership of the labor-and-economic consequence of removing
hand-grinding's bottleneck, the same technique-versus-consequence split
already established between `ENG-020` and `AGR-100`.

## Timing

Chinese water-powered mechanisms (Du Shi's bellows, 31 CE, and
subsequent milling and trip-hammer applications) are the earliest
documented case; Persian vertical-axis windmills mature by around 850;
European horizontal-axis watermills and, later, post-mill windmills
spread widely by 1200.

## Mechanism

A waterwheel or windmill sail converts the kinetic energy of flowing
water or moving air into rotational mechanical power at the wheel or
sail's own axis — gearing then steps that rotation down (trading
rotational speed for torque) to whatever working speed a millstone,
bellows, or trip-hammer actually needs, the same rotation-speed-versus-
force trade-off `ENG-060`'s simple machines describe applied
specifically to continuous rather than intermittent power. The specific
engineering challenge varies by power source: a watermill needs a
reliable flow and a wheel design (undershot, overshot) matched to the
local drop and current, while a windmill needs a mechanism for turning
the whole sail assembly to face shifting wind direction.

## Social consequence

A mill converts a continuously-available natural power source into
mechanical work without requiring continuous human or animal effort at
all, freeing whichever labor previously turned a hand-quern or worked a
bellows manually — the same labor-freeing consequence `AGR-060`
describes, but arriving here as a fixed, site-specific capital
investment (a mill requires a suitable water flow or consistently windy
site to be worth building) rather than something available to any
household with grain to grind.

## Regional specifics

Chinese water-powered mechanisms reach practical application markedly
earlier than either the Persian or European cases, developing gearing
and transmission solutions on an independent trajectory. Persian
vertical-axis panemone windmills predate the more commonly-cited
European horizontal-axis design by roughly two and a half centuries,
a real and often-overlooked priority case. The pre-contact Americas'
complete absence of wheel-and-gearing milling technology leaves grain
processing there purely hand-labor-gated throughout, consistent with
the broader absence of constructed-water-power and draft-animal
traditions already documented elsewhere in this record.

## Hook

A mill converts a site-specific natural power source into continuous
mechanical work without ongoing human or animal effort — a modifier on
the power-source-and-gearing side specifically, freeing the same
hand-grinding labor `AGR-060` already tracks on the consequence side,
but only where a suitable water flow or wind exposure makes the fixed
construction investment worthwhile in the first place.
