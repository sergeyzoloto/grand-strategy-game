A revolt in one power's periphery is an asset to its rivals. Supporting it is
cheap, deniable and costly to the target, so colonial and peripheral revolts are
internationalised almost automatically. The revolt's leaders learn to seek foreign
sponsors, which changes what the revolt becomes.

## Timing

From Louis XIV's backing of the Rákóczi revolt from 1703 and of the Jacobite risings,
through French intervention in the American Revolution from 1776, British
involvement in Spanish American independence from the 1810s, the Greek War of
Independence and Navarino in 1827, British sponsorship of the Arab Revolt from 1916
and Japanese sponsorship of Southeast Asian nationalists from 1941, to Katanga in
1960, Bangladesh in 1971 and the abandonment of the Iraqi Kurdish revolt in 1975.
After 1945 the mechanism is carried chiefly by WAR-340's proxy wars, and this entry
points there rather than repeating them.

## Mechanism

**The inter-polity half only.** A peripheral revolt has an internal cycle of
grievance, rebellion, repression and concession between the centre and its
province. That cycle is imperial governance, not diplomacy, and it has no home yet
in LAW or WAR. It is recorded as a gap and not carried here. This entry carries
only what makes the revolt an international event: the rival's incentive to
sponsor it, the revolt's incentive to seek a sponsor, and the obligation that
sponsorship leaves behind.

**A rival's revolt is cheap to support and expensive to suppress.** Supplying arms,
money, volunteers or a safe haven to rebels in a rival's periphery costs a fraction
of fighting the rival directly. It forces the rival to spend far more on garrisons
and campaigns to hold its own territory, and it can be denied or disguised. France
spent a great deal on America and still paid less than Britain did to lose it.
Every power with a vulnerable periphery is therefore exposed to every rival that can
reach it. That exposure is why the norm of non-interference in internal affairs
(DIP-100) is so regularly breached at exactly this point, since a revolt offers both
an opportunity and a claim that the rebels represent a legitimate cause.

**Rebels learn to seek sponsors.** A revolt that cannot win alone looks abroad for
arms, recognition and intervention. Seeking them reshapes it. Leaders who can
negotiate with foreign courts gain ground over those who cannot. The movement's aims
bend toward what a sponsor will support. Its timing follows the sponsor's wars
rather than its own readiness. The Greek revolt succeeded when it found fleets, and
the Arab Revolt was organised around a British campaign. Revolts in the periphery
become, in part, projects of the powers that fund them.

**Recognition is a weapon.** Recognising a rebel government is an act of war by
other means, and withholding recognition is a way to keep a revolt dependent.
DIP-120 carries recognition of revolutionary regimes, including Haiti's decades of
non-recognition. This entry adds that recognition can be granted or delayed to
extract terms from the rebels, not only withheld to protect the recognising power's
own order.

**The sponsor's cost can be ruinous.** France won the American war and lost its own
solvency. It borrowed over a billion livres at high cost, lacking the funded,
tradable public debt that let Britain borrow cheaply (FIN-110), and the resulting
fiscal crisis opened the way to 1789. A power that sponsors a rival's revolt takes on
a war whose financing can outlast the victory.

**Independence arrives with dependency attached.** A movement that won with foreign
help owes something to the helper, and the obligation often outlasts the fight. It
takes several forms:

- **A king chosen by others.** Greece received a Bavarian prince, a guaranteed loan
  and decades of interference from its Protecting Powers.
- **Loans and commercial dominance.** The new Spanish American republics took London
  loans and British commercial dominance.
- **Mandates instead of independence.** The Arab Revolt received British and French
  mandates.
- **A new occupier.** The Burmese and Indonesian nationalists Japan sponsored found
  themselves under a different master.

Dependency is not automatic. A sponsored movement escapes its sponsor when the
sponsor weakens, as Aung San did in 1945, or when it can decline its obligations, as
the United States did in refusing to join France's war in 1793. Sponsorship can also
end abruptly when the sponsor gets what it wanted from the target: the Iraqi Kurds
were dropped in 1975 the moment Iran settled with Iraq.

**The counter-move is non-alignment.** Newly independent states, many of which had
themselves been sponsored revolts, organised to avoid becoming instruments of the
great powers' rivalry. The Bandung conference of 1955 gathered Asian and African
states around principles of non-interference and mutual respect for sovereignty,
and the Non-Aligned Movement from 1961 (DIP-180) institutionalised the refusal to
be a proxy. It was the peripheries turning the mechanism's lesson into a policy.

## Social consequence

At human scale a sponsored revolt draws in foreigners and exiles:

- **Philhellene volunteers** died at Missolonghi.
- **British legionnaires** fought in the Andes.
- **Japanese officers** trained young Burmese nationalists on Hainan.
- **Belgian officers** commanded Katangese gendarmes.

On the other side, exiled leaders lived on a sponsor's pension, waiting for a war
that would make them useful: Rákóczi in France and then Ottoman exile, Jacobite
courts abroad.

For the population in revolt, sponsorship raises both the odds of winning and the
scale of the war, because it brings a great power's resources into a local conflict.
It also raises the risk of abandonment. People who rose because a sponsor was
behind them are exposed when the sponsor settles with their ruler, as the Kurds were
in 1975 and the Jacobite clans after 1746. The guerrilla struggle this often becomes
is carried in WAR-240.

## Regional specifics

The mechanism runs across every region and is not a European export:

- **Europe.** It supplies the early forms: France backing Hungarian and Jacobite
  revolts to tie down its rivals, then dropping them.
- **The Americas.** They carry the decisive instance in French intervention in
  America, and the dependency cost in the London-financed republics of Spanish
  America.
- **The Mediterranean.** The Greek revolt shows great-power fleets winning an
  independence and then governing it.
- **The Middle East.** It shows both the broken promise of the Arab Revolt and the
  abrupt abandonment of the Kurds.
- **Southeast Asia.** Japanese sponsorship became occupation, and a sponsored army
  turned on its sponsor.
- **South Asia.** India decided a revolt in Pakistan's east directly in 1971. The
  1857 rebellion is the absence: no rival could reach India, so the revolt stayed
  unsponsored and was crushed.
- **Africa.** It carries both directions: a former colonial power backing a
  secession in a new state, and a newly independent state backing revolt in a
  colony.

From 1945 the superpowers' sponsorship of anticolonial and civil-war factions under
nuclear deterrence is carried in WAR-340. Bandung and the Non-Aligned Movement are
the counter-move by states determined not to be sponsored again.

## Contested

How far sponsorship shapes a revolt, rather than merely assisting it, is disputed.
One reading treats revolts as internally driven with foreign help at the margin. The
other holds that the need for a sponsor reshapes a movement's leadership and aims,
and binds its independence to dependency.

Model sponsorship as raising a revolt's chance of success while adding a standing
obligation to the sponsor. The obligation should scale with the share of capacity
the sponsor supplied and with the sponsor's strength when independence arrives, so
that it can bind tightly, as with Greece, or be escaped, as with Burma.

## Hook

**A rival's periphery is a target.** A revolt there offers any power that can reach
it a cheap, deniable way to impose large costs on the rival. Sponsorship follows
whenever access and gain coincide, and a revolt no rival can reach stays
unsponsored.

**Sponsorship reshapes the revolt.** It raises the revolt's chance of success and
the scale of the war. Leadership, aims and timing bend toward the sponsor, and
recognition becomes a lever for extracting terms from the rebels.

**The sponsor pays too.** Its war can strain its own `sovereign credit capacity`
past the point of victory.

**Independence carries an obligation.** It is a standing dependency, owed as
`protection rent` to the sponsor. The dependency is heavier the more capacity the
sponsor supplied and the stronger the sponsor remains. It is escapable if the
sponsor weakens, and it is abruptly cancelled if the sponsor settles with the
target.

**The counter-move.** Newly independent states can coordinate in a non-aligned bloc
to refuse proxy status.
