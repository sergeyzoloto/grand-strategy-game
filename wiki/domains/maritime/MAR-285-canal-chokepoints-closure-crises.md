A canal's value is that it abolishes a longer route. Closing it does more than stop
traffic: it brings back, for as long as it lasts, a route the world's fleet has been
optimised away from. The fleet then rebuilds itself for that route, and the new
ships do not un-build when the canal reopens.

## Timing

From the Suez Canal's opening in 1869 and Britain's purchase and occupation of 1875
and 1882, through Kiel in 1895, Panama in 1914 and the Montreux regime in 1936, to
the Suez crisis of 1956 and the long closure of 1967-75. The Panama handover follows
in 1999, the Neopanamax locks in 2016, and the Ever Given's six-day blockage of Suez
in 2021, with drought-driven transit restrictions at Panama as the contemporary
form.

## Mechanism

**The boundary with COM-130, argued rather than assumed.** COM-130 owns the
control mechanism: whoever holds a strait or canal levies tolls, bases fleets and
holds diplomatic leverage over the traffic using it. This entry is written because
two mechanisms it carries are distinct from control and absent there.

- **Closure changes the fleet.** Closure retroactively reinstates the route the
  canal abolished, and the fleet's capital adapts to it permanently.
- **The lock sets the standard.** A canal's dimensions become a global
  shipbuilding constraint.

Neither is about who collects the toll.

**Closure reinstates the long route.** A canal shortens a voyage so much that
shipping reorganises around it. Ship sizes, schedules, port calls and freight rates
all assume the shorter route. When the canal closes, every voyage that used it
reverts to the long way around. Suez closure means the Cape; Panama closure means
Cape Horn or the Strait of Magellan. The cost is not a toll forgone. It is thousands
of added miles per voyage across the whole fleet.

**The fleet adapts, and the adaptation outlasts the closure.** This is the entry's
central claim, with a necessary correction: **supertanker growth predates 1967.**
Economies of scale in carrying crude oil were already pushing tankers larger through
the late 1950s and early 1960s. The first Suez closure of 1956-57 spurred it, and
Japanese yards drove it; a 200,000-ton tanker was afloat in 1966. The 1967-75
closure did not cause the trend. It made it permanent. With the canal shut
indefinitely, shipowners committed to hulls sized for the Cape route, too large to
transit Suez loaded, because the Cape was now the only route. The very large and
ultra large crude carriers of the 1970s were built for a world without the canal.
When it reopened in 1975 they could not use it, so they kept sailing the Cape.
Egypt answered by deepening the canal in 1980 and building the SUMED pipeline across
the country in 1977 to capture oil the canal could no longer carry. A closure
changed the asset base on both sides: the ships grew to fit the long route, and the
canal and a pipeline were rebuilt to win back ships it had lost. The capital was
specific to a route, with high `capital specificity`, so the choice made during the
closure became the fleet's shape for decades.

**The lock becomes a world standard.** A lock admits ships up to a fixed beam,
length and draught, so every shipowner who wants a vessel that can use the canal
builds to those limits. Panamax and Suezmax are ship classes defined by a canal, not
by any property of the sea or the cargo. An engineering decision made in one country
sets hull dimensions worldwide:

- **Warships.** American battleships were held to Panamax beam for thirty years.
- **Kiel.** The canal had to be rebuilt when dreadnoughts outgrew it (MAR-330).
- **Container ships.** They were sized to Panamax until owners chose to build
  post-Panamax hulls that gave up the canal for scale (MAR-370).

When Panama opened larger locks in 2016, it reset the global limit, and a generation
of Panamax ships lost their size advantage overnight. The standard moves only when
the lock is rebuilt, and each rebuild re-sets the world's fleet.

**Closure is a lever and a hostage.** A state that holds a canal can close it, as
Egypt did in 1956 and 1967 and Turkey can at the straits under Montreux. The lever
cuts both ways. Egypt lost its canal revenue for eight years and the region's trade
paid the rerouting cost. The holder's `protection rent` from the passage exists only
while the passage is open, so closure spends it.

**Accidental closure shows the concentration.** The Ever Given, grounded
across Suez in March 2021, blocked a canal that carries a large share of world trade
for six days and left hundreds of ships waiting. Drought that lowered Panama's Gatun
Lake in 2023 forced transit restrictions. Neither was a political act. Both showed
that a route optimised around a single passage has no fallback, and in 2023-24
attacks on Red Sea shipping sent fleets back around the Cape once more.

## Social consequence

At human scale the canal is a workforce and a town. Pilots, lock operators and canal
administrators run it, and the 1956 Suez crisis turned on them. British and French
pilots walked out after nationalisation expecting the canal to seize up; Egyptian,
Greek and other pilots kept it running, which removed the claim that only Europeans
could operate it. The crews of the fifteen ships trapped in the Great Bitter Lake
from 1967 lived aboard for years, forming their own small community and postal
system.

For economies organised around a canal, closure is a sudden rise in the cost of
everything imported and exported along the route. South Asian and East African trade
with Europe, which Suez had made cheap, became expensive again for eight years. Port
cities at the canal's approaches lost business. Shipyards that could build the
larger ships gained it. The closure redistributed shipbuilding and port activity
toward those able to serve the long route.

The Suez Canal was dug largely by Egyptian corvee labour until 1864, and Nasser's
nationalisation in 1956 followed the withdrawal of Western finance for the Aswan High
Dam. ENG-075 carries both as megaprojects of mobilisation and legitimacy.

## Regional specifics

Suez carries most of the entry: the canal as imperial asset in 1875 and 1882, the
nationalisation and the pilots in 1956, and the long closure that made the
supertanker permanent. Panama carries the dimension-standard mechanism most
clearly:

- **Panamax** as a ship class.
- **Battleships** held to the lock's beam.
- **Handover** to Panama in 1999.
- **Neopanamax** locks re-setting the world's fleet in 2016.

Kiel shows a canal rebuilt because warships outgrew it, and then internationalised
by treaty and court. The Turkish Straits under Montreux carry the closure lever over
a whole sea's naval access. East Asia supplies the shipyards that answered the
closure, and South Asia the trade that paid for it.

Suez was a steam route from the start (MAR-038): its value depended on ships that no
longer needed the wind, and the oil those supertankers carried (WAR-275) is what
made the closure matter.

## Hook

A canal abolishes a longer route. Closure temporarily reinstates that route for all
traffic, at the cost of the full detour, not a lost toll, and it spends the holder's
`protection rent` for as long as it lasts.

**Closure outlives itself.** A closure long enough to shape investment makes
shipowners commit hulls of high `capital specificity`, sized for the long route. Those
hulls cannot use the canal when it reopens, so the closure permanently changes the
fleet and forces the canal to enlarge to win traffic back. Supertanker growth should
be an existing trend that closure locks in rather than creates.

**The lock sets the size limit.** Lock dimensions cap hull size worldwide for every
owner who wants canal access. Ship classes are defined by the canal, warship and
container-ship design is held to them, and enlarging the locks resets the global
standard.

**Concentration is fragility.** A route optimised around a single passage with no
parallel canal has no fallback. Accidents, drought and conflict each produce a
closure crisis whatever the owner intends.
