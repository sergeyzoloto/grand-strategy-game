Gunpowder artillery mounted on a hull, aimed and fired broadside or from
a bow chase — the weapon technology every warship-type entry in this
domain from the age of sail onward assumes exists and keeps improving on
its own timeline.

## Timing

Shipboard cannon appear from around 1350, initially as a supplement to
boarding and ramming rather than a replacement for it; broadside gunnery
matures as the primary naval combat method through the 1500s-1600s;
Henri-Joseph Paixhans's explosive shell gun, developed through the 1820s
and demonstrated decisively at the 1853 Battle of Sinop, replaces solid
shot with a shell that detonates inside a wooden hull, ending wooden
warship viability by the 1858 armored response (MAR-200).

## Mechanism

Mounting gunpowder artillery on a hull converts a warship's combat value
from crew size and boarding capability (MAR-040's original logic) into
gun count, caliber, and broadside weight — a shift that only pays off
once guns are numerous and reliable enough to decide an engagement before
two ships close to boarding range. Paixhans's explosive shell is a
second, sharper threshold within the same weapon lineage: a shell that
detonates inside a wooden hull does damage no equivalent weight of solid
shot ever could, converting naval gunnery from a threat wooden
construction could absorb into one it structurally cannot.

## Social consequence

Gun count becomes a directly countable, comparable measure of a
warship's value long before line-of-battle doctrine (MAR-210) formalizes
that count into an official "rate" — the accounting habit precedes and
enables the institution built around it, the same direction of causation
seen elsewhere in this domain where a raw capability matures before the
doctrine organizing it catches up.

## Regional specifics

The Mediterranean's continued reliance on galley tactics and boarding
actions well into the broadside-gunnery era, Lepanto being the clearest
case, shows the weapon's adoption and its full tactical displacement of
older combat forms were not the same event — a fleet can carry guns for
generations before gunnery alone decides its battles. East Asian naval
forces fielding shipboard cannon without developing an equivalent
gun-count-based fleet doctrine shows the weapon and the accounting system
built around it in Europe are separable achievements, consistent with the
broader absence of line-of-battle tactics already noted for the region.

## Hook

Naval gunnery converts a warship's combat value from crew and boarding
capability into a countable, comparable gun count — a gate every
warship-type entry from line-of-battle doctrine (MAR-210) onward assumes
exists and improves on its own timeline, with the explosive-shell variant
(Paixhans) later forcing the armor response (MAR-200) that resets the
entire naval hierarchy.
