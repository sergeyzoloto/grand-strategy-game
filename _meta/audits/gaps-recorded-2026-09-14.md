# Gaps Recorded — 2026-09-14

Gaps identified during the MAR, DIP, LAW and ENG verification passes of 2026-09-14
and left unwritten by decision. None is fixed here. Each records what is
absent, where the nearest existing coverage stops, and what a future entry or
extension would need to carry.

---

## 1. Nineteenth-century steam freight rate collapse — absent from the corpus

**Status:** gap. No entry.

**What exists.** `MAR-370` Containerization covers the collapse in shipping cost
from 1956 to 1990 only. `COM-200` Railways covers overland bulk transport. `MAR-038`
Winds, Currents, and Sailing Seasons ends at the steam transition around 1870 and
carries the conversion of routes into timetables, but not the cost series that
followed. `MIG-180` carries the passenger side of scheduled steam service.

**What is missing.** The fall in ocean freight rates between roughly 1840 and
1913. It was driven by compound and triple-expansion engines, steel hulls, the Suez
Canal, coaling networks and the telegraph's effect on ship scheduling, and it
lowered the cost of moving bulk grain, coal and ore across oceans far enough to
integrate commodity markets globally. The documented consequences include:

- **Grain.** Transatlantic grain price convergence and the resulting agricultural
  crisis in Europe after the 1870s.
- **Mass migration.** It became cheap.
- **Commodity trades.** Frozen meat and bulk trades that were impossible under
  sail became viable.

The first globalisation's trade boom is usually attributed to this cost collapse
rather than to tariff policy.

**Placement.** MAR, under the boundary rule that maritime trade infrastructure and
practice is MAR. It would sit between `MAR-260` Clippers and `MAR-370`.

## 2. Mutiny as a mechanism — thin inside MAR-230

**Status:** gap inside an existing entry. Extend `MAR-230`, or split if the
mechanism proves substantial enough to need its own Mechanism section.

**What exists.** `MAR-230` Naval Impressment and the Maritime Labor Force is an
impressment entry dated 1664–1815. Mutiny appears only in its Social consequence,
as the Spithead and Nore mutinies of 1797 illustrating collective grievance among
pressed and volunteer crews.

**What is missing.** Mutiny as a mechanism in its own right: why a crew under
discipline acts collectively against its officers, and when that action spreads
from a ship to a fleet and from a fleet to a state. The absent cases include:

- **The Potemkin, 1905.**
- **Kiel and Wilhelmshaven, 1918.** The mutinies that began the German revolution.
- **Kronstadt, 1921.**
- **Invergordon, 1931.** A pay-cut mutiny that helped force Britain off the gold
  standard.
- **The Royal Indian Navy mutiny of 1946.** Already referenced in `WAR-037` as a
  demobilisation-era colonial case.

The mechanism links naval labour conditions to regime crises in a way no current
entry carries: a fleet is a concentrated, armed, organised workforce within reach
of the capital's ports.

**Placement.** MAR. If written as a new entry, it would need a date range past 1815,
which `MAR-230` does not cover.

## 3. Port labour — proposal for its own entry

**Status:** proposal. Not a gap inside `MAR-230`, which is entirely shipboard.

**What exists.** One sentence in `MAR-240` Docks, noting that large dock complexes
concentrate a waterfront labour class and that dock strikes recur. One sentence in
`MAR-370` Containerization, noting that containers eliminated the dockworker
workforce.

**What the entry would carry.** Roughly 1850–1990:

- **Casual hiring by ship arrival.** Demand for labour spikes when a ship docks and
  vanishes when it sails, so hiring is daily, at the gate, from a permanent surplus
  of men.
- **Gang bosses and foremen.** They control who is picked, and that control breeds
  patronage, corruption and ethnic or kinship monopolies over particular docks.
- **A small workforce able to halt national trade.** The concentration and
  indispensability of dockworkers give them strike leverage far beyond their
  numbers. Cases include the 1889 London dock strike, the 1934 West Coast
  waterfront strike, and the Mombasa and Durban dock strikes of the 1940s.
- **Decasualisation.** Schemes such as Britain's National Dock Labour Scheme of 1947
  and hiring halls elsewhere converted casual labour into registered employment.
- **Elimination by containerisation.** Containers destroyed the workforce whose
  leverage had forced decasualisation, which `MAR-370` records from the other side.

Non-European cases should carry weight: Bombay and Calcutta dock labour, Mombasa
(Frederick Cooper, *On the African Waterfront*, 1987), Durban, and the coolie gangs
of the Chinese treaty ports.

**Placement.** MAR. It is a distinct mechanism from impressment, which is a state
coercing labour it cannot afford to pay. Port labour is a casual labour market shaped
by ship arrivals. Folding it into `MAR-230` would give that entry two Mechanism
sections. `LAW-200` Labor Movement and Trade Unions should be linked, not duplicated.

## 4. League minority-protection treaties and kin-state claims — extend DIP-160

**Status:** gap inside an existing entry. Extend `DIP-160`; do not create a new entry.

**What exists.** Ethnic conflict as diplomatic precedent is otherwise well covered:

- **`DIP-160`** Nationalism as a Diplomatic Recognition Principle carries Partition
  and the OAU border principle.
- **`MIG-250`** carries the negotiated Greek–Turkish population exchange of 1923.
- **`MIG-240`** carries refugee status.

**What is missing.** The inter-polity instruments for protecting minorities left on
the wrong side of new borders:

- **The minority treaties of 1919–20.** They were imposed on the new and enlarged
  states of central and eastern Europe as a condition of recognition and supervised
  by the League of Nations.
- **Their failure and abandonment in the 1930s.**
- **Kin-state claims.** A state asserts a right to protect co-ethnics living in a
  neighbouring state. The Sudeten German case of 1938 is the most consequential
  instance.

This is the point at which a minority's treatment inside one state becomes a
legitimate ground for another state's intervention, which is DIP's axis rather than
LAW's or MIG's.

**Placement.** DIP, as an extension of `DIP-160`'s recognition logic: minority
protection was the price attached to recognising new national states.

## 5. The internal rebellion–repression–concession cycle — cross-domain gap

**Status:** cross-domain gap with no current home.

**What exists.** `DIP-115` Peripheral Revolt as an International Opportunity
deliberately carries only the inter-polity half of peripheral revolt: rival
sponsorship and its consequences. `WAR-240` Guerrilla Warfare carries insurgent
military method. `PHI-240`'s asabiyyah carries dynastic cohesion decay, the nearest
existing treatment of centre–periphery dynamics.

**What is missing.** The internal cycle between an imperial or state centre and a
province. It runs through provincial grievance over taxation, levies, officials or
religion, then revolt, then military repression, then concession: tax relief,
amnesty, local autonomy, co-optation of rebel leaders into office. It recurs,
because each concession changes the terms the next grievance works from, and the
centre learns whether revolt pays. The cases span every focus region:

- **China.** Dynastic-era rebellions and the Qing's handling of peripheral revolts.
- **The Ottoman Empire.** Provincial revolts and the co-optation of rebel leaders
  (`WAR-037` touches the Celali case from the demobilisation side).
- **Mughal India.** Zamindar and regional revolts.
- **The Habsburg and Russian empires.** Peripheral risings.
- **Spanish America.** Colonial revolts before independence.

**Placement.** Undecided, which is why it is recorded here rather than proposed.
The boundary between LAW, as internal obligation, and WAR, as the military response,
does not settle it cleanly. The cycle's core claim is about how a centre and a
periphery renegotiate obligation through violence, and that may justify a LAW entry
with `war` touched heavily. It must not be placed in DIP: the DIP domain was flagged
in the 2026-09-08 cross-domain synthesis as the one most vulnerable to a fold-into-LAW
argument, and internal-governance material would strengthen that argument.

## 6. Moral panic as a recurring legitimation mechanism — generalisation gap inside LAW-150

**Status:** gap inside an existing entry. Generalise `LAW-150`; do not create a new
entry. Recorded during the LAW verification pass.

**What exists.** `LAW-150` Witch Trials and Legal Panic (1450–1750) is scoped to
witch trials. Its Mechanism carries one of the three components of a general
moral-panic mechanism at a level that transfers beyond witchcraft: evidentiary
standards collapse under social pressure, so the same courts produce false
convictions without the law's text changing. Its Contested section carries the
popular-demand versus elite-direction dispute.

**What is missing.**

- **Enemy identification as legitimation.** The entry does not carry how naming an
  internal enemy strengthens the authority that names it. Accusation is a source of
  legitimacy for the accusing institution, not only a failure of procedure, which is
  why panics recur where authorities are insecure.
- **Termination when accusation reaches the elite.** The entry notes that
  authorities repudiated their verdicts but not why panics stop when they do.
  Salem ended as accusations reached prominent people, including the governor's
  wife. The general pattern is a panic that expands until it reaches people the
  elite cannot afford to accuse, then collapses. That end condition is modellable
  and absent.
- **Recurrence beyond witchcraft.** The entry treats witch trials as the sole
  instance. The same mechanism runs through heresy inquisitions, the Red Scares,
  purges and show trials, and panics over ritual abuse. Nothing in the corpus
  carries it as a general, recurring mechanism, so later entries that need it,
  such as scapegoating after catastrophe, can only point at a witchcraft entry.

**Placement.** LAW, as an extension of `LAW-150`: the mechanism is the capture of
legal procedure, which is LAW's. Broadening the title and date range would be part of
the extension.

## 7. Timber as structural material and the naval timber crisis — gap inside ENG-055, absent from MAR

**Status:** gap inside an existing entry, with a maritime half that has no home.
Extend `ENG-055` for the structural constraint; the naval timber crisis may justify
its own MAR entry.

**What exists.** `ENG-055` The Organic Energy Economy and Its Ceiling treats timber
as one of four claims on land (fuel, food, fodder, timber) and discusses it almost
entirely as fuel and charcoal, including the English timber shortage read as a fuel
crisis. `MAR-010` Shipbuilding and Hull Construction mentions timber supply only as a
reason shipyards sit where they do.

**What is missing.** Timber as structural material, which is a separate constraint
from fuel because it needs particular species, sizes and shapes that cannot be
substituted by quantity:

- **Ship timber and masts.** Great oaks for frames and compass timber, and tall
  straight conifers for masts, took a century or more to grow. Navies reserved them
  by law: Britain's Broad Arrow policy marking New England mast pines from 1691, the
  Venetian Arsenal's reserved oak forests, and French forest ordinances under Colbert.
  Britain's dependence on Baltic mast and naval stores made the Sound a strategic
  chokepoint and the American colonies a mast reserve; the United States later
  reserved live oak for naval use.
- **Construction timber.** Beams long enough for large roofs and halls became scarce
  in deforested regions, shaping architecture toward masonry and vaulting.
- **Mine props.** Deep mining consumed timber for pit props at industrial scale, tying
  coal output (`ENG-165`) back to woodland.

The naval crisis is a real strategic constraint: fleet size was capped by the supply
of large timber, not by money or labour, until iron hulls ended it.

**Placement.** The structural constraint extends `ENG-055`. The naval crisis is MAR,
under the boundary rule that shipbuilding technique and naval institutions are MAR,
and would link `MAR-010`, `MAR-060` and `MAR-200`.

## 8. Rocketry and launch capability — absent from ENG

**Status:** gap. No entry. Recorded instead of a space-race entry.

**What exists.** A space-race entry would duplicate existing coverage. `NAT-250` Big
Science carries the Soviet and American space programmes, Apollo's budget share, ISRO
and China's first satellite as facility-scale state science and prestige competition.
`ENG-340` Satellite Positioning carries navigation satellites. `MED-140` carries
satellite broadcasting. `WAR-330` Nuclear Deterrence carries delivery systems as
doctrine.

**What is missing.** Rocketry as technique — the engineering line from liquid-fuel
experiment through the V-2 to the intercontinental ballistic missile and the launch
vehicle. Every one of the entries above assumes launch capability exists and none
covers how it was built: propulsion, guidance, staging, the transfer of German
engineers and hardware to both superpowers after 1945, and the convergence of missile
and launcher programmes that made space capability and nuclear delivery the same
industrial base.

**Placement.** ENG, under the boundary rule that ENG owns technique and WAR owns
doctrine. It would sit around 1926–1970 and link `WAR-330`, `NAT-250`, `ENG-340` and
`MED-140`.
